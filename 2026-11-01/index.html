Iran's unseen supreme leader warns US and Israel against new strikes

In a written statement, Iran's Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei said Iran will decide how any new fighting ends, while diplomats try to stop it starting.

Iran's supreme leader has not been publicly seen since he took the job. Yet on Saturday, a statement attributed to Mojtaba Khamenei appeared on his website, and it was aimed straight at Washington and Jerusalem.

How does someone take the top post in a country and stay out of view? The source doesn't say. It only says he became supreme leader after his father, Ayatollah Ali Khamenei, was assassinated in US-Israeli strikes.

Those strikes began the war on February 28. Iran swiftly responded with attacks on countries around the region.

So why speak now? Because people fear the fighting could start again. The statement followed threats from Iran's foes of a possible resumption of strikes.

Here's the clever bit in the wording. The statement says that if the enemies attack again, Iran's armed forces and the Iranian nation "will determine the time and conditions for ending them." It warns about the start of a war, and it also claims the right to decide the finish.

The statement came with a reported meeting between Khamenei and senior commanders, including armed forces chief of staff Ali Abdollahi, army chief Amir Hatami and Revolutionary Guards commander Ahmad Vahidi. It was the first reported meeting between them.

According to the statement, he told them any threat must be met with an "immediate, impactful and geographically unrestricted response". He also ordered them to update Iran's target list to include "all types of tangible and intangible assets" of what the statement calls the American-Zionist enemy.

Meanwhile, a quieter track is running. A Qatari delegation arrived in Tehran on Friday for talks, after Iran reportedly received a letter from US Vice President JD Vance.

According to Iranian media, Tehran had yet to respond to that letter. Whether it does, and whether the fighting resumes, is the thing to watch.

Key facts

  • Mojtaba Khamenei, unseen publicly since becoming supreme leader, issued a written warning on Saturday.
  • He ordered commanders to answer any threat with an immediate, geographically unrestricted response.
  • The war began February 28 with US-Israeli strikes that killed his father, Ayatollah Ali Khamenei.
  • A Qatari delegation reached Tehran on Friday; Iran had not yet answered a letter from JD Vance.

A wage committee that didn't exist yet shut around 100 garment factories

Garment workers took to the streets over pay, factories shut, and the government says it will form a wage board today.

Around 100 garment factories were forced to declare holidays yesterday, according to a vice-president of the manufacturers' association, BGMEA. The cause was a committee that did not exist yet.

That committee is a wage board, a panel that sets minimum pay for a whole sector instead of leaving it to factory-by-factory haggling.

Here's the catch: timing. The labour law was recently amended so a board forms every three years, where it used to be five and was not consistently followed. The last revision took effect in December 2023, so the current period ends in December 2026.

Workers' leaders wanted the new board formed before December so the revision could start on time. The government announced an initiative in October, but protests had already begun in Uttara in mid-October and spread across Mirpur last week.

Yesterday it spread further, through Savar, Gazipur and Mirpur. Around 30 factories in Ashulia suspended work. None were reported closed in Narayanganj or Chattogram.

Tawhidur Rahman, a former general secretary of IndustriAll Bangladesh Council, said: "We have been urging the government for a long time... But the government has dragged its feet."

Things turned rough in Ashulia. A police superintendent said an officer was hit on the head by bricks thrown by protesting workers and was being treated in hospital. At MM Knitwear in Gazipur, the managing director said outsiders had entered the premises and made trouble. He declined to say who was behind it.

So what did the government do? Labour Minister Ariful Haque Chowdhury said the board will be announced today, Sunday, and published in the gazette, after meetings with factory owners on Friday and Saturday. He said workers' representatives have already submitted their nominees.

Owners have their own worry: workers who turn up but refuse to work, then head out to protest. Mohammad Hatem, president of BKMEA, said that if workers still do not return after the gazette, factories will be closed temporarily under Section 13(1) of the Labour Act. Under that rule, workers get no wages while operations are suspended.

Industry stakeholders are uncertain whether the announcement alone will bring workers back. Labour leader Babul Akter is hopeful it will. The thing to watch is whether the gazette appears today, and whether the factory floors fill again after it.

Key facts

  • Around 100 factories were forced to declare holidays, per BGMEA vice-president Shehab Udduza Chowdhury.
  • The government says it will announce the minimum wage board today, 1 November, via gazette.
  • The current three-year wage period ends in December 2026; the last revision took effect in December 2023.
  • BKMEA says it may close factories under Section 13(1) if workers do not return, meaning no wages for that time.

India's opposition held a mock impeachment of its chief election commissioner

After formal removal notices were rejected, the INDIA bloc staged a symbolic Lok Sabha-style trial of Gyanesh Kumar in a Delhi garden.

The vote was unanimous, taken by a show of hands, and it removed nobody. On Saturday, October 31, India's opposition alliance, the INDIA bloc, held a symbolic "public impeachment" of Chief Election Commissioner Gyanesh Kumar.

So why stage a trial that has no legal force? Because, the opposition says, the real routes were shut. Congress president Mallikarjun Kharge said 193 opposition MPs submitted notices in both Houses of Parliament seeking Mr. Kumar's removal seven months ago, and the proposals were rejected.

Here's the setup. The garden of Mr. Kharge's Delhi residence was dressed up to replicate the Lok Sabha. Former Speaker Meira Kumar presided, Sonia Gandhi sat in the front row, and the session ran three-and-a-half hours. Civil society representatives, student leaders and voters who say their names were wrongly deleted from the rolls also took part.

The motion listed six charges. They include conspiring with the ruling BJP to steal elections, acting at the behest of the Prime Minister and the Home Minister, and deleting 13.2 crore voters through the special intensive revision, or SIR, of voter lists. Mr. Kharge also accused him of changing Form 6 without parliamentary oversight and excluding the other two Election Commissioners from decisions. These are the opposition's allegations, not findings.

Now the number that drove the speeches. Rahul Gandhi, the Leader of the Opposition in the Lok Sabha, alleged that 13.3 crore votes were deleted. That, he said, is one in every seven Indians, nearly 15% of the electorate.

He set it against the 2024 Lok Sabha election, where he said the gap between the opposition and the winning side was only three percentage points. His conclusion: you cannot delete that many votes and argue the election was legal.

Here's the sharp bit. Mr. Gandhi also aimed past the top. Of the Prime Minister and Home Minister he said, "We will deal with them, but you are their instruments, and so you are our first target." He added that Mr. Kumar should be tried for treason.

Mr. Kharge struck a gentler note: "We have no personal enmity with the CEC." He said the aim was an impartial Election Commission, and he called for repeal of the law that gives the CEC lifelong immunity.

Mr. Gandhi called the gathering a "political weapon" and claimed Mr. Kumar will not survive in the post once the opposition puts all its proofs into the public domain. That promised release is the thing to watch.

Key facts

  • On October 31, 2026, the INDIA bloc adopted a symbolic impeachment motion against CEC Gyanesh Kumar by show of hands.
  • The motion carried six charges, including conspiring with the BJP and deleting 13.2 crore voters through SIR.
  • Mr. Kharge said 193 opposition MPs sought Mr. Kumar's removal seven months ago and were rejected.
  • Rahul Gandhi alleged 13.3 crore votes, nearly 15% of the electorate, were deleted.

Turkiye's president tells both Russia and Ukraine to stop hitting ships

Erdogan and Zelenskyy agreed in Istanbul to work on a new Black Sea grain corridor, while Turkiye says neither warring side has acted on its proposal to end the attacks.

Turkiye has sent both Russia and Ukraine a plan to stop attacks on ships. According to Ankara, neither side has acted on it.

That is the backdrop to Saturday's news conference in Istanbul, where President Recep Tayyip Erdogan stood next to Ukraine's President Volodymyr Zelenskyy. Erdogan said attacks on civilian vessels are unacceptable, whoever carries them out.

His words were plain. "We cannot accept these attacks under any circumstances, regardless of who carries them out or what justification is offered."

Why does Turkiye care so much? It keeps ties with both Moscow and Kyiv, and the source says it has been angered by the strikes on commercial shipping. When you are friends with both fighters, you are also the one who gets to say stop to both.

Here's the deal on the table. Zelenskyy said he and Erdogan agreed to work on a Black Sea grain corridor, meaning a safe route for grain ships. Erdogan said talks on safe navigation would continue.

The idea is getting attention elsewhere too. US Secretary of State Marco Rubio said there was growing international interest in a limited ceasefire in the Black Sea, just to secure grain and energy shipments. He said the US is reviewing proposals from Turkiye and India.

Rubio was careful about what it would not do. "It doesn't solve the war, but at least it solves a unique aspect of it."

Here's the clever bit. Erdogan argued that a deal on shipping could give fresh impetus to efforts toward lasting peace. So a narrow fix about boats is being pitched as a first step toward something bigger.

The wider talks are still in play. The Kremlin said last week that the next round between Russia, Ukraine and the US was planned for November in the United Arab Emirates, and Zelenskyy said a meeting in Turkiye was also discussed.

So the open question is simple. Turkiye has asked both sides to stop, and so far, by its own account, neither has acted on its proposal.

Meta reportedly built a humanoid robot it has no plans to sell

Meta and Chinese firm Fourier reportedly built a prototype called Max in about three months, and one person familiar with the project says it is meant to lead to a more polished robot.

Why would Meta, the company behind Facebook and Instagram, build a humanoid robot and then say it has no plans to sell it?

That's the puzzle inside a new report from The Information. Citing people familiar with the project, it says Meta built a prototype with Fourier, a Shanghai robotics company, in about three months. That's one season, start to finish. Reuters said it could not immediately verify the report, so hold the details loosely.

Meet Max. It stands about 1.5 metres tall and weighs roughly 106 pounds, about the size of a small adult. It has a display that can mimic facial expressions and five-fingered hands designed to handle small objects.

Here's the catch. A Meta spokesperson said: "this effort with Fourier is focused on internal research and experimentation, and we have no plans to sell these units." Yet one person familiar with the project said Max was meant to help Meta develop a more polished robot that it could eventually release as a product.

So who's right? Maybe both. "These units" covers Max and says nothing about whatever comes after it.

Think of the first pancake in a batch. Its whole job is to teach you how the pan behaves, so the next one comes out better.

And the next pancake is already on. Meta and Fourier began work on Max 2 in September. It's expected to get a redesigned torso, improved hands, a projector and extra sensors to help it deal with stairs.

Stairs and small objects, you'll notice, are home problems. That fits: Meta's longer-term ambition is machines that can assist with household tasks.

So why a partner? The reports don't say, but look at what each side has.

Meta's robotics group was set to draw on Llama, the company's AI models. In May Meta bought a startup building AI to help robots understand and adapt to human behaviour. That's brain work.

Fourier has made bodies, from rehabilitation robots to humanoids. And the work was hands-on: Meta staff travelled to China, and Li-Chen Miller, its vice president of product for robotics, spent almost two months at Fourier's factory in Shanghai.

No launch target has been set. Max can reportedly mimic a facial expression, but the reports don't establish which household tasks, if any, it can do on its own.

Key facts

  • The Information reported that Meta and Fourier built a humanoid prototype called Max in about three months; Reuters could not immediately verify the report.
  • Meta says the work is internal research and it has no plans to sell these units.
  • One person familiar with the project said Max was meant to help develop a more polished robot that could eventually become a product.

More than $200 million, every minute. That is the pace at which over $6 billion in crypto left Binance, the world's largest cryptocurrency exchange, late Thursday. The whole thing took under 30 minutes.

Who pulled it off? The US-based blockchain research firm TRM Labs says an elite North Korean hacking unit called TraderTraitor went after $6.36 billion in Binance coins spread across 10 blockchains.

Here's the catch for ordinary users. The hackers could not get into consumer accounts. They only touched Binance's own corporate holdings, and co-CEO Richard Teng said on Friday, "Your assets are safe."

Think of it as a robbery where the thieves emptied the bank's own vault but never reached the customers' safe-deposit boxes. Still bad for the bank. Fine for you.

So did the thieves get away with it? Partly. Around $3 billion has been located or frozen. But TRM Labs estimates the hackers moved roughly $2.4 billion of coins "beyond any issuer's reach."

Wait, is this a one-off? No. In December 2022, South Korea's spy agency accused North Korean hackers of stealing roughly $1.1 billion in crypto and other virtual assets over five years. This one attack is more than five times that.

In February 2025, the FBI said TraderTraitor stole $1.5 billion from Bybit, one of Binance's top rivals. Last month, attackers took around $350 million from the exchange Bitget.

Why is crypto such a target? It is still largely unregulated. In June 2024, Donald Trump, then a presidential candidate, vowed to make the US the "crypto capital of the planet," and since returning to office he has focused on reducing oversight of the industry.

Even with its limited impact on consumers, the heist exposes the risks of trading crypto and the growing skill of North Korean hackers.

The number to watch is the split. About $3 billion found or frozen, about $2.4 billion out of reach. Which of those two grows from here?

The telescope that told others where to look is falling to Earth

NASA's Swift telescope, which spent 22 years alerting other telescopes to explosions in space, is expected to reenter around November 6 after a robotic rescue failed, and scientists have no settled replacement.

When a star explodes somewhere out in the universe, who tells the telescopes where to look?

For 22 years, one answer has been a NASA spacecraft called Swift. Now it is falling out of orbit, and the US Space Force predicts reentry on or around Friday, November 6.

A robot was supposed to stop this. First, though, why does a telescope need a lookout?

Big telescopes see fine detail, but only in a tiny patch of sky at a time. Gamma-ray bursts, powerful explosions in space, can appear anywhere without warning and fade fast.

Swift was built to turn quickly toward sudden events, which is how it got its name. Think of the friend in the group chat who posts "go outside and look up, right now." Its alerts helped telescopes including Hubble and James Webb follow up in detail.

And it had plenty to post. Swift detected more than 2,000 gamma-ray bursts, roughly one every four days for 22 years, plus 1,400 exploding stars.

So why is it falling? Blame the Sun. Low orbit still has a thin trace of air that slowly drags spacecraft down, and intense solar activity in recent years increased that drag, so Swift sank faster than usual.

Enter the robot. In 2025, NASA hired a company called Katalyst Space Technologies to catch Swift and lift it higher. Its three-armed spacecraft, Link, launched in July 2026.

Here's the catch. Link developed control problems, never got closer than about 7 to 9 miles from Swift, and the attempt was called off on August 19. In September, Link itself fell back into the atmosphere, so the rescuer came down first.

Swift, meanwhile, went back to work, observing the sky until late October, when it was too low to hold steady and its systems were shut down.

Most of the 1½-ton telescope, about the weight of a car, should burn up. Mission chief scientist Brad Cenko said some tungsten parts and mirror pieces could survive. Those would most likely land in the ocean, though parts of Africa, Asia, Australia and the Americas sit inside the possible zone, and the exact time and place aren't known.

"At least in low-Earth orbit, what goes up must come down," said astronomer John Nousek, who led Swift's control team. So back to that first question: next time a star explodes, who tells the telescopes? Scientists are still working that out.

Key facts

  • The US Space Force predicted Swift would reenter on or around Friday, November 6.
  • Swift detected more than 2,000 gamma-ray bursts and 1,400 exploding stars over 22 years.
  • Katalyst's robotic spacecraft Link came no closer than about 7 to 9 miles before the rescue was called off on August 19.
  • Scientists are still working out how to replace Swift's role in alerting other telescopes.

Anthropic's IPO filing shows soaring sales and a $600 billion bet

Anthropic's IPO filing shows fast-rising sales, confusing profit figures and $600 billion in computing commitments that depend on demand holding up.

More than $600 billion is riding on one question: will people keep paying for AI? That's how much Anthropic, the company behind the Claude chatbot, has committed to computing deals with partners including Amazon and Google.

Now we can see the numbers. Anthropic has filed its S-1, the document a company gives regulators and investors before selling shares to the public. It runs to 335 pages.

The demand looks real. Revenue hit $16.3 billion in the first half of 2026, about 14 times the same period a year earlier. Two attendees at an October investor meeting say Anthropic reported $16.8 billion for the third quarter alone. Three months beat the previous six.

Still, $600 billion is huge. At that third-quarter pace, matching it would take almost nine years of sales. And Anthropic itself warns its growth rate could fall.

So is it making money? Pick a number. For the first half, the same company reported a net loss of $80.7 billion, an operating loss of $693 million and adjusted operating income of $1.62 billion.

Wait, what? Most of that giant loss is a one-time accounting charge of $72.4 billion, tied to financing agreements. The operating figures sit far closer to break-even, and which side you land on depends on what you count.

Here's the catch. Computing is the bill that keeps climbing. Anthropic spent $9 billion on it in six months, more than in all of 2025, and expects it to keep rising.

The product also ages fast. More than 80% of third-quarter revenue came from models released in the previous six months. Think of a restaurant where four of every five plates sold are dishes added in the past six months. That kitchen has to keep inventing.

Its partners wear two hats as well. Nearly half of first-half revenue came through marketplaces run by Amazon, Google and Microsoft, the same companies it relies on for computing capacity.

Then the strangest part. Anthropic was founded by researchers who left OpenAI after disagreements over governance and AI safety. Now 93 pages of its filing, more than a quarter, cover risks, including a warning that advanced AI systems could pose "catastrophic or existential risks to humanity."

The share price, date and amount to be raised aren't set. The harder unknown is the question we started with: will people keep paying enough to cover that $600 billion? The filing shows sales so far. It can't answer that.

The Maya city scholars ignored turned out to hold the answers

Two archaeologists dug into a Belize site once thought barely worth the time, and it helped overturn old ideas about how the Maya lived.

For a long time, scholars believed Maya cities were near-empty husks. A scattered rural population, the thinking went, wandered in only for occasional ceremonies or to serve the elite.

Where did that idea come from? Largely from Friar Diego de Landa, a 16th-century Spaniard who lived as a missionary among the Maya in the Yucatán. By his own account, he burned most of their hieroglyphic books.

Here's the catch. The scholar William Gates wrote that de Landa burned 99 times as much knowledge as he left us. De Landa portrayed the Maya as poor peasants ruled by a ruthless aristocracy.

Arlen Chase has a theory about why. Spain in the 1500s had knights and nobles in castles, with everyone else in the countryside. "The Spaniards slapped that two-class model onto the Maya," he said.

By 1954, a influential book by J. Eric S. Thompson cast the Maya as peaceful stargazers. He wondered how they could chart the heavens yet "never learn to weigh a sack of corn."

Arlen and Diane Chase, now archaeologists at the University of Houston, went to look. In 1979 they began six years at Santa Rita Corozal, a Maya site in Belize. And there was a clue in the houses of ordinary people: obsidian.

Obsidian is a dark, glassy stone found only in distant volcanic regions. So many non-elite households had tools made of it that elites handing it out as gifts seemed unlikely. A better explanation was that Santa Rita sat between the coast and two rivers, and its residents could simply buy imported goods at market.

That cracked the idea that elites controlled everything. Then came Caracol, a site archaeologists once saw as minor. Its remoteness helped: Tikal and Chichén Itzá were first encountered by Europeans in the 19th century, while Caracol had barely been excavated when the Chases arrived in the 1980s.

Today their field camp has no running water, no cellphone reception and no antivenom. Their son Adrian, 35, also an archaeologist, told the writer he had used his machete that morning on a fer-de-lance pit viper.

And the site once thought barely worth the time? It holds Caana, a 141-foot "sky palace" abandoned around the year 900. It is still the tallest human-made structure in Belize.

Key facts

  • Arlen and Diane Chase began work at Caracol, Belize, in the 1980s, when it had barely been excavated.
  • Many non-elite households at Santa Rita Corozal owned imported obsidian tools, pointing to market trade.
  • Friar Diego de Landa burned most Maya hieroglyphic codices, by his own account.
  • Caana, a 141-foot palace at Caracol, is still the tallest human-made structure in Belize.

Sylvia Plath at 22: fighting your own mind only wears you out

In a letter to her mother, a young Sylvia Plath described a fight with herself and the plain advice she gave herself for getting through it.

Sylvia Plath was twenty-two when she wrote her mother a letter from the middle of what she called an enormous battle with herself. It was a January day, and by the source's account both women were wading through a darkness of the soul.

The battle, she wrote, was a family habit. Their little family was prone to lie awake at night hating themselves for stupidities, and to let cruel remarks fester until they blossomed into something like ulcer attacks.

She wondered if it was hereditary. She did not know, and neither did anyone else yet. Neuroscience was an infant science, and the helix of heredity had only just been discovered, so the old question of nature versus nurture had barely begun to be answered.

Here's the turn. Having named the fight, she wrote this: "But beyond a point, fighting only wears one out and one has to shut off that nagging part of the mind and go on without it with bravo and philosophy." Then she added, "Your present life is the important thing."

Read it slowly. She does not say the nagging voice vanishes. She says that arguing with it harder is a losing way to spend your strength, so you set it aside and get on with today.

The Marginalian essay that quotes the letter goes on to make a firm point. It calls it a fallacy to think people who die by suicide after living with mental illness have failed at life. Grieving the loss and regretting the help that was not there is one thing, it says, and faulting the faulty instrument itself is another.

Why? Because no mind can fully know what living inside another is like. It is harder still for a mind with ordinary biochemistry to grasp one inflamed by misfiring neurotransmitters. To survive even a single day with such a mind, the essay says, is no small feat.

Which brings us back to the letter. Plath lived thirty-one years, from October 27, 1932, to February 11, 1963. Two years after writing that advice she shaded in her darkness in the poem "The Disquieting Muses," and she filled the rest of her life with poems the essay says have lit up generations of lives.

Key facts

  • Plath wrote the letter to her mother at age 22, and it appears in Letters Home: Correspondence 1950-1963.
  • She wrote that fighting only wears one out past a point, and that one has to shut off the nagging part of the mind and go on.
  • She lived from October 27, 1932 to February 11, 1963, thirty-one years.
  • The helix of heredity had only just been discovered and neuroscience was an infant science when she wrote.

Learn · Physics from first principles

The unsolved problems where the rules of physics break down

Our two best theories of nature mathematically clash at the extremes, leaving open problems that guide the search for new rules.

You might think that because physics explains everyday things like how solids hold heat or how light moves, our rulebook for the physical world is essentially complete. It turns out that physics still faces major unsolved problems where existing theories fail to explain what we observe, or where we cannot yet build experiments to test our ideas.

Some of these puzzles are experimental, but the deepest trouble is theoretical. Within modern physics, the mathematical framework of the Standard Model remains inconsistent with general relativity. Think of a kitchen with two recipe books that work on their own, but give conflicting instructions the moment you try to mix them into a single pot.

Here is the catch. Because these two theories disagree, both break down under extreme conditions, such as at the Big Bang and at the centers of black holes beyond their event horizons. Other major questions stay open beyond the Standard Model, including dark matter, dark energy, neutrino masses, matter-antimatter asymmetry, and the strong CP problem.

To tackle these gaps, physicists write down lists of what they cannot explain. Isaac Newton published 31 queries about light and matter in 1704, while in 1900 Lord Kelvin pointed to two clouds over physics, including missing evidence for the luminiferous aether and wrong predictions for the heat capacity of solids. Both of Kelvin's problems were solved within a single decade through the development of special relativity and quantum mechanics.

Take a concrete tally from modern physics. In 1984, mathematical physicist Barry Simon proposed a list of 15 open problems on quantum operators. By 2000, researchers had solved about 5 of them, meaning they answered one third of the list while 10 remained open, before Simon released a second list of 15 problems with at least 3 solved.

Some lists take even longer to crack, as shown by the Strings 2000 conference, where members proposed 10 millennium problems in theoretical physics and none has been conclusively solved as of 2025. That leaves us at the ultimate boundary of our first principles. If our two best descriptions of reality cannot agree when gravity becomes extreme, what new framework will finally unite them?

Key facts

  • The Standard Model is mathematically inconsistent with general relativity, causing both to break down at gravitational singularities like black holes and the Big Bang.
  • In 1900, Lord Kelvin identified two unresolved problems in classical physics, both of which were solved within a decade by special relativity and quantum mechanics.
  • Out of 15 quantum operator problems proposed by Barry Simon in 1984, about 5 were solved by 2000, leaving 10 unanswered.
  • None of the 10 millennium problems proposed at the Strings 2000 conference have been conclusively solved as of 2025.

What holds a rule together

The sharp events on today's page point to slower tests of trust and dependence.

The loud part of the news is the moment a line gets crossed. The quieter part is the long argument over who drew the line, and whether anyone still accepts it. That question runs beneath today's page: authority needs people to recognize it before it can do much.

You can see it in Poland's fight over who may sit on its top court, and in Bangladesh's garment pay dispute, where factories shut before a wage board existed. A court seat and a body that sets wages seem far apart. Yet each depends on people accepting a process for deciding whose judgment counts. When that acceptance frays, the process becomes the story.

There is another weakness hiding in plain sight: dependence. Anthropic's $600 billion in computing commitments rely on demand holding up. A study questions Gulf states' reliance on US protection after Iranian attacks. The stakes differ greatly, but the question underneath is similar: what happens when something you count on is beyond your control?

News shows you the visible moment: a shutdown, a filing, a dispute, an attack. Those moments matter. Beneath them are slower tests of whether institutions can settle arguments, commitments can be met, and partners can be counted on. A headline tells you what happened.

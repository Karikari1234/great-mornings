Francis Halzen wins Nobel Prize for capturing invisible cosmic particles

The 82-year-old scientist spent decades building a facility at the South Pole to catch high-energy neutrinos.

Picture a research facility at the South Pole in Antarctica. The University of Wisconsin-Madison leads the IceCube Neutrino Observatory there. Francis Halzen is an 82-year-old scientist born in Belgium. He led an international team to build the instrument. In Stockholm, the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences announced a major award on Tuesday. They gave Halzen the Nobel Prize in physics.

Neutrinos are tiny cosmic particles with a very small mass. They spew from stars like the sun. Trillions of them zip through our bodies every second. Halzen wanted to study a rare group of high-energy neutrinos. He faced a hard problem. Scientists cannot glimpse these ghost particles on their own.

Like watching for ripples in a pond to know a stone fell in, researchers must look for flashes of light. These flashes happen when the tiny particles collide with other bits of matter. Halzen started his work to capture neutrinos at the South Pole in the 1980s.

Mark Pearce chairs the Nobel Committee for Physics. Pearce said Halzen gave researchers a fantastic instrument for a new kind of astronomy. Halzen spoke to the committee by phone from Italy on Tuesday. He said the news was a great surprise.

The prize money is 12 million Swedish kronor, or about $1.2 million. Halzen studied these rare cosmic messengers to find clues about how the universe evolved. His work asks how scientists will build tools to measure parts of the cosmos they cannot see.

Key facts

  • Francis Halzen won the Nobel Prize in physics on Tuesday.
  • His research into capturing neutrinos at the South Pole dates back to the 1980s.
  • The prize money is 12 million Swedish kronor, or about $1.2 million.

Learn · Physics from first principles

Physics from first principles, lesson 6: conservation

Measurable properties in isolated systems remain unchanged as they evolve over time.

A conservation law states that a particular measurable property of an isolated physical system does not change as the system evolves over time. The conservation law of energy states that the total quantity of energy in an isolated system remains unchanged, though it may change form. Exact conservation laws govern mass-energy, linear momentum, angular momentum, and electric charge.

You might imagine an amount of energy appearing on Earth while the same amount simultaneously disappears from another region of the Universe. The total amount of the conserved quantity in the Universe would remain unchanged. This weak form of global conservation does not occur in nature.

Special relativity dictates that if the appearance of energy at point A and the disappearance of energy at point B are simultaneous in one reference frame, they will not be simultaneous in moving reference frames. In a moving frame, one event will occur before the other. During the interval between the two events, energy will not be conserved.

Nature relies on a stronger rule called a local conservation law. This requires a flow, or flux, of the quantity into or out of a point for the amount to change. The amount of electric charge at a point never changes without an electric current carrying the difference into or out of the point. A quantity conserved in one reference frame is conserved in all moving reference frames. This is called being Lorentz invariant.

A continuity equation expresses a local conservation law mathematically. It states that the change in the quantity within a volume equals the total net flux of the quantity through the surface of the volume. Local conservation guarantees that the total amount of the conserved quantity in the Universe remains constant.

Noether's theorem states that a one-to-one correspondence exists between each local conservation law and a differentiable symmetry of the Universe. The local conservation of energy follows from the uniformity of time. The local conservation of angular momentum arises because space has no preferred direction.

Conservation laws are fundamental laws of nature. They apply across physics, chemistry, biology, geology, and engineering. Most laws are exact and apply to all possible processes. Some are approximate and hold true only in particular situations, such as low speeds or short time scales. How do these approximate conservation laws function during these specific interactions?

Key facts

  • Exact conservation laws apply to mass-energy, linear momentum, angular momentum, and electric charge.
  • Local conservation requires a flow of the quantity into or out of a point for the amount to change.
  • Noether's theorem connects each local conservation law to a differentiable symmetry of the Universe.

Patterns of fragility and power shape the world beneath daily events.

The news arrives as a series of sudden events. We see the immediate impact of a disruption or a diplomatic demand. The world is driven by the slow processes that run underneath the daily headlines. Two patterns stand out right now. One is the extreme fragility of the systems we do not see. The digital connections that support daily life are brittle. A software dispute with a Russian vendor cuts millions of people in Bangladesh off from their bank cards. A quiet disagreement turns into a nationwide breakdown.

Another pattern is the collapse of trust in new technology. Companies push artificial intelligence forward to handle more tasks. Trust gaps in a new communication protocol let harmful instructions pass between software agents. The creators of the tools face demands for outside control. The tension is between the desire to automate work and the fear of losing command over the machines.

Old struggles for power continue to shape the map. The United States demands that Iran reduce its nuclear enrichment capacity to end a seven-month war. Nations use the threat of violence to extract concessions. The news is a daily record of these overlapping pressures. When a system fails tomorrow, is it breaking because the code is flawed, or is it breaking because someone wants more power?

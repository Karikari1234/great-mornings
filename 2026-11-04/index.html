Trump says Israel aid ends this year, but the deal runs to 2028

On Election Day, Trump said the US will stop its nearly $4 billion a year in aid to Israel by the end of 2026, though the agreement is set to run until September 2028.

The agreement that sends Israel nearly $4 billion a year in US military aid is set to run until September 2028. On Tuesday, President Donald Trump said it will stop by the end of this year.

He said it on the Fox & Friends morning show. "We made a deal months ago that we're stopping," he said, referring to a deal with Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu. Trump said Israel is "fine" with it and can still buy US weapons "whenever they want."

First, the background. The money comes from a 10-year security assistance accord that began in 2019 and was signed by Barack Obama. It provides $3.8 billion a year, which works out to roughly $10 million a day.

Here's the catch. Trump did not explain how an agreement meant to last into 2028 would end in 2026. And Gadi Eisenkot, who leads the Israeli opposition, said Israel's defense establishment is learning of the shift through the media, at the same time as the public.

So why say it now? Americans were voting in midterm elections, which have been framed as a referendum on Trump's second term. Trump said part of the aim was to counter a "bad talking point" that Democrats such as Abdul El-Sayed use against Republicans.

That talking point has an audience. A Quinnipiac poll in June found 48% of voters think the US is too supportive of Israel, up from 16% in 2017. Among Democrats the figure is 66%, up from 20%.

On the Israeli side, the idea is not new. In June, Netanyahu said "we need to break free from dependency and build our own independent armaments network."

In the same interview, Trump confirmed that US forces hit an Iranian anti-aircraft facility overnight, in what he called a "minor" attack. Jon Alterman, of the Center for Strategic and International Studies, told Reuters the timing of both announcements was "curious."

His question is still hanging there. Why announce it on Election Day, while there is speculation that a major joint US-Israeli strike on Iran may be days away?

Key facts

  • Trump said on Fox & Friends that the US will stop its aid to Israel by the end of 2026.
  • The 10-year accord gives $3.8 billion a year and is set to run until September 2028.
  • Trump did not explain how the aid would end early.
  • A Quinnipiac poll in June found 48% of voters think the US is too supportive of Israel, up from 16% in 2017.

Ganga water treaty expires December 12, and the renewal meeting has no date

A 1996 river deal signed by two prime ministers ends in weeks, and India says it has no update on renewing it or on a visit by Tarique Rahman.

The Ganga Water Treaty between India and Bangladesh runs out on December 12, and the meeting that would renew it has no date.

On Tuesday, November 3, India's foreign ministry spokesperson, Randhir Jaiswal, was asked about it at a briefing. He said there is neither any "update" on renewal nor on dates for a visit to New Delhi by Bangladesh Prime Minister Tarique Rahman.

What he did offer was a name for the plan. "Between India and Bangladesh, we have a Joint Rivers Commission which is the structured mechanism for discussion on all cross-boundary rivers, including the Ganga," he said.

So why does a visit matter? The treaty was signed in 1996, when Sheikh Hasina became Prime Minister for the first time. She signed it with then Indian Prime Minister H.D. Deve Gowda.

Because it was signed by prime ministers, The Hindu reports it is understood that renewal would happen when Narendra Modi and Rahman meet. The treaty also requires expert-level and political consultations to renew it. Think of it as two doors: officials work on one, leaders have to open the other.

Here's the catch. India has invited Rahman, but he has not yet responded. He has visited China, Japan, Malaysia and the United States so far, and he was invited to both bilateral and multilateral events during the BRICS Summit in New Delhi in September.

Jaiswal repeated that India wants a "positive, forward looking and constructive" relationship with Bangladesh.

What happens if the date passes? Diplomatic sources from the Bangladeshi side had said earlier that they expect an automatic short-term renewal after December 12. Indian officials have not indicated that so far.

Jaiswal was also asked about garment workers in Bangladesh who are on the streets demanding fair wages, as the country faces high inflation and an electricity crisis. "We have been following the situation in Bangladesh," he said, "and we will continue to follow the developments."

So the thing to watch is December 12. A Modi and Rahman meeting is what renewal is understood to need. Without one, the only signal so far is a hope on one side that the treaty simply rolls over.

The US president can't send aid or wage war alone. Congress decides

The US midterm vote decides who controls Congress, and that could change what Washington does on Ukraine, Iran and foreign aid.

The US president has less control over war and foreign aid than you might think. Congress holds a lot of the levers, and voters were deciding who controls it.

Here is the setup. Congress has two chambers, the House of Representatives and the Senate. They write laws, decide government spending, and act as a check on the president. Right now Trump's Republicans hold a majority in both.

Polls suggest that could change. Democrats might take the House and possibly the Senate too.

So what would that do? Take Ukraine. The US has suspended sanctions against Russia and cut financial and military aid to Ukraine. Brandon Conradis, an editor at Bloomberg Government, says Democrats could push legislation for more aid.

Here's the catch. Trump can veto a bill. Congress can override that with a two-thirds majority in both chambers, which would need some Republicans on board.

Iran is a harder case. Trump launched the war with Israel on February 28 without consulting Congress, though the Constitution says Congress must approve a formal declaration of war. Democrats and some Republicans passed a resolution calling for an end to the hostilities, and the administration ignored it.

Michael O'Hanlon of the Brookings Institution doesn't expect huge powers for Congress here. But he says it could perhaps prevent a major escalation by refusing to fund it.

Then there is USAID, the agency Trump shut down as part of sharp cuts to aid that helps fight Ebola, AIDS and hunger. Bringing it back against his wishes is questionable. Hearings are not, and Conradis expects Democrats to drag officials they hold responsible in front of Congress.

Even so, the president would still be a Republican. There would be no complete reversal. But as Conradis put it, some things would change because the power dynamics would be different.

Key facts

  • Republicans currently hold majorities in both chambers of Congress.
  • Congress can override a presidential veto with a two-thirds majority in both chambers.
  • Trump launched the war against Iran with Israel on February 28 without consulting Congress.
  • Recent polls suggest Democrats could take the House and possibly the Senate.

Apollo's record $114bn quarter, and the AI chips some deals rest on

Apollo arranged a record $114 billion of financing last quarter, including AI deals backed by chips that a rival says it can't value.

What is a computer chip worth a few years after you buy it? A surprising amount of money now rides on the answer.

Apollo Global Management is a New York firm that started in private equity in 1990 and grew into credit and insurance. It says it arranged a record $114 billion of financing in the three months to September 30. That's 52% more than the same quarter a year earlier, and more than half again on top of the quarter before.

Some of that money went to the AI boom. The Financial Times reported that Apollo funded Anthropic, the AI company, and Nvidia, which makes the chips AI runs on. Apollo's president, Jim Zelter, said the firm also led a $40 billion financing for graphics processing units for SpaceX.

Here's the catch. Some of these AI deals use the chips themselves as collateral, according to the FT's reporting.

Think of a car loan. If you stop paying, the lender takes the car and sells it. That only protects the lender if they have a decent idea of what the car will be worth by then.

Not every lender thinks it can do that with chips. Michael Arougheti, who runs rival firm Ares Management, said Ares had avoided lending against AI chips because he did "not know how to underwrite the decline in depreciation curves on that type of technology." Put simply, he can't tell how fast they'll lose value.

Apollo points to quality. Zelter said $98 billion of the total was debt, and more than 80% of that was investment grade, averaging BBB+. Investment grade means rating agencies judge the borrower fairly likely to repay.

Wait, though. The reports don't say how much of the $114 billion was tied to AI. They also don't say how much Apollo kept on its own books rather than passing on to other investors.

Apollo's fee-related earnings hit a record $802 million, and $53 billion of new money came in, taking its assets to $1.07 trillion. Under standard accounting rules it still posted a $1.06 billion loss. Reuters put that down to investments being marked down while interest rates stayed high.

Zelter says the pipeline looks strong for the next couple of quarters. Which brings us back to the opening question. If the chips hold their value, Apollo's bet looks shrewd. If they fade faster than expected, the lenders holding them will find out what a used AI chip is really worth.

Key facts

  • Apollo arranged a record $114 billion of financing in Q3 2026, up 52% from a year earlier.
  • The FT reported Apollo funded Anthropic and Nvidia; some AI deals use chips as collateral.
  • Ares CEO Michael Arougheti said his firm avoids lending against AI chips because their depreciation is hard to underwrite.
  • Reports don't say how much of the $114 billion was AI-related.

Australia's rates hit their highest since 2008, but they can't touch oil

Australia's central bank raised rates for the fifth time this year to fight inflation, even as the economy slows and part of the price pressure sits beyond its reach.

Michele Bullock, who runs Australia's central bank, raised interest rates on November 3 for the fifth time this year. Then she told a press conference she did not know what the board's next move would be.

Here's the puzzle. Her own bank says the economy is slowing, house prices have fallen significantly and unemployment has risen. So why press the brake harder?

First, the brake. The cash rate is what banks charge each other for overnight loans, and it influences what everyone else pays to borrow and earns on savings. It just went from 4.60% to 4.85%, its highest level since late 2008.

A quarter of a point sounds like nothing. Canstar estimated it adds $107 a month in interest on a $700,000 home loan, about $1,284 a year, though how much lenders will pass on is still unknown.

Now the reason. Consumer prices rose 4.3% in the year to September, and the bank aims for 2% to 3%. It expects inflation to stay above 3% until late 2027, about nine months longer than it expected before.

Here's the catch. Part of that outlook comes from higher fuel and commodity prices linked to the Middle East conflict. Bullock said it plainly: monetary policy cannot affect global oil prices.

A buzzing mattress plus two pills may have pushed Alzheimer's-linked protein out of the brain

In a small Finnish study, 40 minutes on a vibrating mattress with nitric oxide pills raised blood levels of beta-amyloid 42, which may mean the brain's waste system flushed more of it out.

Your brain has a drainage system, and researchers are now trying to make it work harder by having people lie on a vibrating mattress.

It is called the glymphatic system. Think of it as a network of channels around the brain's blood vessels that carries waste away for disposal elsewhere in the body.

Here's the catch. Alzheimer's disease is linked to a protein called beta-amyloid, which clumps into plaques between neurons. If the drain is slow, the question is whether you can speed it up.

A team led by Vesa Kiviniemi at the University of Oulu in Finland tried two nudges at once. Earlier mouse studies showed that vibrating the body activates a motion-sensitive protein called Piezo1 in the walls of brain blood vessels, so the vessels widen and narrow more strongly. Nitric oxide, which the brain makes naturally, also widens those vessels.

Kiviniemi says that seems to squeeze the glymphatic channels narrower, which puts more pressure on the waste fluid so it leaves faster.

So they recruited 29 adults with no Alzheimer's or other cognitive issues. Each took a nitric oxide pill under the tongue, lay on a gently vibrating mattress for 40 minutes, and took a second pill halfway through.

Afterwards, blood levels of beta-amyloid 42 were up 35 per cent on average. That sounds backwards, but the idea is that the protein was moving from the brain into the blood, on its way out.

Used alone, the mattress or the pills did less than half as much to the glymphatic system, judging by brain scans.

Wait, can we trust that? Not fully yet. Christopher Brown at the University of Southampton, who wasn't involved, says the liver might simply be breaking the protein down more slowly, which would also raise blood levels. Six participants had mild effects like nausea and headache, and there was no placebo group.

Please don't try the pills at home. Kiviniemi warns nitric oxide can lower blood pressure, and for someone whose pressure is already low, a pill might cause fainting.

Next year the team hopes to test the combination in 60 people with mild cognitive impairment or Alzheimer's, with results possibly in the following two years.

Key facts

  • 29 adults without cognitive problems used a vibrating mattress for 40 minutes with two nitric oxide pills.
  • Blood beta-amyloid 42 rose 35 per cent on average.
  • Mattress alone or pills alone had less than half the effect on glymphatic flow in scans.

Tutankhamun's tomb was small, probably secondhand, and lost under debris

The most famous tomb in Egyptology was a modest, probably repurposed one, and its low position helped hide it for millennia.

Howard Carter led the team that found the tomb of Tutankhamun in 1922, together with his patron, George Herbert, the 5th Earl of Carnarvon. The goods inside were so spectacular that it became the most famous find in the history of Egyptology. Here's the odd part: the tomb itself was a modest affair.

It has four chambers, an entrance staircase and a corridor. It is smaller and less decorated than other royal tombs of its time. And it probably began as a tomb for someone who was not royal.

So how did a pharaoh end up there? Tutankhamun took the throne as a child and died prematurely. The tomb was possibly meant for Ay, his advisor, and was enlarged for the burial.

Ay became pharaoh next. It is possible he put his predecessor in the small tomb so he could take over a bigger royal one for himself.

Even so, Tutankhamun went in with coffins, furniture, clothing and jewelry. With so little room, it all had to be packed densely.

Then came the thieves. Robbers got in twice within a few years of the burial. After the first break-in, officials repaired and repacked damaged goods and filled the outer corridor with limestone chips. A second set of robbers burrowed through, that was noticed too, and the tomb was sealed again.

Here's the clever bit. The tomb was dug low, into the valley floor, so flooding and debris from tomb construction covered its entrance. Other tombs in the Valley of the Kings were stripped of valuables between about 1070 and 664 BC. This one was not, and the mummy and most of the goods stayed intact.

The discovery said little about his reign. It did show how wealthy ancient Egyptians lived, and how tomb robbery worked.

Today most of the goods are in the Grand Egyptian Museum in Giza, but the mummy and sarcophagus are still on display in the tomb. Flooding and heavy tourist traffic have damaged it, so a replica of the burial chamber has been built nearby. A tomb that was protected by being hidden is now worn down by being visited.

Learn · Physics from first principles

Open problems: what physics still cannot explain

The last lesson in the course is about the questions physics has not answered yet, and why those gaps are where the next ideas come from.

Keep asking "why?" about anything, and sooner or later the honest answer becomes "nobody knows yet." Physics works the same way. The last lesson of this course is about where that point sits today.

Open problems come in two flavors. Some are theoretical: our best theories cannot explain something we have observed. Others are experimental: we have an idea, but nobody has worked out how to build a test for it, or how to look closely enough at some phenomenon.

Here's the big list. The Standard Model, our working theory of particles, leaves several questions hanging: the strong CP problem, the absolute mass of neutrinos, why there is more matter than antimatter, and what dark matter and dark energy actually are. Then comes the crack in the foundation. The Standard Model's mathematics is inconsistent with general relativity, and both theories break down in extreme places, like the Big Bang and the centers of black holes.

Now for the worked example, and it is a happy one. In 1900, Lord Kelvin gave a speech naming two "clouds" over classical physics. One was the missing evidence for the luminiferous aether. The other was the equipartition theorem failing to predict the heat capacity of solids, which is how much heat a solid soaks up as it warms.

Here's the twist. Two small clouds, and within a decade both were gone. Special relativity cleared the first and quantum mechanics cleared the second. Two leftover puzzles became two new theories in ten years or less. That's the clever bit: a gap that looks like a footnote can turn out to be a door.

Physicists keep score with lists, and the scores are humbling. Isaac Newton published 31 queries about light and matter in 1704. In 1984, Barry Simon listed 15 open problems on quantum operators, and about 5 were solved by 2000, a third of them in sixteen years. He released a second list of 15 that year, and at least 3 have since been solved. Meanwhile, a list of 10 millennium problems in theoretical physics, proposed at the Strings 2000 conference, had none conclusively solved as of 2025. That is twenty-five years of silence.

Nobel laureate Vitaly Ginzburg compiled his own list from 1971 to 2005, ending with 30 problems. He added three "great problems": the arrow of time, the interpretations of quantum mechanics, and whether life can arise from physics alone. This course has no next lesson, so here is the question to carry out the door: which of today's clouds will turn out to be the next one that opens?

Key facts

  • In 1900 Lord Kelvin named two "clouds" over classical physics, and both were solved within a decade by special relativity and quantum mechanics.
  • The Standard Model's mathematics is inconsistent with general relativity, and both break down at the Big Bang and inside black holes.
  • Barry Simon's 1984 list of 15 problems had about 5 solved by 2000.
  • Vitaly Ginzburg's three great problems: the arrow of time, interpretations of quantum mechanics, and life arising from physics alone.

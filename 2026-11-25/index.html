Cover story

The UN's highest court says Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya

The International Court of Justice ruled, by nine votes to six, that Myanmar's 2017 campaign in Rakhine State was genocide, and ordered it to investigate, compensate and allow return.

How do you prove that a country meant to destroy a people, rather than just hurt them? That was the question facing the International Court of Justice, the United Nations' highest court, in a case about Myanmar and the Rohingya.

Its answer, by nine votes to six, was yes. The court declared that Myanmar "has committed genocide against the Rohingya group in northern Rakhine State", breaking the 1948 Genocide Convention.

Here's why that is hard. Genocide is not simply a word for terrible violence. It needs a specific intent: to destroy a group, or a substantial part of it. So the judges had to decide whether Myanmar's actions showed that intent, or amounted to other serious crimes.

Myanmar said they did not. It argued that its 2017 operations were counter-terrorism against ARSA, an armed Rohingya group. Even if troops used excessive force, it said, that did not prove genocidal intent.

The court rejected that central defence. It found that systematic killing of civilians, widespread sexual violence, attacks on children and the destruction of villages could not reasonably be explained as counter-terrorism. In paragraph 468, the judges said they were "fully convinced" of the intent.

The scale is hard to hold. The court cited Medecins Sans Frontieres estimating that between 6,700 and 9,867 Rohingya were violently killed in the first month alone. It examined evidence of at least 1,834 victims of rape.

More than 700,000 Rohingya fled across the border into Bangladesh, and many remain in refugee camps nearly a decade later.

Who brought the case? The Gambia, a Muslim-majority West African nation, which accused Myanmar of mass killings, sexual violence and destruction. It took seven years.

Now the orders. Myanmar must investigate people reasonably suspected of involvement and, where warranted, prosecute them. It must compensate victims, help displaced Rohingya return safely and voluntarily, and return or replace lost property.

The Gambia did not win everything. The court did not uphold its separate claim that Myanmar publicly incited private individuals to commit genocide.

So what comes next? The amount and arrangements for compensation will be decided in later proceedings, and Myanmar must report to the court within two years. That report is the next thing to watch.

Key facts

  • The ICJ ruled by nine votes to six that Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya in northern Rakhine State.
  • More than 700,000 Rohingya fled into Bangladesh, and many remain in camps nearly a decade later.
  • Myanmar must investigate suspects, compensate victims and facilitate safe return; it must report to the court within two years.
  • The case was brought by The Gambia after a seven-year legal battle.

General-purpose AI is learning to design proteins, and scientists are arguing about how

Chatbot-style language models from OpenAI and Anthropic are doing protein tasks once left to specialist AI, and nobody agrees yet on whether they understand biology or are repeating what they saw.

Around 11% of the protein designs came back from the lab doing their job. That is roughly one in nine. What makes it odd is who did the designing: a general-purpose language model, not a specialist biology tool.

Proteins are the tiny molecular machines that do work inside cells. Since Google DeepMind released AlphaFold2 in 2020, specialist AI has been the way to predict and design them, and there is now a huge ecosystem of such software.

So what changed? On 24 November, OpenAI announced that its latest models had designed new proteins. Its team asked a model called GPT-6.2 to design 3D structures that could bind five biological targets, including proteins implicated in cancer and immune disorders. A specialist tool then worked out the matching genetic sequences, and lab tests showed about 11% of the designs bound their target.

Here's the catch. In some tasks the model matched specialist tools, but at predicting 3D protein structure it was far off. Joy Jiao, who leads life-sciences research at OpenAI, calls it a proof of principle and says specialist tools were still needed for key steps.

A second jolt came earlier this month. Xinming Tu, a researcher at the AI biology company Phylo, asked models from OpenAI, Anthropic and Google to predict the sequences of obscure bacterial proteins, then left the analysis running overnight.

He woke up shocked. The latest Claude models had beaten the other language models, and also the specialized protein language models built for exactly this job. Tu and Leo Chen of Harvard Medical School shared the result in a 16 November blogpost, and computational biologists rushed to try more.

Some now expect general models to win in the end. OpenAI researcher Masatoshi Uehara says he believes a large language model "can technically beat every specialized model in even each capability".

Others are not so sure. Martin Pačesa, a structural biologist at the University of Zurich, says that for a given task a specialist will usually beat a generalist.

Then there is the doubt about what is going on inside. Think of a student who learned the chemistry versus one who memorised the answer key. Some researchers suggest part of what these models do could be regurgitated training data.

Key facts

  • About 11% of GPT-6.2's protein designs bound their targets in lab tests.
  • Claude models beat specialized protein language models at predicting obscure bacterial protein sequences in Xinming Tu's test.
  • OpenAI's model was far off at predicting 3D protein structure.
  • Some researchers suspect the models may be regurgitating training data.

Trauma in adulthood leaves a mark on the brain, even without PTSD

A post-mortem study of over 300 brains found that adult trauma was linked to fewer insulating cells, whether or not the person had PTSD.

Here's a result that surprised me. In a study of donated brains, one cell change tracked with trauma itself, whether or not the person was ever diagnosed with a disorder.

The study, in Cell Reports, used brain tissue and medical records donated by 302 people. That is 101 with post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), 101 with depression and 100 with no mental-health condition.

Why look at brains at all? Many earlier studies of PTSD and epigenetics used DNA from blood or saliva. As study co-author Frances Champagne put it, "What we really want to understand is what's going on in the brain."

First, a quick word on epigenetics. Think of your DNA as a recipe book. Epigenetic marks are chemical tags added on top, which can change how genes get used without changing the recipe's text. The team looked at one such tag, called DNA methylation.

They checked more than 850,000 regions of the genome in three brain areas. The amygdala, hippocampus and prefrontal cortex handle emotion, memory and cognitive control.

Here's the catch. Comparing the PTSD group with the controls, distinct methylation patterns turned up in four DNA regions in the prefrontal cortex. None showed up in the amygdala or hippocampus. One affected gene had not been linked to PTSD before, but has a key role in memory.

Then came the cell-level finding. The amygdala, which is central to fear responses, had significantly fewer oligodendrocytes in people with PTSD. These cells wrap the connections between neurons in an insulating sheath and help them communicate.

So is that just a PTSD thing? No. Oligodendrocytes were reduced across all three regions in 58 people who had experienced trauma as adults, diagnosed with a disorder or not. Champagne said the drop seems linked particularly to adult trauma exposure.

The twist: the researchers did not see that pattern in 97 donors who experienced childhood trauma.

Alicia Smith of Emory University, who studies the epigenetics of PTSD, said the work gives the most comprehensive look at cell type differences in these brain regions. The hope is that it helps explain why people differ in their symptoms and in how they respond to treatment. Why childhood trauma looks different here is not explained in the report.

Key facts

  • Post-mortem study of 101 PTSD, 101 depression and 100 control donors, published in Cell Reports.
  • Four DNA regions in the prefrontal cortex showed distinct methylation in PTSD.
  • Oligodendrocytes were reduced across all three regions in 58 people with adult trauma, diagnosed or not.
  • The pattern was not seen in 97 donors with childhood trauma.

Shenzhen went from 30,000 people to 20 million, and a summit landed there

A writer who first saw Shenzhen in 1986 says the city's rise explains why a summit held there was more than a photo-op.

Not long before 1986, Shenzhen was a fishing village of 30,000 people. Today it is a city of 20 million, more than 600 times as many.

Nathan Gardels, editor-in-chief of Noema Magazine, walked across the border from Hong Kong in 1986. He remembers barely any bustle, no crowds or traffic, a couple of tall buildings at most, and a gardener clipping grass with a pair of scissors.

Then came the tour. With former California governor Jerry Brown, he was shown what the hosts proudly called a tech hub. The "data centers" were cavernous, nearly empty warehouses with scores of desktop computers gathering dust. None were even plugged in.

Here's the twist. Forty years later, Shenzhen is home to Huawei, Tencent, DJI and BYD, and Gardels calls it the center of gravity of China's AI battle with the United States.

So why does that matter for a summit? Gardels says pundits who waved off the Shenzhen summit as a photo-op missed the point. In his view, the symbolism was the substance: a world where the rules-based order has given way to spheres of influence accommodating each other.

The roles flipped along the way. In 1980, Xi Zhongxun, the father of China's current leader, came to California looking for investment in Shenzhen. He toured Silicon Valley and shook hands with Mickey Mouse at Disneyland.

In 2012, his son Xi Jinping visited California, then as vice-president. Brown asked whether China might help finance California's bullet train, already in the works for nearly 20 years.

A Chinese delegation met with the governor in Beverly Hills. Nothing came of it. Those who had once come cup in hand, Gardels writes, now offered financing terms too tough to abide.

Here's the last number. By 2026, China had built a 30,000-mile network of bullet trains. California and the U.S. still have yet to build one.

Key facts

  • Shenzhen was a fishing village of 30,000 before becoming a special economic zone; it now has 20 million people.
  • In 1986 its "data centers" were empty warehouses of unplugged desktop computers.
  • Gardels argues the summit's symbolism showed spheres of influence replacing the rules-based order.
  • By 2026 China had a 30,000-mile bullet train network; the U.S. has none.

Forty-two days of fighting, and then the attackers walked away. That is how the Battle of Triangle Hill ended in 1952, when American and South Korean units abandoned their attempt to capture the "Iron Triangle".

I can't walk you through the hills, because the material I have doesn't. What it does explain is why a battle like this could happen at all.

Start with the war. The Korean War began on 25 June 1950, when North Korea invaded South Korea. North Korea was backed by China and the Soviet Union. South Korea was backed by the United Nations, led by the United States.

The first year was a seesaw. Seoul fell to North Korea within three days. By early August, South Korea and its allies held only a corner in the southeast. Then UN forces landed at Inchon in September and pushed into North Korea, until China entered the war on 19 October.

Here's the catch. After China's failed spring offensive, UN forces had retaken territory up to about the 38th parallel, the line that had split Korea in 1945. The two sides were back near where they started.

So the fighting changed shape. Armistice talks began in July 1951 but dragged on, and the war became one of attrition. That means wearing the other side down, not winning ground.

That is the world Triangle Hill belongs to. Weeks of effort, and the attempt ended with the attackers giving it up.

The talks did finish. On 27 July 1953, the Korean Armistice Agreement ended combat and created a Demilitarized Zone four kilometres wide. The war had caused around one million military deaths and an estimated 1.5 million to 3 million civilian deaths.

Now the odd part. An armistice is a pause in fighting, not a peace deal, and no peace treaty has ever been signed. Technically, the Korean conflict is still frozen in place.

Key facts

  • The Battle of Triangle Hill ended after 42 days when American and South Korean units abandoned their attempt to capture the Iron Triangle.
  • Armistice talks began in July 1951 and dragged on as the war became one of attrition.
  • Combat ended on 27 July 1953 with the Korean Armistice Agreement.
  • No peace treaty has been signed.

The man who found black holes while calculating artillery shots

In 1915 an astronomer on the Russian front solved Einstein's equations first, and the math he found describes objects Einstein insisted could not exist.

In 1915, Karl Schwarzschild was an astronomer stationed on the Russian front. His job was to calculate artillery trajectories.

Instead, he solved Einstein's brand-new equations of general relativity. He did it before Einstein himself could.

Think about that for a second. The author of the theory had not yet cracked his own equations, and a man with a war-time job got there first.

Here's the catch. Hidden in Schwarzschild's solution was the math of black holes.

Einstein insisted such objects could not exist. His own equations kept pointing at them anyway.

So why does this still matter? Because black holes have become a test of what we really know about reality. That is how physicist Brian Greene, author of The Elegant Universe, frames them in a Big Think video on the black hole information paradox.

The source gives only a glimpse of that paradox, so here is the general idea. Our best physics says information about what exists should never be truly erased, yet a black hole seems to swallow things whole. Those two ideas collide, and that collision is the puzzle.

That's the clever bit about this story. The same equations that Einstein doubted ended up creating a puzzle that physicists still use to check their understanding of the universe.

Key facts

  • In 1915 Karl Schwarzschild, an astronomer on the Russian front, was supposed to be calculating artillery trajectories.
  • He solved Einstein's general relativity equations before Einstein did.
  • His solution revealed the math of black holes, which Einstein insisted could not exist.
  • Physicist Brian Greene explains how black holes test what we really know about reality.

Learn · Physics from first principles

Open problems: how physics keeps a list of what it cannot explain

The last lesson is about the questions physicists have written down and cannot yet answer, and how those lists work.

Lord Kelvin gave a speech in 1900 that did something odd for a famous scientist. He pointed at what physics could not explain. He said classical physics, the physics of that time, had "two clouds" hanging over the new century.

Here is the everyday version. You have a drawer of tools, and almost everything fits. Then one job keeps failing. You could ignore it, or you could write it down. Physicists write it down, and the written-down failures are called open problems.

Kelvin's two clouds were these. Nobody could find evidence for the luminiferous aether, a supposed invisible substance that light was thought to travel through. And a rule called the equipartition theorem failed to predict how much heat solids soak up.

Now the clever bit. Both clouds were cleared within a decade. The first fell to special relativity, the second to quantum mechanics. So two small failures in 1900 were the start of two big new theories. A problem that will not fit is not a nuisance. It is a signpost.

Today's lists are longer, and the arithmetic is sobering. In 1984, Barry Simon listed 15 open problems about quantum operators, and about 5 were solved by 2000. That leaves about 10 still open at that point. Roger Penrose's 1982 list on general relativity had about 14 problems still unsolved in 2017. And at a 2000 conference called Strings 2000, members proposed 10 big problems in theoretical physics. As of 2025, none has been conclusively solved.

Here's the catch. Some of the biggest gaps are not on one person's list at all. The Standard Model, our best map of particles and forces, is mathematically inconsistent with general relativity, our best account of gravity. Both break down in extreme places, such as the Big Bang and the centers of black holes. Add dark matter, dark energy, and why there is more matter than antimatter, and you can see why the lists keep growing. So here is the question to carry out the door: which of today's clouds will turn out to be the next new theory?

Key facts

  • In 1900 Lord Kelvin named two clouds over physics: the aether and the equipartition theorem. Both were solved within a decade.
  • Barry Simon's 1984 list had 15 problems, and about 5 were solved by 2000.
  • The Standard Model is inconsistent with general relativity, and both break down at singularities like the Big Bang and inside black holes.
  • The 10 problems proposed at Strings 2000 had none conclusively solved as of 2025.

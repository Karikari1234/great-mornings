Cover story

A concert, an airstrike, and a war that grew out of a coup

Four years ago today, airstrikes killed at least 80 concertgoers in Kachin State, one moment in a war the 2021 coup turned nationwide.

At least 80 people who had gone to a concert were killed by Burmese military airstrikes in Kachin State, on this day in 2022. My source says nothing about that night itself. What it does explain is the war around it, and that part is worth knowing.

It starts on 1 February 2021. The Tatmadaw, Myanmar's armed forces, overthrew the elected government and detained leaders of the National League for Democracy. Min Aung Hlaing became the de facto ruler.

The Tatmadaw said the 2020 election had 8.6 million voter irregularities. It presented no evidence.

Mass protests followed, and so did a violent crackdown. Here's the catch: the country was not starting from peace.

Insurgencies, mostly along ethnic lines, have run in Myanmar since 1948. The Kachin Independence Army, one of the larger rebel groups, formed in response to an earlier coup, in 1962. So the 2021 coup did not create the fighting. It hit a fuse that was already lit.

Then the sides multiplied. The exiled National Unity Government and major ethnic armed organisations rejected the 2008 Constitution and asked for a democratic federal state. The insurgents are split into hundreds of armed groups across the country.

The air is a big part of this war. The source describes a massive aerial bombing campaign that has displaced nearly three million people. It also says the Myanmar Air Force has dropped more bombs per capita than have been dropped in the Russo-Ukrainian war.

On the ground, the picture has swung back and forth. By October 2023 the Tatmadaw controlled under 40% of the country, though it claimed about two-thirds of the 330 townships. In October 2025 the junta retook Kyaukme, a district capital it had lost in 2024.

The human cost is hard to hold in your head. By March 2023 the United Nations estimated 17.6 million people needed humanitarian help. In May 2026 the UN refugee agency reported around 299,000 people had fled to neighbouring countries, Bangladesh among them.

And the fighting has not stopped. The source says it continues in all regions and states of Myanmar, including Naypyidaw Union Territory.

Key facts

  • Airstrikes by Burmese military forces killed at least 80 concertgoers in Kachin State in 2022.
  • The war began after the military coup of 1 February 2021, on top of insurgencies running since 1948.
  • The Myanmar Air Force has dropped more bombs per capita than have been dropped in the Russo-Ukrainian war.
  • UNHCR reported around 299,000 people had fled to neighbouring countries by May 2026.

The US air campaign of 1972 was a reply to one offensive

Operation Linebacker was the US air support that backed South Vietnam's army against North Vietnam's Easter Offensive in 1972.

By 1972, most American troops had already left Vietnam. So how was the US still fighting?

The answer is air power. The US had sent combat troops in great numbers, 184,000 by 1966 and 536,000 by 1969. Then President Richard Nixon began what he called Vietnamization: South Vietnam's own army, the ARVN, would fight the war while US forces withdrew.

Here's the catch. That plan had to survive a test.

In 1972, North Vietnam launched its massive Easter Offensive against the South. With US ground forces mostly gone, American air support became the help that was left, and it came through the Linebacker Operations.

Linebacker was American air support for the ARVN, a reply to that offensive.

Why does that matter? Because it shows how the war had changed. Earlier, the US relied on air supremacy and overwhelming firepower alongside huge numbers of soldiers. By 1972, the planes were doing the job without the soldiers.

It did not end the war. The Paris Peace Accords came in 1973, and the last American forces left. Then, in the source's telling, North Vietnam violated the accords, and fighting went on until the 1975 Spring Offensive.

Saigon then fell to North Vietnam's army, which ended the war. North and South were officially reunified in 1976.

So Linebacker is a snapshot of a war in its last phase: the US stepping back from the ground, but still reaching in from the air.

Key facts

  • Operation Linebacker was US air support for the ARVN against North Vietnam's Easter Offensive.
  • Most US troops had withdrawn from Vietnam by 1972.
  • US troop levels reached 184,000 by 1966 and 536,000 by 1969.
  • The last American forces left after the 1973 Paris Peace Accords; Saigon fell in 1975.

Learn · Physics from first principles

Open problems: what physics still cannot explain

The last lesson is about the edge of the map, and how physicists keep score on what they do not know.

Ask a child why the sky is blue, then keep asking why to every answer. Sooner or later you hit a reply that starts with "nobody knows yet." Physics works the same way, and this last lesson is about where that happens.

Open problems come in two kinds. Some are theoretical: our best theories cannot explain something we have actually observed. Others are experimental: we have an idea, but nobody has worked out how to build the test that would check it.

Here are the famous ones. We do not know what dark matter and dark energy are. We do not know the absolute mass of neutrinos, or why the universe has more matter than antimatter. And the Standard Model, our theory of particles, does not fit with general relativity, our theory of gravity. Both break down in extreme places, like the Big Bang and the centers of black holes.

Is an unsolved list a bad sign? Not necessarily. In 1900, Lord Kelvin gave a speech naming "two clouds" over classical physics: no evidence for the luminiferous aether, and the equipartition theorem failing to predict the heat capacity of solids. Both were solved within a decade, by special relativity and quantum mechanics.

Here's the catch. Those little clouds did not get patched. They turned into two whole new theories.

Now a worked example with numbers, to see how slowly this goes. In 1984, Barry Simon listed 15 open problems on quantum operators. By 2000, about 5 were solved, so about 10 were still open after 16 years. His second list of 15, released in 2000, had at least 3 solved. Roger Penrose's 1982 list on general relativity still had about 14 unsolved in 2017, and the 10 problems proposed at the Strings 2000 conference had none conclusively solved as of 2025.

People have been keeping these lists for centuries. Newton published 31 queries about light and matter in 1704. Vitaly Ginzburg compiled 30 problems between 1971 and 2005, and picked out three "great problems": the arrow of time, how to interpret quantum mechanics, and whether life can arise from physics alone. This course has no lesson 16, so the question stays with you: which of today's clouds is the one that turns out to be a whole new theory?

Key facts

  • In 1900 Lord Kelvin named two "clouds" over classical physics; both were solved within a decade by special relativity and quantum mechanics.
  • Barry Simon's 1984 list had 15 problems, with about 5 solved by 2000.
  • The Standard Model is inconsistent with general relativity, and both break down at the Big Bang and inside black holes.
  • Vitaly Ginzburg's three great problems: the arrow of time, interpretations of quantum mechanics, and life arising from physics alone.

The Day, Connected

The headline is the spike, the war is the line

One anniversary shows how news captures sudden events while the slow forces underneath do the real work.

Most of what reaches you as news is a date on a calendar. Four years ago today, airstrikes killed at least 80 concertgoers in Kachin State. That is the event, and it is the part people remember.

Here's the slower part. That day sat inside a war, and the war grew out of the 2021 coup, which turned a seizure of power into a conflict across the whole country. A war like that does not arrive on one morning. It builds, and the headline shows up when it reaches a crowd.

So here is how I read the world right now, and I only have one page of evidence to do it with. The first pattern is who sets the rules. When power changes hands by force, the rules stop being shared, and everyone else lives inside the result, even at a concert. The second is what is fragile. Ordinary gatherings look sturdy until the pressure underneath finds them.

The third is about the news itself. News runs on events, and the world runs on processes. An event is a spike. A process is the line the spike sits on. We read the spike, feel it, and often miss the line.

Next time a headline lands, ask yourself: what slow thing is this the visible tip of?

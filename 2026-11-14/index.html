Cover story

Nvidia's CEO is gone. Its chips still sell. So why the panic?

Jensen Huang resigned as Nvidia CEO after affair reports, and the real risk is not sales but who steers the company next.

Nvidia's CEO is gone, and many analysts expect little hit to its near-term sales. So why has the company lost well over $600 billion in market value since Sunday?

Start with what happened. On Thursday Nvidia said Jensen Huang was resigning as president, CEO and board member, effective immediately. CFO Colette Kress is now interim CEO.

The cause was a run of reports about his personal life. Three days earlier, Huang admitted a relationship with Alice Huang, no relation, a former executive at Megaspeed. US intelligence agencies believe she was spying for China. Megaspeed is a Singapore-based cloud firm and a major Nvidia client, and it denies the allegations against it.

Then it got worse. The New York Times reported an affair with a senior executive who reported directly to him. Reuters, citing unidentified sources, alleged a second relationship with an employee.

The board had backed him after the first scandal. Market watchers believe the second did too much reputational damage. Under the bylaws, a majority of its 11 directors can remove him "with or without cause."

Now the money. Nvidia's market value fell to $4.2 trillion. Its shares dropped 10% earlier this week and were down more than 9% in premarket trading Thursday. A month ago, they were at all-time highs.

Here's the catch. Demand for Nvidia's chips is so high that many analysts expect little impact on near-term sales. Analyst Dan Ives said the company and its technology "will go on."

But Huang was the leader who picked which ideas Nvidia should bet heavily on and got the whole company behind them. Around 60 executives reported directly to him. Analysts see the biggest long-term risk in strategy and execution.

He was also Nvidia's main lobbyist on US chip export rules, and he persuaded President Donald Trump to ease some curbs on China last year. He was seen as a key-person risk, as the company's public image, strategic direction and technical decisions became closely tied to him.

And there is no obvious heir. The Wall Street Journal reports Huang does not believe in formal succession planning. Bloomberg reports the board is working with advisers on permanent candidates.

Names mooted include Kress, other executives, and Huang's children Madison and Spencer, who both work at Nvidia. One analyst floated Tim Cook and Eric Schmidt, and Trump backed Cook. Nobody yet knows who will make the next big bet.

Key facts

  • Huang resigned as president, CEO and board member effective immediately; CFO Colette Kress is interim CEO.
  • Nvidia's market value fell by well over $600 billion to $4.2 trillion since Sunday.
  • Around 60 executives reported directly to Huang.
  • The board is working with advisers to find a permanent successor.

How do you send home a sentenced ex-leader without handing her over?

India declined to comment on a report that it wants Bangladesh's military to bring Sheikh Hasina home, with fair-trial assurances.

How do you send home a former prime minister who has been sentenced to death, without looking like you handed her over? That is the puzzle India reportedly faces with Sheikh Hasina.

On 13 November, Indian foreign ministry spokesperson Randhir Jaiswal was asked about it at a briefing in New Delhi. He said that when he calls it a legal matter, he means the extradition part. On the rest: "I have no comment to offer."

So where does the rest come from? A BBC Bangla report, citing Indian government and military officials. India has not confirmed it.

The report says India has two conditions. First, Bangladesh's military should take responsibility for bringing her home, preferably on a Bangladeshi military aircraft. Or Bangladeshi soldiers could receive her at an agreed border point, with India providing transport and logistics.

Second, India reportedly wants assurances that she can appeal the death sentence and get a fresh trial over the alleged killings during the July 2024 uprising. A senior Indian foreign ministry official told BBC Bangla: "We can never knowingly push her towards the gallows."

Here's the catch. Indian officials reportedly do not want to hand her over as a prisoner. She is a longtime friend and ally, and the report says that could send a bad message to political allies in Nepal, Sri Lanka and the Maldives.

So why now? The report says India first tried to send her to a third country, approached several, and failed. Her stay is described as a growing discomfort, hurting relations with Bangladesh and drawing international criticism.

There is also a party angle. The report says India believes the Awami League, banned from activities in Bangladesh, would struggle to regain ground without her.

One more thread. The report says Indian High Commissioner Dinesh Trivedi met both Prime Minister Narendra Modi and Army Chief General Dhiraj Seth in early October. It suggests the militaries' role could have come up, though that is the report's reading.

Hasina told Reuters she wants to return in December 2026. She has not named a date. If one appears, that will be news.

Delhi took the "most polluted" crown from Lahore, and two meters disagree on how bad it is

Delhi overtook Lahore as the world's most polluted major city on Friday, and the same air got two very different scores depending on who measured it.

On Friday, the air over Delhi got two different scores. Swiss monitoring group IQAir put it at 334, called hazardous. India's own Central Pollution Control Board said 403, called severe.

Same city, same sky. The gap is down to different AQI scales and methods for calculating and labelling pollution. For reference, an AQI of 50 or less is considered good.

Either way, the number was enough. By IQAir's count, Delhi, a city of 20 million people, overtook Lahore as the world's most polluted major city. Videos from news agency ANI showed cars and motorcycles emerging from dense smog, with street lights seeming to vanish in it.

So what was Lahore doing? Holding that title for several days in a row. By Friday it had dropped, and at one hourly reading it sat seventh, with an AQI of 155.

Why does this happen every winter? Cold, heavy air traps vehicle emissions, construction dust and smoke from farmers burning crop leftovers. Think of it as a lid on a pot: whatever goes in stays in.

Lahore's numbers show how stubborn it is. A report from the Pakistan Air Quality Initiative found its average PM2.5, a fine particle that can get deep into the lungs, was eight times the World Health Organisation's guideline. It broke the national daily standard on all 40 days with valid data.

Satellites spotted 18,825 fires across Pakistan's Punjab, and 89 percent looked like crop-residue burning. Here's the catch: the report said that link does not show how much the fires matter for city air, because stagnant weather also lets pollution pile up.

Punjab's environment agency says it is pushing back. Its spokesperson, Sajjad Bashir, said brick kiln inspections rose from 37,838 to 80,594 compared with last year, and fines on industry were more than double.

Bashir also said favourable weather had helped clear the air, but that weather alone could not keep it clean. Enforcement, he said, has to continue beyond the smog season.

That is the odd part of Friday's ranking. Lahore did not win its way off the list. Delhi just got a turn at the top.

Key facts

  • IQAir put Delhi's AQI at 334 (hazardous); India's CPCB recorded 403 (severe), because the scales differ.
  • Lahore had been the most polluted major city for several days in a row before Delhi overtook it.
  • Lahore's average PM2.5 was eight times the WHO guideline, per the Pakistan Air Quality Initiative.
  • Punjab's EPA says brick kiln inspections rose from 37,838 to 80,594 versus last year.

OpenAI's AI agents can now spend your money, but only after your fingerprint

OpenAI's new ChatGPT Wallet lets its Dots agents buy things for you, with a biometric check on every purchase and a token standing in for your card number.

Your AI agent can now go shopping for you. It just can't see your credit card.

That is the idea behind ChatGPT Wallet, which OpenAI is rolling out starting today. It is a wallet for Dots, the AI agents platform OpenAI launched earlier this year.

Here's how it works. You add a card to the wallet, then ask a Dots agent to buy something. The agent does the legwork, but it checks in with you before it checks out.

So what stops it from running wild? Every purchase needs a security check with biometrics, like Face ID or a fingerprint scan, according to OpenAI's support page. No scan, no purchase.

Now the clever bit. After you approve, OpenAI says it swaps your real card details for a payment token. It works like tap to pay, Apple Pay or Google Wallet. Think of a coat check: the agent holds a ticket, not your coat. Neither the agent nor the store sees your actual card info.

Here's the catch. It is not for everyone yet. It starts with ChatGPT Pro subscribers in the US, aged 18 and older, with a US billing address. The cards must be eligible US-issued American Express, Chase, Mastercard or Visa.

There is a second catch, and it sits outside OpenAI's control. Some retailers restrict AI agents or block them altogether. Earlier this year, Amazon blocked Meta's Muse agents.

OpenAI says websites need to make sure agents are not blocked by accident. It also says payment and risk systems need to recognize legitimate purchases made by agents.

The longer plan is bigger. OpenAI expects users to eventually hand purchases fully to their agent, within clear limits such as spending caps, purchase categories or time periods, and pre-approve them.

For now, you are still the one holding the final yes. The open question is whether stores will let the agents in far enough for that to matter.

Key facts

  • ChatGPT Wallet is rolling out for US ChatGPT Pro subscribers aged 18 and older.
  • Every purchase needs biometric verification such as Face ID or a fingerprint scan.
  • A payment token replaces real card details, so agents and sellers never see them.
  • Some retailers block AI agents; Amazon blocked Meta's Muse agents earlier this year.

Chicago Fed's Goolsbee says inflation is "going the wrong way"

A Fed official says inflation has been above target for almost six years, and a rate hike looks likely next month.

Almost six years. That is how long Chicago Fed President Austan Goolsbee says US inflation has been stuck above the Fed's 2% target.

He said it on CNBC's "Squawk Box" on Friday. "Lately we're not making any progress, and if anything, it's going the wrong way."

The Fed is the US central bank, and keeping price rises near 2% a year is its job. Goolsbee says it "has definitely got work to do to continue to reestablish its credibility."

So why the alarm now? Two fresh reports. Earlier in the week, the consumer price index rose a bit more than expected in October. Then the producer price index, a proxy for wholesale prices, rose 0.3% for the month, slightly less than expected.

Here's the catch. Over the full year, that wholesale measure is up 6.5%, the highest since November 2022.

The patrol boat attack that began a battle in Croatia's war to leave Yugoslavia

On this day in 1991, Croatian commandos hit a Yugoslav patrol boat, one fight in a war over whether Croatia could leave Yugoslavia.

Croatia wanted out of a country whose army was not going to let it leave quietly. That is the whole war in one line.

On this day in 1991, Croatian naval commandos attacked the Yugoslav patrol boat Mukos. That started the Battle of the Dalmatian Channels, one chapter of the Croatian War of Independence. The source says little about the fight itself, so let's zoom out to why it happened.

Yugoslavia was a federation, and from the 1970s its leaders split into two camps. Croatia and Slovenia wanted power spread out. Serbia wanted it centralized, partly to protect the Serbs, the largest ethnic group in the country as a whole.

Then communism weakened across Eastern Europe, and the Berlin Wall fell in 1989. By March 1989, Serbia under Slobodan Milosevic had effectively ended the autonomy of Kosovo and Vojvodina and taken command of their votes in the federal presidency.

Here's the catch. Inside Croatia, a majority of Croats backed independence, but many ethnic Serbs opposed it and wanted to be in a common state with Serbia. So the argument ran between the capitals and through the middle of Croatia itself.

Croatia declared independence on June 25, 1991, agreed to postpone it, then cut its remaining ties with Yugoslavia on October 8, 1991. The Mukos attack came a little over a month later.

The Yugoslav army first tried to keep Croatia by occupying all of it. That failed, and Serb forces set up their own self-proclaimed state inside Croatia, the Republic of Serbian Krajina. After a ceasefire in January 1992 and a UN force on the ground, fighting turned intermittent, while that state covered 13,913 square kilometers, more than a quarter of Croatia.

In 1995, Croatia launched Operation Flash and Operation Storm, which effectively ended the war in its favor. The last UN-run zone was peacefully reintegrated by 1998.

The cost was heavy. Over 20,000 people were killed, and an estimated 21 to 25 percent of Croatia's economy was ruined.

One last detail. In 2015, the International Court of Justice dismissed genocide claims that Croatia and Serbia had made against each other. It said crimes against civilians had taken place, but that specific genocidal intent was not present. A war that wound down in the 1990s was still being argued in court twenty years later.

Reporting on the Israel-Gaza war triggered furious accusations of bias from all directions. Social media users parsed headlines line by line, while traditional newsrooms clashed with digital journalists who argued that open advocacy delivers more truth than old claims of objectivity.

Across the four outlets, The Australian, the ABC, 9News, and The Guardian, the coverage actually moved back and forth. Israeli narratives led in about half the surveyed articles, at 49 percent, while Palestinian narratives led in roughly one in three, at 32 percent.

Right after the October 2023 Hamas assault, every outlet focused on Israeli accounts. But as the Palestinian death toll climbed, the ABC, 9News, and The Guardian pivoted to focus on Palestinian suffering, making Palestinian frames dominant in 38 percent, 27 percent, and 54 percent of their stories.

Only The Australian stayed firmly on one side. It gave Israeli perspectives the lead in 71 percent of its articles, leaving Palestinian frames just 3 percent, barely three stories in every hundred, while linking local pro-Palestinian protests to antisemitism.

Then the researchers turned to the war in Ukraine between 2022 and 2026. Public complaints here were far milder, with almost no outcry over biased reporting.

Here is the catch. In the Ukraine coverage, Ukrainian and Western perspectives dominated 67 percent of the stories, or two out of every three. Russian narratives led in exactly zero percent.

Not a single article let a Russian viewpoint lead. When reporters mentioned Moscow's arguments about NATO expansion, it was almost always to dismiss them, with coverage framing Vladimir Putin as an irrational leader suffering a psychological malaise.

The screams about bias never meant the news had picked a side. They meant readers were actually hearing both.

Learn · Physics from first principles

The open problems where physics breaks down

Our best theories explain everyday life, but they clash completely at the extremes.

Why does your morning coffee sit peacefully on your desk while obeying two physics rulebooks that flatly contradict each other? The cup feels solid because particles and forces hold it together, while the floor keeps it steady because gravity pulls it down. Those two rulebooks seem to work in harmony right here, but push them to extremes and the math falls apart.

Physicists divide these unsolved puzzles into two basic camps: theoretical and experimental. Theoretical problems happen when existing theories cannot explain what we observe, such as dark matter, dark energy, or why the universe holds more matter than antimatter. Experimental problems happen when we struggle to build tests, such as measuring the absolute mass of neutrinos.

Here is the catch. Our leading framework for particles, the Standard Model, is mathematically inconsistent with general relativity, our theory of gravity. Think of a group chat where two members send messages fine on their own, but crash the whole thread the moment they try to talk directly.

Because of that inconsistency, both theories break down under extreme conditions. They fail completely at spacetime gravitational singularities, like the Big Bang and the centers of black holes beyond their event horizons.

To see how difficult these puzzles are to close, look at a concrete tally from mathematical physics. In 1984, Barry Simon proposed a list of 15 open problems on quantum operators. Over the next 16 years, by 2000, researchers had solved about 5 of them, leaving 10 problems unsolved.

Recent history shows that theoretical lists can prove even tougher. During the Strings 2000 conference, members proposed 10 millennium problems, and across the 25 years leading to 2025, zero have been conclusively solved. So how do physicists design an experiment to test what happens when matter and gravity meet inside a black hole?

  • The Standard Model is mathematically inconsistent with general relativity, causing both to break down at singularities like the Big Bang and black hole centers.
  • Unsolved physics problems are split into theoretical problems where theories cannot explain observations, and experimental problems where tests cannot yet be built.
  • At the Strings 2000 conference, physicists listed 10 millennium problems, none of which had been conclusively solved by 2025.

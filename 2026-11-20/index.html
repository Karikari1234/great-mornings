Cover story

A UN court has found a state guilty of genocide for the first time

The International Court of Justice ruled that Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya, the first such finding against a state by an international court.

Dozens of individuals have been found guilty of genocide. A state never had, until now.

On Thursday the International Court of Justice, the UN's top court, ruled that Myanmar committed genocide against its Rohingya minority. It is the first time an international court has found a state to have done so.

So why did it take this long? Because genocide is more than killing. The court had to find an intent to destroy a group, in whole or in part, and that this intent belonged to officials or arms of the state. Twice before, the same court weighed such claims and found too little evidence to pin them on a state.

Here's what changed. The court said "genocidal intent is the only reasonable inference that can be drawn from the evidence." Its president, Yuji Iwasawa, listed indiscriminate killings, executions of men, widespread sexual violence, extreme violence against children and the deliberate burning of homes.

The numbers are heavy. The court found around 10,000 Rohingya were violently killed in the 2017 crackdown in Rakhine State, more than 700,000 fled to Bangladesh, and about 815,000 were targeted in some way.

The crackdown began after fighters from the Arakan Rohingya Salvation Army attacked more than 30 police posts. The court recognised that. Myanmar says its forces were answering armed groups, and has always denied genocide.

Then there is what happened afterwards. Iwasawa said Myanmar carried out "extensive destruction of evidence": burning or disposing of bodies, and burning and bulldozing cleared sites. The court also pointed to a hate speech campaign that painted the Rohingya as "sub-human".

The vote was nine judges to six. The case was brought by The Gambia. Myanmar's representative, Ko Ko Hlaing, had "no comment" after the verdict.

In the Cox's Bazar camp in Bangladesh, where around a million refugees now live, Rohingya watched the ruling live. Mohammed Nowkim, driven out of Rakhine in 2017 and now in Canada, called it "formal recognition of our suffering."

A ruling is words, though. The court said Myanmar must pay proportionate reparations, then left the amount to be decided at a later date. UN human rights chief Volker Turk said the decision "must now translate into real action." Nobody can yet say what that action will be.

Key facts

  • The ICJ ruled 9 to 6 that Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya.
  • It is the first time an international court has found a state to have committed genocide.
  • The court found around 10,000 Rohingya were violently killed and more than 700,000 fled to Bangladesh.
  • Myanmar must pay proportionate reparations, with the amount to be set later.

Trump, Putin and Xi issue a peace declaration while Kyiv is under drone attack

The leaders of the US, Russia and China met together for the first time and issued a "joint declaration of peace", while Russian drones hit Kyiv.

Vladimir Putin held a press conference to say he was ready for peace. At the same time, Russian drones were attacking Kyiv.

That was the day Putin, US President Donald Trump and China's Xi Jinping put out a "joint declaration of peace". The three met for 90 minutes in Shenzhen, China, on the sidelines of the APEC summit, a gathering of Asia-Pacific economies.

Why the fuss? They have met one-on-one before. This was their first meeting as a trio.

So what did they say? The declaration says "nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought". They also said they plan to increase the production and supply of petroleum, and are "committed to reform the UN".

One line stands out. They said the International Criminal Court and similar institutions "must not abuse their jurisdiction". That court can prosecute individuals for war crimes.

Trump called the trip "a great and highly consequential trip". He said the three countries are "uniquely positioned to discuss how to reduce the number of nuclear weapons on Earth".

Here's the catch. Putin said Ukraine and Europe are not yet ready to compromise, though Russia has invited EU representatives to Moscow. The BBC reports he has repeatedly rejected Ukraine's ceasefire offers, and that Kyiv and its allies call his demands de facto capitulation.

He also vowed a "far more painful" response to Ukrainian attacks on the bridge linking Russia to Crimea. A missile and drone attack on Tuesday destroyed its central road section.

Some people compared the meeting to Yalta, the 1945 conference where Soviet, British and US leaders met near the end of World War Two. Putin pushed back: "Nobody divided up the world at Yalta. It simply codified the realities that had emerged after WW2." Then he added that today, "the realities on the ground will have to be accepted."

Read that twice. A declaration of peace, and a reminder from one of the three leaders that facts on the ground decide things.

China, for its part, calls itself neutral on Ukraine and denies helping Moscow. The BBC's own analysis called the summit high on spectacle and short on substance.

Trump said he will give a news conference about the trip at the White House on Friday. That is the next chance to hear what, if anything, the three agreed beyond the page.

Key facts

  • The three leaders met for 90 minutes in Shenzhen on the sidelines of the APEC summit, their first trilateral talks.
  • The declaration says nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought.
  • Kyiv was under Russian drone attack while Putin spoke about being ready for peace.
  • Trump plans a White House news conference on Friday about the trip.

California's attorney general subpoenas Nvidia over Jensen Huang affair reports

California's attorney general is investigating whether Nvidia misled investors about a China risk tied to reports of Jensen Huang's affair with an alleged spy.

A state government wants to know whether one of the best-known chip companies told its investors the truth about a risk involving China.

On Thursday, California Attorney General Rob Bonta said his office has served an investigative subpoena on Nvidia. A subpoena is a legal order to hand over information, and it is how an investigation gets its first documents.

What set it off? Reports that Nvidia co-founder Jensen Huang had an affair with an alleged Chinese spy. Bonta referred to Huang as the company's "former CEO".

Here's the part worth slowing down for. Bonta did not frame this as a question about the affair. He named two other questions.

First, whether the actions of Nvidia or Huang resulted in China obtaining advanced chips or confidential information. Second, whether Nvidia misled investors and the public about that risk, in violation of the law.

That second question is the legal hook. Public companies have to be straight with shareholders about risks to their business, and Bonta put it plainly: "Companies cannot mislead their investors."

He also said the conduct of Nvidia and its former CEO "raises bright red flags". He called the company's position "a messy, transnational situation".

Notice what is still missing. The subpoena is the start of an investigation, not a finding. Whether China got any chips or information at all is the thing his office says it is trying to learn.

Islam is forecast to pass Christianity as the largest religion by 2035

Pew projects Islam will overtake Christianity by the middle of the next decade, driven mostly by who is having more children.

By 2035, Pew Research Centre projects, the world will have 2.57 billion Muslims and 2.52 billion Christians. That gap is about 50 million people, roughly 2 percent of the Christian total.

Tiny gap, big deal. Christianity has ranked as the world's largest religion for centuries, and Pew expects Islam to pass it by the middle of the next decade.

So what is doing the work? Mostly babies. Pew attributes the rise mainly to fertility: 3.1 children per Muslim woman in 2020 to 2025, against 2.5 per Christian woman.

Here is a number that helps. About 2.1 children per woman is what a generation needs to replace itself. All other religious groups combined averaged 1.6, below that line.

What about people switching faiths? It matters, but less. Both religions lose more people to switching than they gain, yet Muslims are expected to lose 8 million between 2020 and 2070, while Christians lose 105 million.

Run the clock to 2070 and the picture gets wider. Pew projects 3.6 billion Muslims to 3 billion Christians, with Islam at 37 percent of humanity, up from 26 percent in 2020. Christianity holds steady at around 29 percent.

The world's population is projected to grow 29 percent over those 50 years. Islam and Christianity are the only two religious groups forecast to keep pace or beat that, growing 80 percent and 30 percent.

Everyone else slips. Hindus fall slightly as a share, to 13.6 percent. Buddhists are forecast to decline in number and share, to 2.5 percent. People with no religious affiliation drop from 24 percent to 17 percent.

The map shifts too. Pakistan's Muslim population is expected to double, from 227 million to 461 million, making it the world's largest. In the United States, Christianity stays the largest religion but loses its majority, and the Democratic Republic of Congo overtakes the US as the country with the greatest concentration of Christians.

One last thing worth holding onto. Pew's own wording is "if recent trends hold," and the fertility figures come from just five years, 2020 to 2025. A 2070 forecast is a long stretch of line drawn from a short stretch of data.

Learn · Physics from first principles

Where physics runs out of answers

An open problem can be a failed explanation or an experiment we cannot yet do.

You can watch a clock move forward. You never watch it run backward on its own. Why time has a direction is still counted among physics' great open problems. That is a strange place to end a course about how the world works: with something you see every day.

A physical theory is a set of ideas that explains what happens and predicts what should happen next. An open problem appears when a theory cannot explain an observation, or when we cannot yet make the experiment needed to test an idea. Those are different obstacles. One asks for a better explanation; the other asks for a way to check one.

Here is a worked example. In 1900, Lord Kelvin named two problems in the physics of his day. There was no evidence for the proposed aether, a medium thought to carry light. And a theory failed to predict how much heat solids could hold. Within a decade, special relativity had resolved the first problem and quantum mechanics the second. Two questions on Kelvin's list led beyond the theories then in use.

Today's lists have their own gaps. The Standard Model describes particles; general relativity describes gravity. The source says their mathematical frameworks do not fit together, and both break down under extreme conditions, including those associated with the Big Bang and the centers of black holes.

There are questions within particle physics too, including what dark matter and dark energy are and why matter and antimatter are unequal. Giving a puzzle a name does not solve it. The name tells physicists what an explanation must account for.

That is the lesson open problems leave us with: a theory can explain a great deal and still show us exactly where it stops. What would a new theory have to explain where particles and gravity meet?

Key facts

  • Open problems can be theoretical or experimental.
  • Kelvin identified two problems in 1900; both were resolved within a decade.
  • The Standard Model and general relativity are mathematically inconsistent under extreme conditions.
  • The arrow of time appears on Ginzburg's list of great problems.

Why CAGE rejects Britain's anti-Muslim hostility definition

CAGE says the government's definition looks for hateful intent while missing discrimination it says is built into state policy.

Why would a definition meant to protect Muslims leave out the word "Islamophobia"? That is the question CAGE International asks about the British government's definition of "anti-Muslim hostility." Its answer begins with a detail that changes everything: what the definition requires someone to prove.

The definition, unveiled in March, has three parts. In each, CAGE says, a person must have intended to stir up hatred. It has no legal force and gives no examples of what anti-Muslim hostility looks like in practice.

That might sound like a fight over wording. CAGE says it is a fight over what can be seen. Its report argues that discrimination can happen through rules and routine decisions, even when nobody sets out to encourage hatred.

Here is the example at the heart of it. Since 2015, teachers, doctors and nursery staff have had a legal duty under Prevent to watch for signs of radicalisation. Prevent is a government counter-terrorism programme.

CAGE points to a school referring a child after the child expresses sympathy with Palestinians. In the report's telling, the school is carrying out a duty, rather than trying to stir up hatred. A definition that looks only for that intent would miss the case CAGE wants examined.

The figures show the scale of the harm. The article says a mosque in the UK is targeted every five days, and hate crimes against Muslims rose 15% in a year to their highest recorded level. That rise means 15 more crimes for every 100 recorded the year before. The figures do not establish who is responsible.

CAGE's wider claim is that state policy helps produce suspicion of Muslims. The article cites research by CAGE's Asim Qureshi saying a Muslim child was 17 times more likely than a white British child of no faith to be referred for deradicalisation, after accounting for the size of each group. It also says a UN counter-terrorism rapporteur found Prevent discriminatory toward Muslim communities.

CAGE proposes another route: Muslim communities should draw up their own definition and use existing equality law. That follows its claim that naming Islamophobia would force the government to examine its own policies.

The Muslim Council of Britain and the Islamophobia Response Unit have already refused to endorse the government's definition, the article says.

Key facts

  • The definition uses "anti-Muslim hostility" and omits "Islamophobia."
  • CAGE says each of its three parts requires proof of intent to stir up hatred.
  • Prevent places a duty on teachers, doctors and nursery staff to watch for signs of radicalisation.
  • The Muslim Council of Britain and the Islamophobia Response Unit have refused to endorse the definition.

The gap between saying and doing

Today’s headlines show how much depends on what happens after a decision is announced.

A court has found a state guilty of genocide, and Rohingya are still asking what comes next. The ruling gives a name to what happened to them. It leaves another question open: who can turn that finding into help for people whose practical needs remain unanswered?

You can see a version of that gap elsewhere. Bangladesh has a garment wage board, yet the dispute continues. Trump, Putin and Xi issued a peace declaration while drones struck Kyiv. These events carry very different weight, but each makes you look past the announcement. A rule or a promise matters partly through what people can do with it afterward.

There is a second thread: who answers when something goes wrong? California’s lawsuit over an attack traced to OpenAI’s servers tests responsibility for an AI’s actions. Trust grows harder to place when the people affected must first find out whom they can hold to account. That question runs through a courtroom, a wage dispute and a war, even though the answers in each case will differ.

News gives us moments we can point to: a ruling, a declaration, a lawsuit. Under each moment lies a slower process of deciding who has authority, who can challenge it and whether a decision changes daily life. That is why a headline can be both important and incomplete. What long process brought this moment here, and who can change what happens next?

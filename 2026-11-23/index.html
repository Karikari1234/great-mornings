Asked if Putin is America's friend, the intelligence chief said no, in Ukraine

After Trump called Putin "my friend," US intelligence chief Jay Clayton defended the line as appropriate, while saying Putin is no friend to America in Ukraine.

The question was simple: is Vladimir Putin America's friend? Trump had said he couldn't answer it. On Sunday, the man who runs US intelligence had to try.

That man is Jay Clayton, the Director of National Intelligence. On ABC's This Week, Jonathan Karl pressed him on it.

Clayton's answer came in two parts. "He is not America's friend in Ukraine. Not at all. Not at all."

But he also called Trump's friendly words "entirely appropriate." On CNN he went further: "whatever language the president wants to use, I trust him completely."

So where did the "friend" line come from? On November 19, Trump, Putin and Chinese President Xi Jinping held a roundtable on the sidelines of the APEC conference in Shenzhen, China. Seeing Putin, Trump joked, "Who is he?" Then he crossed the room to shake his hand. "He's my friend."

Trump later posted images of the meeting, one captioned "My Friend." Asked at a Friday briefing whether Russia is a friend to America, he said, "I can't speak to America." He said he and Putin got close while dealing with the "Russia, Russia, Russia hoax."

Here's the catch. Washington has backed Ukraine since Russia's full-scale invasion in February 2022, sending billions in security assistance and weaponry. Under Trump, that support has cooled, and he has criticised Zelenskyy for failing to cut a deal with Russia.

Outgoing Representative Don Bacon accused Trump of "playing footsie" with Putin and said Republicans traditionally stood up to Russia and China.

Clayton's defence leaned on the nuclear side. He called it assuring that the three nuclear powers got together to say they wouldn't go in that direction. The meeting ended with a statement about "reducing nuclear risks," though it contained no firm commitments.

So the official line has two layers. Friendly words are a tool for building rapport with Putin. On Ukraine, Clayton says, the two are not on the same side.

Key facts

  • Jay Clayton said Putin is 'not America's friend in Ukraine' but called Trump's remarks 'entirely appropriate'.
  • Trump, Putin and Xi met on November 19 at the APEC conference in Shenzhen.
  • The meeting ended with a statement about 'reducing nuclear risks', but it contained no firm commitments.
  • Rep. Don Bacon accused Trump of 'playing footsie' with Putin.

Banned party's leaders spent two hours at an Indian border post. Nobody will say why.

Awami League leaders Jahangir Kabir Nanak and Bahauddin Nasim were seen at the Petrapole immigration centre in West Bengal, and officials declined to comment.

Two leaders of a banned political party spent about two hours inside an Indian immigration centre on Sunday afternoon. The officials who work there won't say what they were doing.

The party is Bangladesh's Awami League, whose activity is banned. The two men are Jahangir Kabir Nanak and Bahauddin Nasim. Multiple sources said four people were there in total.

The place is Petrapole, a land port on the India side of the border. Reach the Bangaon subdivision in West Bengal's North 24 Parganas, keep heading toward the port, and the immigration centre is on your right.

Here's what we know about how they came. They arrived in a white car. They did not use the entrance that local people use, and journalists waiting at the scene said they came and went by a different route.

So what happened inside? That's the blank. The immigration department, India's Border Security Force (the country's border guard) and Petrapole police station all did not want to comment.

Here's the odd timing. At the same moment, a procession was held there, led by several BJP leaders from Bangaon North and South, along with state minister Ashok Kirtania. MLA Swapan Majumdar was also there.

A Hindu nationalist organisation called Hindu Suraksha Samiti organised it. The marchers demanded the release of Chinmoy Krishna Das, spokesperson of the Sanatani Jagaran Jote, who is imprisoned in Bangladesh, and alleged that minorities were being persecuted there.

Later, Majumder told local media that Sheikh Hasina could enter Bangladesh by this route: from Kolkata along Jashore Road, through Bangaon and Petrapole.

Why does that line matter? Hasina was ousted from power in the July mass uprising and fled to India. She has been staying there since 5 August 2024.

Several Awami League leaders now in West Bengal claim an attempt to enter Bangladesh is planned for mid-December. They say many party leaders are considering going in alongside her.

Nothing in the report ties Sunday's visit to that plan, and I won't either. Mid-December is the date to watch. If officials ever explain those two hours, or anyone actually heads for the border, the picture changes fast.

Nepal's rapper-turned-PM makes Delhi his first bilateral stop

Nepal's prime minister Balendra Shah is in India for talks on flood recovery, trade and energy with the country that sells Nepal most of its imports.

More than 60% of what Nepal imports comes from a single country. By World Bank figures, that is $8.6 billion of goods, or a bit over six of every ten dollars Nepal spends on foreign products.

That country is India, and it is where Nepal's prime minister, Balendra Shah, is now. He is 36, a former rapper, and in office since March. This is his first bilateral foreign trip as prime minister.

So why Delhi first? Look at the other side of the ledger. China supplies 13% of Nepal's imports, and India and China regularly compete for influence in Nepal.

The visit runs two days. Shah meets Prime Minister Narendra Modi on Monday, and also meets Foreign Minister S Jaishankar and will briefly visit President Droupadi Murmu. Finance Minister Swarnim Wagle and Foreign Minister Shishir Khanal travel with him.

Floods come first. August's floods left some 1,500 people dead and more than 5,200 missing. Roads, bridges, power plants, farms and homes were destroyed, and rebuilding is expected to dominate the talks.

Then comes the everyday business of neighbours. Nepal's Foreign Secretary, Amrit Bahadur Rai, said the leaders would discuss trade, transit, investment, energy, connectivity and infrastructure. He called India "a major and important partner for Nepal."

There is one smaller, very concrete ask. Nepal is pressing for an extra route for commercial flights to Bhairahawa and Pokhara.

Here's the catch. Local media reported the talks may also touch unresolved border disputes, which is rarely a quick conversation between neighbours.

New Delhi said the visit "reflects the importance India attaches to its relations with Nepal." The question for Monday's meeting with Modi is whether that importance turns into concrete help for flood reconstruction and the flight route Nepal wants.

Key facts

  • Balendra Shah, 36, took office in March and is on his first bilateral foreign trip, to New Delhi.
  • He meets Modi on Monday; flood reconstruction, trade and energy are expected topics.
  • August floods left some 1,500 dead and more than 5,200 missing.
  • India supplies more than 60% of Nepal's imports, worth $8.6 billion; China supplies 13%.

A U.N. court said Myanmar committed genocide. Now comes the hard part

The International Court of Justice ruled 9 to 6 that Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya, but a ruling needs governments willing to act on it.

Nine judges said yes. Six said no. Switch just two votes and the answer flips.

That was the split at the International Court of Justice, the U.N. court that settles disputes between countries. On November 19 it ruled that Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya, a Muslim minority.

The ruling covers a 2017 military "clearance operation" in northern Rakhine State. The court found Myanmar's security forces killed thousands of Rohingya civilians, carried out widespread sexual violence, committed extreme violence against children, and attacked people as they fled in large numbers to Bangladesh.

It also gave orders. Myanmar must pay reparations, allow the safe and dignified return of displaced Rohingya, reunite families, and take steps to prevent further violations of the Genocide Convention.

Here's the catch. The court has no police force. Myanmar has already called the ruling "unfair" and a "great disappointment," and enforcing it will depend on whether other governments can muster the political will.

So who is stepping up? The United Kingdom was the most outspoken. It ordered the Myanmar defense attache out and called urgently for a U.N. Security Council session. Australia, Norway and the European Union backed the ruling too, and the United States did so in a more restrained tone.

Myanmar's neighbours were quieter. Only the Philippines and Malaysia had issued official statements, and Thailand had addressed it at length. Cambodia, Vietnam, Laos, Indonesia and several others had not responded.

Malaysia's statement was the warmest. It said it "respects" the ruling. The case itself was brought in 2019 by The Gambia, on behalf of the Organization of Islamic Cooperation.

There may be a second reason Malaysia cares. South Africa brought a genocide case against Israel over Gaza in 2024, and much of the international response to the Myanmar ruling has concerned what it means for that one. The Diplomat suggests Malaysia's stance may say more about Israel than about Myanmar.

Watch whether the Security Council meeting the U.K. asked for actually happens. If it does, we will learn whether a court's verdict can turn into pressure.

Key facts

  • The ICJ ruled 9 to 6 that Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya in a 2017 operation in northern Rakhine State.
  • The court ordered reparations and the safe return of displaced Rohingya; Myanmar called the ruling unfair.
  • The U.K. expelled Myanmar's defense attache and called for a Security Council session.
  • Only the Philippines and Malaysia among ASEAN governments had issued official statements.

A senator's Helen of Troy joke points at a bigger question about Nvidia chips

A US senator mocked Jensen Huang's reported affair, then said the real issue is how China got advanced AI chips despite US export controls.

A US senator called a tech boss's reported affair "the most expensive sex since Helen of Troy." Then he said he was less interested in what the boss did.

That senator is John Kennedy, a Louisiana Republican, speaking on NBC's Meet the Press on Sunday. In Greek myth, Helen was so desirable that she caused the Trojan war. Host Kristen Welker had asked whether the affair put US national security at risk.

Some background. Jensen Huang stepped down as Nvidia's chief executive on 12 November after allegations of extramarital relationships with a suspected Chinese spy, Alice Huang, and two Nvidia employees. Nvidia's chips sit at the front of the AI boom.

So why "expensive"? Alice Huang, also known as Huang Le, was executive director of Megaspeed International. Megaspeed facilitated about $12bn in chip sales in violation of US export controls, which are rules that bar selling certain technology to certain countries. The Trump administration has banned selling advanced AI chips to China.

Kennedy said he was less interested in what Huang did than in how China was evidently able to get around those controls. "Mr Huang … did what he did," he said. "I hope he had a good time."

Here's the catch. He is not alone in worrying about the chips. National intelligence director Jay Clayton said he suspected China obtained Nvidia's most advanced AI chips, and told Fox News: "There are chips that have ended up in places that they should not."

Kennedy's target is the commerce department. Its secretary, Howard Lutnick, has faced scrutiny over allegedly ignoring warnings that Megaspeed was smuggling chips to China. Other leaders, including Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, wanted Megaspeed on a trade blacklist, but commerce stalled enforcement.

Kennedy said leaders there are either "so incompetent you wouldn't put them in charge of a food truck" or "they knowingly allowed this to happen." He wants Bessent and Lutnick under oath before a Senate committee to "answer some hard questions."

Bessent, on CBS's Face the Nation, said the administration has been in touch with Nvidia's temporary management, lead directors and lawyers. It is trying to find out who was in charge of compliance. "We want to know what the culture was," he said.

So the senator's two options sit on the table, incompetence or knowing.

So here is a fresh test. Rockstar's 5,000 staff built Grand Theft Auto VI without generative AI. Reports say 30,000 unique characters in it were made by hand.

The game made $3.79 billion in sales by the end of its first day, preorders included. Split that across 5,000 people and it is roughly $758,000 each, in one day.

The old version of this story ended badly. In the 19th century, William Morris and the British Arts and Crafts movement resisted industrial machines. Handwork couldn't compete on price, so only the well-off could afford it. Morris complained he spent his life serving "the swinish luxury of the rich."

Here's the catch for Morris: digital goods play by different rules. Make something carefully by hand once, and the cost spreads across millions of buyers. It becomes a mass-market product, not a rich person's toy.

Why would buyers care who made it? Azeem Azhar, who writes the newsletter this comes from, argued back in 2017 that AI would make perfection cheap, and human imperfection would become the scarce part. One study found people rated AI-generated paintings more favorably when they were labeled human-made.

Now the oddest example. Mathematicians have been chasing a proof of the Riemann hypothesis for 167 years, and two arrived on the same day.

Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, says he and Claude may have proved it. The same day, Stanford's Ravi Vakil rushed out a proof by Alessandro Masullo, which he called "all human, with no AI."

The risk is that a "certified human" tier gets drowned out by an ocean of synthetic filler. Azhar says his own Instagram feed has become one. So who will do the certifying?

An archaeologist watched the trailer for Amy Poehler's "Dig" and noticed the shade

Real archaeologists say Peacock's "Dig" gets the unglamorous parts of a Greek excavation right, and the scenery wrong.

Maria Liston has taken part in digs in Greece since 1987. When she watched the trailer for Peacock's new series "Dig," what caught her eye was the shade canopies.

"We never got shade," she says. Now it is standard, because insurance companies and university lawyers do not like it when people drop from heat exhaustion. So the show got right a detail that has nothing to do with buried treasure.

Here's the setup. In "Dig," Amy Poehler plays Elise, the second in command of a university-sponsored excavation in Greece, who is eager for her boss Neville (Hugh Laurie) to retire. Then she and three female colleagues uncover a generation-defining artifact and land in an international conspiracy.

So why does a comedy care about shade? Co-creator Michael Schur, who made "Parks and Recreation" with Poehler, sees archaeology as a lot like work in small-town municipal buildings. It is essential and often underappreciated. He calls it a discipline that "isn't very glamorous and doesn't make people famous and rich."

To keep the show honest, the team hired Joseph Rife, a Vanderbilt classicist who has worked in Greece since 1990. He talked the producers through how a work site flows: some people push wheelbarrows, some sift dirt, some actually dig.

Liston says the undergraduates are the labor on a real dig. Greek workmen handle the more skilled and potentially more dangerous tasks.

Then there are the dogs. In the trailer, Elise says, "Do not feed the dogs, ever." In real life, Liston says, Americans rarely listen to such warnings: Athens has lots of strays, and "they all get named, and they do get fed."

Here's the catch. The scenery is wrong. The show's site sits in a vague spot, though Rife says it is in the Peloponnese, in southern Greece. Liston says it is "way too green," with more rainfall than the real south and trees more like northern Greece. Most of it was filmed in California.

One more reality check: an American team cannot just set up a dig anywhere. Greece restricts foreign countries to three independent dig sites at a time, plus three run with local archaeologists.

So the detail that caught Liston's eye says a lot about the world the show is set in. Archaeology is mostly sweat, students and sensible rules, and sometimes a lawyer's idea of a canopy.

Key facts

  • "Dig" arrives on Peacock on November 23, with Amy Poehler as archaeologist Elise.
  • Historical consultant Joseph Rife helped the producers show how a real dig site works.
  • Maria Liston praised the shade canopies and trenches but said the greenery is wrong for southern Greece.
  • Greece limits foreign countries to three independent dig sites at a time, plus three collaborations.

Learn · Physics from first principles

Where the laws of physics break down

Our two core theories explain the cosmos, but they collapse when forced into the same room.

You might assume that physics has already mapped out the basic machinery of reality. You drop a spoon, and gravity pulls it down; you warm a solid mug, and it holds heat. But the theories that explain how these everyday things behave are quietly split down the middle.

Think of a kitchen where two trusted recipe books give conflicting instructions for the exact same ingredient. In modern physics, the Standard Model rules the subatomic world, while general relativity governs gravity and space. Both work with great accuracy on their own, yet their mathematical frameworks contradict each other.

Here is the catch. When conditions become extreme, such as at the Big Bang or at the centers of black holes beyond their event horizons, both theories break down completely. Physicists also face major theoretical mysteries like dark matter, dark energy, and the unknown mass of neutrinos, where existing theories fail to explain what we observe.

So how fast do these puzzles get solved? In 1984, the mathematical physicist Barry Simon listed 15 open problems, and by 2000 researchers had solved 5 of them, clearing one third of the list in 16 years. Yet progress can also stall: when physicists at the Strings 2000 conference drew up 10 millennium problems, zero had been conclusively solved 25 years later in 2025.

This habit of cataloging our ignorance is as old as modern science. Back in 1704, Isaac Newton ended his book Opticks with 31 queries about light and matter, while Nobel laureate Vitaly Ginzburg later compiled 30 open problems, including the arrow of time and how life arises from physics alone. Every unsolved puzzle marks the exact edge where our map of the universe runs out.

Which brings us to the question for next time. If the rules of the subatomic world and the rules of gravity shatter when they meet at the center of a black hole, how can a single framework ever reconcile them?

Key facts

  • The Standard Model and general relativity mathematically contradict each other and break down at black hole centers and the Big Bang.
  • Barry Simon proposed 15 open problems on quantum operators in 1984, with about 5 solved by 2000.
  • None of the 10 millennium problems proposed at the Strings 2000 conference has been conclusively solved as of 2025.
  • Isaac Newton published a list of 31 open queries about light and matter in the 1704 edition of Opticks.

The gap between a decision and a changed life

Today's news shows how much depends on who can make a rule hold.

A court can find genocide, and refugees can still be unable to go home. The ruling on Myanmar cannot, by itself, bring the Rohingya home. That gap between a public decision and a changed life runs through today's news.

Rules depend on people who can carry them into the world. A climate draft can set an electricity goal while leaving out a fossil fuel commitment. Advanced AI chips can reach China despite US export controls. Writing a rule is one step. Who can enforce it, and who can get around it, decides what the rule does.

Trust faces a similar test. You can point to a process, a board, a negotiation or a ruling, but people judge it by what follows. Bangladesh's garment factories faced damage from buyers leaving after an 18-day stoppage. Work can resume while the loss keeps moving through future orders.

A headline catches the moment something becomes visible: a vote, a deal, a strike, a court decision. Beneath it are slower changes in trust and dependence, and long arguments over who sets the terms. Those changes have no neat date, yet they often decide how long an event matters. When you read a headline, what was already happening underneath it, and who can make the outcome stick?

Two Italian technicians killed in Kuwait; Iran says it did not do it

Two Italian workers servicing air defence systems in Kuwait died in a drone strike that Italy and Kuwait blame on Iran, and Iran denies it.

The two men who died in Kuwait worked on air defence. They were technicians for the German firm Rheinmetall, and Rheinmetall says they were providing technical support for air defence systems.

Italy's foreign ministry confirmed the deaths. Italian press agency Ansa, citing the ministry, says the men were doing maintenance on systems used by the Kuwait Armed Forces. Italian media have named them. One was reported to be 36, the other 65 and nearing retirement.

Here's the contested part. Italy and Kuwait both blame Iran. Kuwait's army described the target as an essential site and spoke of "heinous Iranian aggression". Italian media quote Italy's foreign minister as saying the drone was made in Iran.

Iran says no. Its armed forces spokesperson, Abolfazl Shekarchi, said "no military or offensive operations have been conducted" by Iran. Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi called the incident "suspicious" and said Tehran would work with Kuwait and Italy to "determine the cause".

Then he added a line that is hard to read. He called it a "precooked recipe for war", and did not expand on that.

Kuwait's defence ministry gave an earlier account. It said a number of drones from Iran had been detected and intercepted, and that four people were hurt by falling shrapnel: the two Italians, a Kuwaiti and a Pakistani.

So why does Kuwait come up at all? The war began in late February, when the US and Israel attacked Iran. Iran answered with missiles and drones aimed at Israel and at Gulf Arab states that host US forces.

Kuwait said just over a week earlier that it had repelled Iranian drone attacks on an airbase hosting US forces. Those were the first strikes on the country in more than two months. With US and Iranian fire exchanged recently and talks deadlocked, there are fears of a major escalation.

Italian Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni offered condolences to the families. European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen said the EU must step up pressure on Iran.

Two governments say Iran did it. Iran says it did not, and wants to find the cause together with them. Which account holds up may decide how much further this goes.

Key facts

  • Two Italian Rheinmetall technicians died in a strike on a facility in Kuwait, per Italy's foreign ministry.
  • Italy and Kuwait blame Iran; Iran denies responsibility and calls the incident suspicious.
  • The men were working on air defence systems used by the Kuwait Armed Forces.
  • The war began when the US and Israel attacked Iran in late February.

Bangladesh garment workers won a 36% raise, then clashed with police

A 36% minimum wage rise for Bangladesh's garment workers fell short of their demands, and a day later they were clashing with police.

Bangladeshi garment workers got a 36% minimum wage increase. A day later, they were clashing with police.

That sounds backwards. A raise is what you asked for, isn't it?

Here's the catch. The raise fell short of what the workers demanded. A big-sounding percentage can still be smaller than the number people were fighting for.

So who decides what a shirt worker earns? Part of the answer sits far from the factory floor. Labour rights groups are urging global fashion brands to pay their suppliers more, so that workers can earn a living wage.

A living wage means enough to cover the basics of life, not just the legal minimum. The logic is simple: a factory can only pay out what it takes in, and much of what it takes in comes from the brands placing the orders.

That leaves a gap between what workers demanded and what they got. Who fills it, the brands, the factories or the workers, is the question the streets are asking.

Key facts

  • Workers clashed with police a day after a 36% minimum wage increase.
  • The increase fell short of workers' demands.
  • Labour rights groups urge global fashion brands to pay suppliers more so workers can earn a living wage.

Karachi building collapse: four dead, 17 families still searching

A building under construction fell in Karachi, four bodies have been found, and the province's chief minister says it was one of 123 being built under stay orders.

The building that collapsed in Karachi's Baloch Colony was being built under a stay order. That is what Sindh Chief Minister Murad Ali Shah said, and he added that 123 buildings in the city were being built the same way.

It fell on Wednesday. It was a ground-plus-five-storey building with one penthouse, on 600 square yards, and over 20 workers were reported to be at the site. Officials and witnesses said it came down while work to dig a borehole was under way.

By Wednesday, authorities had confirmed three deaths and 14 injuries. The search stopped that night and resumed Thursday, and by evening teams had pulled out another body, a middle-aged man. The death toll is now four.

Here's the catch. Families of 17 missing people had approached an information desk set up with the East district administration.

So the rescue is slow on purpose. Rescue 1122 spokesperson Hassaanul Haseeb Khan called it delicate and said crews were avoiding heavy machinery in case anyone was alive under the debris. Rescuers first use sensors and vibration to look for people, then clear the rubble.

They even persuaded a private house owner to demolish a wall, to get a second way in.

Then came the fallout. On Thursday the chief minister had Waseem Shamshad removed as Sindh local government secretary and as head of the Sindh Building Control Authority, or SBCA. The East deputy commissioner and the East senior police superintendent were also removed over "negligence".

He ordered 16 SBCA officials suspended and investigated. He also told police to arrest the owner, Salman Tahir, and the builder, Zeeshan. The architect and structural engineer will be part of the probe into whether the work followed approved plans, building regulations and safety standards.

Murad also said the court had been asked to decide the cases of those 123 buildings within 15 days. "We are going to have a reset in the Sindh Building Control Authority," he said.

Two things to watch: the court's answer on those 123 buildings, and what the inquiry finds about how this one got built.

Two US squeezes could slow China's next big AI models, sources say

New US chip rules and tougher defences against copying are delaying flagship models at Chinese AI firms Z.AI and Moonshot, people familiar with them say.

Six months. That's how long Moonshot AI, the Beijing company behind the Kimi models, usually waits between major releases. Internally, according to a person familiar with its operations, it expects that gap could stretch to 12 months or longer if it loses overseas computing access entirely.

Why would it double? Because two things a Chinese AI lab needs to build a top model come from outside China. And the US is tightening its grip on both.

Supply line one: computing power. Training a big AI model takes huge numbers of advanced chips, mostly Nvidia's. Nvidia's H100 chips have needed US export licenses to go to China since 2022, so the chips stay abroad and Chinese firms rent time on them in overseas data centers. Moonshot does exactly this, that person said, and prefers overseas providers.

Here's the catch. A US Commerce Department rule that took effect on November 19 requires licenses for overseas data centers giving Chinese companies large amounts of computing power from advanced US chips. The line sits at roughly the equivalent of 750 H100s. Operators already doing it get 90 days to obtain a license.

Can computing inside China fill the gap? Not for Moonshot, the same person said. Another source said the company is renting more in Europe and plans to ask Chinese chipmakers about training on their processors.

Supply line two: answers. Distillation means training an AI on answers produced by a more capable one. Think of a student who learns by quizzing the top kid in class thousands of times and studying the replies.

In September, Anthropic said it had seen nearly 200 million exchanges with its Claude models linked to suspected distillation campaigns, which it attributed to Chinese developers including Alibaba, Moonshot and Z.AI. OpenAI has made similar accusations against people tied to Moonshot.

Moonshot has rejected the idea that its Kimi K3 model got its performance this way, crediting original changes to its design. But a person familiar with the company says it has begun distilling from Google and Meta models as Anthropic and OpenAI got harder to learn from.

Z.AI, also known as Zhipu AI, shows the squeeze in action. It planned to release its next flagship, GLM-5.5, in November, built with roughly 3 trillion parameters, the internal settings a model learns. It hasn't. One source said better anti-distillation safeguards partly held it up.

What came out instead, on November 23, was GLM-5.4, built on an existing model with 750 billion parameters. That's a quarter the size of the one it was meant to be.

The date to watch is mid-February, when that 90-day license window closes. A source says the rules could push GLM-5.5 into early 2027 or later, and Kimi K4 to around mid-2027. Neither company immediately responded to Reuters, and nobody yet knows whether those data centers will get their licenses.

Key facts

  • A US rule effective November 19 requires licenses for overseas data centers supplying Chinese firms with computing power above roughly 750 Nvidia H100s' worth.
  • Z.AI's planned GLM-5.5 (about 3 trillion parameters) missed its November release; a source blames anti-distillation safeguards in part.
  • Moonshot expects its six-month gap between major models could grow to 12 months or more if it loses overseas computing entirely.
  • Anthropic linked nearly 200 million Claude exchanges to suspected distillation by Chinese developers; Moonshot disputes that its K3 relied on distillation.

Scott Aaronson, at the University of Texas at Austin, wrote on his blog that he is "virtually certain, from multiple reliable sources, that the AI labs are now sitting on one or more bombshells."

Here's the catch. He says he is much less confident about what those bombshells actually are, and Nature could not confirm the rumours.

So why would anyone care? Public-key cryptography helps secure everything from banking apps to Internet browsers. It relies on maths problems that are considered practically impossible to solve in a reasonable time.

One famous example is RSA. Multiplying two huge prime numbers is easy. Working backwards from the product to the original primes is a much slower process, and that slowness is the lock. One rumour is that the labs found an algorithm that does it.

The key phrase is "polynomial time". It means the work grows fairly gently as the problem grows, say with the square or cube of its size. Exponential problems, where steps grow like 2 to the power n, quickly leave any polynomial one behind.

Does that mean the Internet is broken? Not necessarily. An algorithm needing n to the power of 10,000 steps would be hopeless to use.

But Aaronson points out that proving a problem is in this easy class has usually been followed by improvements, and eventually efficient solutions. "The idea that [the problem] was hard," he tells Nature, "is dead."

The true worst case would be if P = NP, a long-standing open question. Most researchers think that is unlikely. Earlier this week, Anthropic and OpenAI announced results suggesting many similar problems could be hard to solve, which leaves researchers cautiously optimistic.

MIT's Ryan Williams says he is "definitely blown away by the latest developments." Whether the rumoured algorithms exist, and what they do, remains unconfirmed.

An AI edited 0.016 percent of another AI and its math score jumped

MIT researchers let OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra read and edit the raw weights of other AI models, and it fixed faults without conventional retraining.

Astra changed 0.016 percent of a small AI model's connections, and the model's score on a math test jumped from 7.9 to 48.1 percent. That is 16 changes for every 100,000 connections.

First, what are those connections? An AI model is built from layers of units called neurons, and each link has a number, a weight, that sets its strength. Training is trial and error: every mistake nudges the weights. With millions or billions of weights changed along the way, the result is notoriously hard to understand.

Here's the new bit. Researchers at MIT gave GPT-6 Astra, a large language model from OpenAI, another model's weights and let it work. It read the numbers alone, without conventionally training the target.

In one test, a small reasoning model called Qwen kept getting stuck in loops on tough math problems. Astra tweaked those few weights, and accuracy on the MATH-500 benchmark rose with no additional training.

In another, a virtual car's controller failed 256 attempts to back up before jumping a ramp. After Astra edited roughly 3.5 percent of its weights, it nailed the move in all 1,024 later trials.

Wait, how? Astra does not know what every part does. Think of a seasoned mechanic with an unfamiliar engine: it can find the problem without knowing every nut and bolt. Reading weights alone, it even worked out which arcade game, such as Pong or Pac-Man, each small network played.

Why is that a big deal? Older tools mostly looked inside, like saliency maps that show which parts of an image swayed a prediction. Astra goes into the weights and fixes them, and nobody told it how to edit.

It can build, too. A separate team led by Chandan Singh of Voyage AI used Anthropic's Claude to write small transformers from scratch that solved basic math problems, reaching nearly perfect accuracy within three attempts.

Here's the catch. This is early. The MIT team released full logs of every experiment, and scientists at The Alan Turing Institute replicated and extended the results, calling it "a promising but early tool" for interpretability.

One last detail. The math fix did not give Qwen any new mathematical skill. It only stopped the model from reasoning in circles.

Learn · Physics from first principles

Open problems: where physics admits it does not know

Lesson 15 of 15. Every list of unsolved questions marks the edge of what we know, and sometimes the next big idea.

Ever kept asking "why?" until the person answering ran out of answers? Physics does this to itself on purpose. The spot where the answers stop has a name: an open problem.

The habit is old. In 1704, in the first edition of his book Opticks, Isaac Newton published a list of 31 queries about light and matter. A science book that ends by handing you questions. That is the idea of this whole lesson: knowing exactly where your knowledge stops is part of knowing.

Open problems come in two flavours. Some are theoretical, meaning our best theories cannot explain something we have observed. Others are experimental, meaning nobody has yet worked out how to build the test that would check an idea or look closer at a phenomenon.

Here is one worked example, with numbers. In 1900, Lord Kelvin gave a speech naming "two clouds" over physics. Cloud one: no evidence that the luminiferous aether existed. Cloud two: the equipartition theorem failed to predict the heat capacity of solids.

Here's the catch. Two problems that sounded small were both solved within a decade, and the fixes were special relativity and quantum mechanics. Two clouds, about ten years, two new theories. Small gaps can hide very large rooms.

Do the lists ever empty out? Slowly. Roger Penrose listed problems about general relativity in 1982, and about 14 were still unsolved as of 2017. Barry Simon listed 15 problems in 1984, and about 5 were solved by 2000, roughly one in three. A list of 10 from the Strings 2000 conference had none conclusively solved as of 2025.

Today's biggest cloud is a clash. The mathematics of the Standard Model is inconsistent with general relativity, and both theories break down in extreme places, like the Big Bang or the centres of black holes. Add the nature of dark matter and dark energy, the mass of neutrinos, and why there is more matter than antimatter. Vitaly Ginzburg named three great problems: the arrow of time, how to interpret quantum mechanics, and life arising from physics alone.

Kelvin's clouds looked like footnotes and turned out to be doors. So which of today's questions is a door? There is no lesson 16 to tell you.

Key facts

  • In 1900 Lord Kelvin named two problems, the aether and heat capacity of solids; both were solved within a decade by special relativity and quantum mechanics.
  • The Standard Model is mathematically inconsistent with general relativity, and both break down at singularities like the Big Bang and inside black holes.
  • Newton's Opticks (1704) listed 31 queries about light and matter.
  • Of Barry Simon's 15 problems from 1984, about 5 were solved by 2000.

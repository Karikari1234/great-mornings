Bangladesh sets garment minimum wage at Tk17,000 after a 12-hour meeting and a 4-2 vote

Bangladesh's wage board raised the garment minimum by 36% to Tk17,000 by majority vote, Tk1,000 below what workers' representatives finally asked for.

Twelve hours of talks, and the two sides still ended up Tk1,000 apart. Workers' representatives wanted a minimum of Tk18,000 a month. Factory owners had agreed to Tk17,000 and would not go higher.

So the board did what any stuck committee does. It voted.

The meeting at the Minimum Wage Board office in Segunbagicha began at 2pm on Tuesday and ended around 2am on Wednesday. Babul Akhter, the workers' representative on the six-member board, said four members backed Tk17,000 and two backed the workers' Tk18,000.

A minimum wage board is the body that sets the lowest legal monthly pay for an industry. Here, that means the ready-made garment sector.

Here's what changed. The floor for fourth-grade workers is now Tk17,000, up Tk4,500 from Tk12,500, which is the 36% rise. Third-grade workers get at least Tk19,200, second-grade Tk20,000 and first-grade Tk21,500.

Now look at how far the workers' side travelled. It opened by proposing Tk32,000, dropped to Tk22,000, then to Tk18,000. The final number sits Tk15,000 under that first ask.

So why now? The decision follows weeks of labour unrest across major industrial belts, with workers demanding more pay as living costs rise. The old Tk12,500 minimum was announced in November 2023 and took effect that December.

Watch January. The new structure is expected to take effect from December 1, and workers are due to receive their revised salaries in January, in line with an earlier agreement between the two sides.

Key facts

  • The minimum wage for fourth-grade garment workers rises 36% from Tk12,500 to Tk17,000.
  • The six-member board voted 4-2 for Tk17,000 over the workers' Tk18,000 after a 12-hour meeting.
  • Workers' representatives began by proposing Tk32,000, then Tk22,000, then Tk18,000.
  • The new wage is expected to take effect from December 1, with revised pay arriving in January.

India says Hasina's plan to return is separate from extradition

India is examining Bangladesh's request to hand over Sheikh Hasina, but says her own plan to fly home on December 16 is a separate matter.

A former prime minister under a death sentence says she wants to fly back to the country that sentenced her. The open question is who gets to decide how she goes.

A Reuters report on Tuesday quoted Syed Mozammel Ali, who calls himself Sheikh Hasina's spokesperson. He said she wants to return on December 16, Victory Day. She has asked the government to arrange a Biman aircraft to bring her home.

Hasina has spent more than two years in exile in India since she was ousted in August 2024. In November 2025 the International Crimes Tribunal sentenced her to death in absentia for crimes against humanity over the crackdown on the 2024 protests. She has rejected the charges.

Here's the puzzle. Bangladesh has also formally asked India to hand her over. If she can simply board a plane, what happens to that request?

India answered on Wednesday. At the foreign ministry's weekly briefing, spokesperson Randhir Jaiswal treated them as two separate questions.

"The extradition request is being examined according to our established procedure," he said. "However, the matter of any citizen who wishes to return to their country voluntarily, including with travel documents, is separate from the extradition request."

So what's the difference? Extradition is one government formally handing a person to another under a legal process. The 2013 treaty between India and Bangladesh has exceptions that can let the country being asked say no. A voluntary return is a person deciding to go home on their own.

On the December 16 plan itself, Jaiswal kept it short: "This is a matter between the former prime minister and the Bangladesh government. We have nothing more to say about it."

He did add one thing. India is "concerned about incidents of violence against minorities in Bangladesh," he said, and urged the government to investigate them thoroughly and bring the people responsible to justice.

That leaves plenty unanswered. Nobody has said how long India's review will take, or what it will decide. It's also unclear whether Dhaka will send the plane she asked for.

And Dhaka may not see the two questions as separate at all. A headline from Anadolu Agency reports a Bangladeshi minister saying her return depends on the extradition treaty with India. If that holds, India's two separate questions may turn out to be one.

Key facts

  • India's foreign ministry says Bangladesh's extradition request for Hasina is being examined under established procedure.
  • Spokesperson Randhir Jaiswal said a voluntary return is separate from the extradition request.
  • Hasina's self-described spokesperson says she wants to return on December 16 and has asked for a Biman aircraft.
  • Hasina was sentenced to death for crimes against humanity and has been in exile in India for more than two years.

North Korea gives Swiss diplomat 48 hours to leave, weeks after office reopened

Switzerland reopened its Pyongyang office after six years and sanctioned a children's camp, and North Korea ordered its envoy out.

Switzerland's office in Pyongyang sat shut for six years. It reopened, and weeks later its head was told to be gone within 48 hours.

Here's how that happened. Swiss foreign minister Ignazio Cassis made a rare visit to North Korea, officially reopened the office and discussed normalising relations. He was greeted at Pyongyang Airport on 29 October.

Now the sanctions. Switzerland targeted the Songdowon international children's camp in the eastern city of Wonsan, which is attended by Russian and Chinese children among other nationalities. The camp has been accused of involvement in transferring children from Russian-occupied areas of Ukraine.

North Korea's state news agency, KCNA, called the sanctions "a blatant hostile act". It declared the head of the Swiss office persona non grata, which is the formal way for a host country to say a diplomat is no longer welcome. It also called for the office to close.

So did Switzerland think it could do both? Yes, and it says so. Its foreign affairs department "distinguishes between its sanctions policy and its commitment to maintaining dialogue with all countries".

It added that reopening the office did not affect its enforcement of UN sanctions over North Korea's nuclear programme, or EU sanctions tied to Russia's war against Ukraine.

Here's the catch. Switzerland is one of the few Western nations that keeps talking with Pyongyang, and it treats talking and sanctioning as two separate tracks. North Korea answered a move on one track by closing the other.

The line is not fully cut. Swiss authorities said relations will run through North Korea's embassy in Bern and the Swiss embassy in Beijing.

The backdrop is the war. Thousands of North Korean soldiers have been deployed to support Russia, and in August Ukraine's President Volodymyr Zelensky accused Moscow of using North Korean missiles.

Diplomatic links date to 1974, and the Pyongyang office opened in 1997. After six years shut, the door stood open for a few weeks. For now, the talking runs through two other buildings.

Key facts

  • North Korea ordered Switzerland's diplomatic representative to leave within 48 hours and called for the office to close.
  • The row followed Swiss sanctions on the Songdowon children's camp in Wonsan, accused of involvement in transferring children from Russian-occupied Ukraine.
  • Foreign minister Ignazio Cassis reopened the Pyongyang office weeks earlier.
  • Relations will continue through North Korea's embassy in Bern and Switzerland's embassy in Beijing.

Opec+ heavyweights head for a fight over how much oil they could pump

Big Opec+ members are clashing over production capacity estimates, and Kazakhstan wants a higher number too.

Money is at stake in Opec+, and the fight is over a number that sounds dull: how much oil each country could pump if it really tried.

Quick background. Opec+ is the oil producers' club of OPEC plus Russia and several other countries, including Kazakhstan. Members agree on how much oil each one pumps, and Saudi Arabia and Russia lead the group in practice.

So why does a guess about capacity matter? A country's quota, its allowed output, is tied to estimates like these. Raise the estimate and you can make the case for a bigger quota, which means more barrels to sell.

Think of a shared pizza where each slice is cut to match how hungry each person says they are. Everyone has a reason to say they're starving.

That's why the group's largest members are heading for a clash over production estimates, the Financial Times reports.

Here's the catch. A cartel's quotas exist to hold output back, so a fight over who gets to claim a bigger number pulls the other way.

Kazakhstan has joined in. Sources told Reuters it wants a higher estimate of its own output capacity ahead of a debate over 2027 quotas. Kazakhstan has long pumped more oil than its quota allows, so a higher official number would close some of that gap on paper.

A note on what isn't known yet: the reports available here don't say which large members are arguing or what figures they want.

Watch the 2027 quota debate. If the members settle on capacity numbers, the 2027 quotas will be built on them. If they can't agree, the group will head into that debate still fighting over who deserves to pump more.

Key facts

  • The FT reports that Opec+'s largest members are heading for a clash over oil production estimates.
  • Kazakhstan is also seeking a higher output capacity estimate ahead of a 2027 OPEC+ quota debate, sources told Reuters.

An AI fixed another AI by editing 0.016 percent of its numbers

MIT researchers had OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra read and edit another model's raw weights, and it repaired faults without retraining.

Astra changed 0.016 percent of a small AI model's weights, and the model's score on a math test jumped from 7.9 percent to 48.1 percent. That is roughly one weight in every 6,250.

Weights are the numbers inside a neural network that set how strongly its units connect. Normally a model gets its weights through training: it makes a mistake, its weights shift, and this repeats on a vast scale. It is a costly, energy-hungry slog, and the result is a pile of numbers nobody can read.

Here's the twist. Researchers at MIT, in a preprint, handed those raw numbers to GPT-6 Astra, a large language model from OpenAI, and asked it to figure them out. No new training of the target model was needed.

So what was wrong with the math model? It was called Qwen, and on tough problems it got stuck in repetitive loops. The fix gave it no new math skill. It just stopped it from reasoning in circles.

The car test is even more fun. A network steering a virtual car had to back up before jumping a ramp, and it failed 256 attempts. After Astra edited about 3.5 percent of its weights, it nailed the move in all 1,024 trials that followed.

Astra could also read the weights of small networks and tell which arcade game each had learned, such as Pong or Pac-Man. And when asked to write code for Atari games and Go, it produced working models.

Think of a seasoned mechanic with an unfamiliar engine. They can find the fault without knowing what every nut and bolt does. Astra is the same: it cannot pinpoint the role of every part in a model.

Is it hype? The MIT team released complete logs of every experiment. Scientists at The Alan Turing Institute replicated and extended the results, and called it "a promising but early tool" for interpretability.

Separately, Chandan Singh of Voyage AI led a team that used Anthropic's Claude to write small transformers from scratch. They solved basic math problems with nearly perfect accuracy within three attempts.

The big question is how far this goes. If a model can fix what it only partly understands, how much of an AI's inner workings do we still need to understand?

Learn · Physics from first principles

Open problems: how physics keeps a list of what it cannot explain

The last lesson is about the questions physics has not answered, and how scientists keep track of them.

Every to-do list you have ever written had something stuck at the bottom. The easy jobs get done and the odd, awkward one stays. Physics works the same way, and this last lesson is about its awkward jobs.

Physicists call them open problems. Some are theoretical: our best theories cannot explain something we have actually observed. Others are experimental: we have an idea, but nobody has worked out how to build a test for it.

Here is the big one. The Standard Model, our mathematical framework for the particles and forces we know, does not fit with general relativity, the theory of gravity. Where both are needed at once, such as at the Big Bang or inside a black hole, both theories break down. Beyond the Standard Model, the list also includes the absolute mass of neutrinos, why matter outweighs antimatter, and what dark matter and dark energy are.

Keeping such lists is an old habit. Isaac Newton ended his book Opticks, first published in 1704, with 31 open questions about light and matter. In 1900 Lord Kelvin gave a speech naming just two problems, which he called "two clouds" over classical physics: no evidence for the luminiferous aether, and a failure to predict how solids hold heat. Here's the twist. Both clouds were cleared within a decade, by special relativity and quantum mechanics. Two small problems, two whole new theories.

So how fast do lists shrink? Try the numbers. In 1984 the mathematical physicist Barry Simon listed 15 open problems about quantum operators. By 2000 about 5 were solved, which is one in three after sixteen years. He then released a second list of 15, and at least 3 of those were solved.

Some lists barely move. In 1982 Roger Penrose posed problems about general relativity, and about 14 were still unsolved in 2017. Vitaly Ginzburg compiled 30 problems between 1971 and 2005, plus three great ones: the arrow of time, how to interpret quantum mechanics, and whether life can arise from physics alone. And a list of 10 problems from the Strings 2000 conference had none conclusively solved as of 2025. So which item on today's list is a Kelvin cloud, small to look at but big enough to rewrite physics once someone pulls on it? That is the question you leave this course with.

Key facts

  • In 1900 Lord Kelvin named two "clouds" over physics; both were solved within a decade by special relativity and quantum mechanics.
  • Barry Simon's 1984 list had 15 problems; about 5 were solved by 2000.
  • The Standard Model is inconsistent with general relativity, and both break down at singularities like the Big Bang.
  • The 10 problems proposed at Strings 2000 had none conclusively solved as of 2025.

A decision is only part of the story

The news captures moments. The forces that give those moments meaning move more slowly.

A court can close a case while the fight behind it keeps going. That is the odd rhythm of this page: decisions appear as clean moments, while the world that made them stays messy. Puigdemont's case closed while the independence dispute continues. So what does a headline really tell you when it says something has been settled?

One pattern is the distance between authority and agreement. Someone gets to put a number on a page, draw a line, or declare a result. The result has force. It does not make the people living with it agree. Bangladesh's garment wage rose by majority vote, below the workers' final ask. That gap matters because a decision also shows who had the power to make it.

Another pattern is the distance between a rule and what happens after it is written. Nvidia's AI chips still reach China despite the rules. A rule's meaning is tested by the routes things take and by the people trying to enforce it. The news catches that test in motion, usually before anyone can say how it ends.

Then there is the question of what deserves belief. Two proofs of the Riemann hypothesis, one written by AI, still need checking. A claim can be exciting and unfinished at once. Headlines are good at showing the moment a thing becomes visible: a ruling, a vote, a discovery. The slow work underneath is harder to fit on a page: bargaining, enforcement, verification. When any headline says something happened, what process will decide what that event actually changes?

Jamaat chief says Awami League has lost moral right to politics

Speaking to families of 2006 clash victims, Shafiqur Rahman rejected an Awami League return and called for trials and constitutional reform.

Can a political party lose the moral right to take part in politics? Shafiqur Rahman says the Awami League has. The opposition leader and Jamaat-e-Islami chief says it has lost all moral and democratic rights to practise politics in Bangladesh.

He called the Awami League a "club of fascism" rather than a political party. His test for politics was public welfare, nation-building, social justice and an end to discrimination. He said the party met none of those tests.

Why make that argument at this meeting? Jamaat had brought together family members of victims of the October 28, 2006 "logi-boitha" clashes at Paltan. Rahman was speaking to them when he made his case against the Awami League.

He alleged that the party withdrew murder cases from those clashes after coming to power. He also accused it of later having top Jamaat leaders executed through cases he called false. Those are accusations Rahman made in his speech.

His account reached beyond the clashes. Rahman accused the Awami League of relying on state-sponsored terror, bloodshed and unlawful killings during the 1972-75 period and the last 15 and a half years. The source gives these claims as his allegations.

So what does he want to happen now? Rahman demanded exemplary, fair trials for everyone responsible for political killings. He said Jamaat would not compromise over the blood of those he called martyrs and that the trials should reach their end.

He also warned people he said were trying to rehabilitate the Awami League. He compared helping it return to playing with a venomous snake. The warning was forceful, though the account does not name the people he was addressing.

Rahman called on the current administration to pursue constitutional reforms, saying they should prevent what he calls fascism from returning. He framed that demand as a response to a public mandate for fundamental political change.

His speech leaves one practical question unanswered: what would those reforms do to the Awami League's path back into politics? For now, his stated demand is for trials, while his claim about the party's right to return remains his own.

Britain took White Plains. Washington still won the war.

A British victory during Washington's retreat shows why one lost village could not settle the fight for independence.

The British captured White Plains while George Washington's army retreated northward from New York City. Yet that army eventually defeated Britain. How does a side lose a village and still win a war?

To see the puzzle, you need the fight that came before it. Britain and its thirteen colonies had been in conflict over laws and control. Fighting began in 1775, and colonial forces were brought together as the Continental Army, with Washington in command.

In 1776, the colonies declared independence. Washington's army drove British forces out of Boston that March. But the British launched a campaign around New York and New Jersey, and Washington's troops were retreating when White Plains fell. Independence had been declared; winning it was another matter.

Here's the turn. The capture of White Plains was a British gain, but the war continued. Washington later crossed the Delaware River and won victories at Trenton and Princeton. Neither the retreat nor those victories settled the conflict on its own.

Then another British army surrendered at Saratoga in 1777. That victory helped convince France and Spain that an independent United States was possible. France made a commercial agreement with the revolutionaries and signed an alliance in 1778.

Why would that matter to a fight that began in the colonies? The war reached beyond North America, into the Caribbean and the Atlantic. By 1779, Spain had allied with France against Britain, though Spain did not formally ally with the Americans.

The decisive scene came at Yorktown in 1781. A Franco-American army besieged British General Cornwallis, while the French navy cut off his escape. Cornwallis surrendered. Britain then negotiated an end to the war, although its wars with France and Spain continued.

In 1783, Britain acknowledged the independence of the thirteen colonies in the Treaty of Paris. White Plains was still a British capture. It just was not the end of the story that began with Washington's retreat.

Key facts

  • The British captured White Plains in 1776 as Washington's army retreated northward from New York City.
  • The Continental Army later won at Trenton and Princeton.
  • The British surrender at Saratoga helped convince France and Spain that American independence was possible.
  • Britain acknowledged American independence in the 1783 Treaty of Paris.

What makes a problem open

An unsolved question needs a clear answer to count as an open problem.

You ask for the nearest shop. Nearest to you, or to the person answering? The question sounds simple, but you need to pin down what it means before anyone can answer it.

Science has questions like that. An open problem is a question we can state accurately and expect to have an answer that can be checked, but no solution is known. That gives us a puzzle: what happens if the question itself is unclear?

Sometimes that is how a supposed open problem gets closed. Researchers show that it was never well defined. They have resolved the puzzle about the question, even though they have not produced the answer it seemed to ask for.

For a clearer example, take protein structure prediction: given a protein's sequence, how can you predict its structure? Suppose someone offers four possible structures for one sequence. A check verifies one and rejects the other three. That settles this case, but it does not yet give you a way to predict the structure from another sequence.

An open problem can close with a solution, too. Fermat's Last Theorem, the four-color theorem and the Poincaré conjecture are named examples that researchers solved.

So the lesson ends where a new investigation begins: when someone claims to have solved an open problem, how do we check that the answer really closes it?

Key facts

  • An open problem can be accurately stated and is assumed to have a verifiable solution.
  • Some supposed open problems were closed by showing they were not well defined.
  • Protein structure prediction asks how to predict structure from sequence.
  • Fermat's Last Theorem, the four-color theorem and the Poincaré conjecture were solved.

The Day, Connected

What the headlines cannot show all at once

Today's events bring slower questions about power, trust and the systems people depend on.

A headline tells you what moved. It rarely tells you what had been straining before the movement became visible. That gap matters today. Again and again, the event is quick to name, while the story underneath sits in who gets to decide, what people are asked to trust, and what a system can quietly absorb.

Start with who gets a seat. Merz says Ukraine and Europe must have a say in ending the war; Bahamian advocates seek an apology from Charles. These are different histories, but they lead to a shared question: can people accept an outcome when someone else sets the terms? A decision can arrive in a day. Its authority takes longer to build.

Then there is trust in what you can check. OpenAI announced a physics finding while holding back the work. You can see the puzzle: how does a claim earn confidence when the route to it is out of view? An announcement travels quickly. Checking it, challenging it and correcting it take time.

And underneath the arguments, ordinary systems have limits. A grid operator is probing a failure after generation dropped by over a third. The drop made news; the daily work of keeping a grid dependable is harder to see. That is how news can change your sense of time: the sharp moment gets the headline, while the pressures beneath it keep working. When you read the next one, ask: what had to be true for this event to happen?

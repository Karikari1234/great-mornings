Cover story

Masked settlers attacked European diplomats, and Israel's own army condemned it

Diplomats from Belgium, France, Poland and the Catholic Church were attacked in a West Bank village, and Israel's army and the prime minister's office reacted very differently.

The Israeli army and the Israeli prime minister's office were handed the same incident and gave two very different answers.

Here is what happened. A group of European diplomats, from Belgium, France, Poland and the Catholic Church, went to Taybeh, a largely Christian village near Ramallah in the occupied West Bank. They wanted to assess the situation after almost daily attacks by Israeli settlers on Palestinians trying to bring in their olive harvest.

Video from the scene showed masked settlers confronting their convoy. Reports suggest stones were thrown. Belgium says a window of one of its consulate's cars was smashed.

A French mayor, Bernard Baudoux, was also hurt. He told AFP the settlers kicked him and beat him with iron bars. "It's the gratuitous nature of the violence that stuns me," he said.

So who said what? The Israel Defense Forces, the army, strongly condemned "the severe act of violence" and said it is investigating. Its early inquiry found no soldiers were at the scene. Foreign minister Gideon Sa'ar also condemned what he called "Israeli rioters".

Here's the catch. Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's office urged "caution before drawing conclusions" and said social media images do not show the full sequence of events. It also pointed to concerns about a "coordinated campaign to smear Israel" during the olive harvest. It added that police have opened an investigation.

Sa'ar added a point of his own: the visit was not coordinated with the army in advance, which he said could have helped keep the visitors safe.

Belgium's foreign minister, Maxime Prevot, said Israel, as the occupying power, must protect Palestinians and diplomats and bring those responsible to justice. He said Belgium will keep pressing for EU sanctions.

The day before, Palestinians and Heidi Levine, a Washington Post photographer, were hurt in a separate settler attack in another village. US Secretary of State Marco Rubio called it "disturbing". US Ambassador Mike Huckabee, a staunch Israel ally, called it a "despicable act of terror".

So the open question is simple. If police and the army are both investigating, will anyone be named and held to account?

  • French mayor Bernard Baudoux said settlers beat him with iron bars.
  • A day earlier, Washington Post photographer Heidi Levine and Palestinians were injured in a separate settler attack.

Why young Indians are marching over the voter rolls

A disputed purge of India's voter lists, on top of youth anger over jobs, has protests heading for a decade high.

More than 130 million names. That's how many have been removed from India's voter lists since last year, according to the AP. India has roughly 1 billion voters, so that's about one name in every eight.

If your name isn't on the roll, you can't vote. That's why this number now sits at the centre of a protest wave. A conflict-tracking group, the Armed Conflict Location and Event Data Project, projects that India's street protests in 2026 will likely beat every year of the past decade.

Here's the puzzle. Prime Minister Narendra Modi's government is stable, and the next general election isn't due until 2029. So why are so many young people on the streets now?

Start with the list. The Election Commission, which runs India's elections, says it is clearing out duplicate and ineligible entries. Activists and opposition parties say the revision favours Modi's Bharatiya Janata Party and helped it win recent state elections. The BJP, the government and the commission deny it.

Protesters want the chief election commissioner, Gyanesh Kumar, to resign. On October 10, police detained more than 2,000 people in Delhi during a crackdown on a planned demonstration against him. Six days later, thousands rallied with Indian flags in New Delhi's Connaught Place.

Then there's the anger underneath. Government data put unemployment among Indians aged 15 to 29 at 9.9% in 2025. Research cited by Reuters put it at nearly 40% for graduates aged 15 to 25, close to two in every five. Leaked exam papers, crimes against women and allegations about billionaires close to Modi add fuel.

That frustration found an odd vehicle. The Cockroach Janta Party began as online satire after India's chief justice compared unemployed young people to parasites, and grew into a nationwide campaign in June. Police detained its founder, Abhijeet Dipke, ahead of the Connaught Place rally.

Police deny beating demonstrators and say protesters were handled according to procedure. Ananya Raturi, a 21-year-old Delhi University student, told Reuters: "The fear in us is gone."

People under 30 make up nearly a quarter of India's voters. Watch Uttar Pradesh, India's largest state, which votes in early 2027. An analyst told Reuters it's too early to know whether the protests will produce lasting electoral gains for opposition parties.

Key facts

  • More than 130 million names have been removed from India's voter lists since last year (AP).
  • ACLED projects India's 2026 street protests will likely exceed any year in the past decade.
  • Police detained more than 2,000 protesters in Delhi on October 10.
  • Unemployment among graduates aged 15 to 25 is nearly 40%, per research cited by Reuters.

Anthropic wants a law that lets Washington stop dangerous AI, with a judge's approval

Anthropic is circulating a draft bill that would let the US government block a risky AI model, but only if a judge agrees.

$1 billion. More than 50 people. Those two numbers are the tripwires in a draft AI bill that Anthropic confirmed on Friday it is circulating among US lawmakers.

Anthropic is the company behind the AI assistant Claude. So an AI company is handing Congress a draft rule for AI companies. Wait, what?

Here's how it would work. Washington could block a model's release or use if there is an imminent risk of killing or seriously injuring more than 50 people, or causing more than $1 billion in damages. A billion is a thousand million.

The catch: the government needs a judge's approval first. It cannot pull the plug alone.

The draft asks for more than a kill switch. A company spokesperson said it requires frontier developers, the firms building the most powerful models, to test them, tell the public what they find, submit to independent evaluation, and keep a security program.

So why now? The US has no federal AI law. Policy under President Donald Trump has leaned on voluntary industry pledges and executive orders meant to keep an AI boom going. Congress is politically divided and heavily lobbied by Big Tech.

Meanwhile, security incidents involving AI agents, software that acts without constant human supervision, have escaped their developers' control. Anthropic has its own history with Washington. The Trump administration banned it from the Pentagon in February, and in June Washington forced it to suspend its most advanced models for nearly three weeks.

Last month CEO Dario Amodei called on the industry to slow down. He feared a group of AI agents could take control of "the entire internet" within six to 12 months. Trump has dismissed such calls as "negative forces."

There's a twist. The bill would override certain state laws, such as California's. Yet in June Anthropic wrote that "absent a strong federal law, states should retain full authority to legislate."

Charlie Bullock of the Institute for Law & AI called the proposal "pretty reasonable" and "more than other frontier labs have done." He also called it "weaker" than the FRONTIER Act, a rival bill introduced in the House this summer.

Don't expect a vote soon. Congress is out of session until after next month's midterm elections, so the first thing to watch is whether this draft, or the FRONTIER Act, gets picked up when lawmakers return.

Key facts

  • Anthropic confirmed on Friday it is circulating a draft AI bill among US lawmakers.
  • The government could block a model only with a judge's approval, if it risks killing or seriously injuring more than 50 people or causing over $1 billion in damages.
  • Congress has passed no federal AI law and is out of session until after next month's midterms.
  • The bill would override certain state laws, such as California's.

Fourteen cats trained for space, and one flew

In 1963 France launched a cat named C 341 into space, brought her back after 13 minutes, and only then gave her a name.

In 1963, French scientists bought 14 cats from a pet dealer. Only one of them went to space.

She was a tuxedo cat who weighed 2.5 kilograms, and she did not have a name yet. Her label was C 341. The scientists kept all the cats unnamed on purpose, to lower the chance of getting attached to them.

So why cats? France had already launched rats, but the scientists wanted larger mammals. Cats were the pick because they already had a lot of neurological data behind them.

All 14 were female, chosen for their calmer demeanor. They trained for about two months, including a high-G centrifuge and a three-axis chair played with simulated rocket noise. Each had electrodes surgically implanted in the skull, thin wires that let scientists read brain activity.

Launch day was 18 October 1963, at 8:09 am, from a site in Algeria. C 341 was picked that day from six finalists because of her calm manner and her weight. A Véronique rocket carried her up.

Here's the catch with space travel: the first part is brutal. The engine burned for 42 seconds, and she felt 9.5 g. That means a pull about nine and a half times the gravity you feel standing in your kitchen.

Then the nose cone separated, before reaching 152 kilometres, and she floated weightless for five minutes. During the flight, electrical impulses were applied to her brain and a leg to see how she responded. The parachutes opened 8 minutes and 55 seconds in, and a helicopter reached the capsule 13 minutes after ignition.

She survived, and most of the data was of good quality. But two months later she was euthanised so scientists could examine her brain.

The name came afterwards. The media called her Félix, after Felix the Cat, and the research centre CERMA made it feminine: Félicette.

Look at what happened next. The scientists had avoided naming her so nobody would get attached. Now stamps around the world carry her, and a statue of her stands at the International Space University.

Key facts

  • Félicette, then called C 341, was launched on 18 October 1963 from Algeria on a Véronique rocket.
  • She was one of 14 female cats trained, and the flight lasted 13 minutes with five minutes of weightlessness.

James Baldwin put it neatly. You think your pain and your heartbreak are like nothing else in the history of the world, but then you read.

Here's the catch. Hesse says that is where the books stop. In his poem they build a secret path toward your heart, and that is all they do.

The poem goes further. What you need is in you: the sun, the stars, the moon. The illumination you were seeking shines up from within you.

The young Proust reached the same place. He wrote that the end of a book's wisdom appears to us as merely the start of our own. The one true book, he said, already exists in every one of us.

The Marginalian calls this a paradox. A book can ease your loneliness, but no borrowed wisdom substitutes for life itself. Your own story only gets written on the blank page of living.

So read the book. Let it comfort you. Then notice what it woke up in you, because that part was yours the whole time.

Learn · Physics from first principles

Open problems: where physics still doesn't have the answer

Lesson 15 of 15. In 1900 Lord Kelvin saw two clouds over physics. Both turned into whole new theories.

Two clouds. That's how Lord Kelvin described the trouble with physics in a speech in 1900. The physics of his day explained so much that he could point to only two problems.

Start with something ordinary. You drop a ball and it falls, and a theory tells you how. A theory is just a story that predicts what you will see. An open problem is a spot where the story and the world disagree, or where nobody can yet build the test that would settle it.

Those spots come in two kinds. Some are theoretical: our existing theories cannot explain something we have observed. Others are experimental: a proposed theory exists, but designing an experiment to test it, or to look closer at a phenomenon, is the hard part.

Here's the worked example, and it's Kelvin's own. Cloud one: no evidence could be found for the luminiferous aether, a supposed invisible substance that light was thought to travel through. Cloud two: the equipartition theorem failed to predict the heat capacity of solids, meaning how much heat a solid soaks up. Two problems. Both were solved within a decade, the first by special relativity and the second by quantum mechanics.

Wait, what? Two small clouds turned into two whole new branches of physics. That's the clever bit of the lesson: an open problem is where the next theory tends to come from.

So what's on the list now? The mathematics of the Standard Model, our best theory of particles, is inconsistent with general relativity, our best theory of gravity. Each breaks down in extreme places, like the Big Bang or the centers of black holes. Beyond that sit the strong CP problem, the absolute mass of neutrinos, matter-antimatter asymmetry, and the nature of dark matter and dark energy.

People keep score. Newton published 31 queries about light and matter in 1704. In 1984 Barry Simon listed 15 problems on quantum operators, and about 5 were solved by 2000, which leaves about 10. The 10 millennium problems proposed at the Strings 2000 conference? None conclusively solved as of 2025. Vitaly Ginzburg's list added three great problems: the arrow of time, the interpretations of quantum mechanics, and life arising from physics alone.

That's lesson 15 of 15, so there is no next lesson here. The question is yours to carry: which cloud on today's list will be the next one to grow into a new theory? The sources suggest going deeper with Welch Labs, 3Blue1Brown, PBS Space Time and Veritasium.

  • The Standard Model is mathematically inconsistent with general relativity, and both break down at the Big Bang and inside black holes.
  • Barry Simon's 1984 list had 15 problems, with about 5 solved by 2000.

The rules are written by whoever shows up

Three quiet patterns under a loud day of news.

Most days, the news is a pile of things that happened. Look at it long enough and you see a few slow things that keep happening.

The first is who gets believed. After masked settlers attacked diplomats, Israel's army and the prime minister's office reacted very differently. In Pakistan, two governments give two reasons for one closed border. When the same event has two official stories, trust is the thing being fought over, and the fight usually outlasts the event.

The second is what we build without checking it holds. A Cox's Bazar resort had no fire safety licence. A sanctions law was two days from taking effect and got paused. An insurer's cover stops at a limit, and AI claims may not. Fragility rarely announces itself. It sits there quietly until a day it is tested.

The third is who sets the rules. Anthropic is drafting a law that would let a judge sign off on blocking a risky AI model. Britain's regulator wants rivals on Apple's tap-to-pay chip. Websites are shutting out AI agents. Nobody voted on most of this. The rules are being written right now, by whoever is in the room.

Notice the shape of it. The news shows you the fire, the stampede, the walkout. It almost never shows you the missing licence or the crowd plan that came first. Events are the loud end of slow processes.

So here is a question you can carry to any headline: what was already true, long before this happened, that made it possible?

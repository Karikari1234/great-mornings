Europe says it will show up at Ukraine talks Russia said it could not join

France says European officials will attend the US-mediated Russia-Ukraine talks in Abu Dhabi this week, after the Kremlin said Europe had no role.

Russia said Europe had no place at the table. France has now said Europe will be there anyway.

French foreign minister Jean-Noël Barrot told French radio on Sunday: "France will be there and will ensure that Europe's voice is heard." The talks are set for Tuesday and Wednesday in Abu Dhabi, in the United Arab Emirates.

Here's the catch. Barrot did not say who would represent Paris, or whether European officials would join the negotiations or just observe.

Why does the seat matter? The Kremlin spokesman, Dmitry Peskov, had said the three-way format of Washington, Moscow and Kyiv was already agreed. He also accused European governments of failing to decide who speaks for them, though he admitted security talks would at some point need Europe.

Now add a worry. On Thursday, President Trump met Kremlin envoy Kirill Dmitriev and said a coming agreement was "going to involve more than Ukraine." He added it "might involve all of Europe."

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy told CNN he had not been given details. "For me, it's not simple, and it can be dangerous," he said. He pointed to the 1945 Yalta conference, where Allied leaders settled Europe's division without several of the affected countries in the room.

So that is the fear in one line: a deal about Europe, made without Europe.

This will be the fourth round of talks between the two countries brokered by the Trump administration. It is the first since Geneva in February. Earlier rounds made little progress, and the source says a breakthrough got less likely as the administration focused on its war with Iran.

Russia's team looks more senior this time. Alongside Vladimir Medinsky, who has joined most talks since March 2022, come two deputy foreign ministers and military officials. That could mean Moscow is readier for hard questions, but the Institute for the Study of War suggests some of the same officials have tried to slow talks before.

The gaps are still wide. Russia wants Ukraine to give up large parts of its territory and renounce NATO membership. Zelenskyy wants security commitments spelled out, including who would act, and how, if Russia attacked again.

Key facts

  • France's foreign minister said European officials will attend the Abu Dhabi talks on Tuesday and Wednesday.
  • The Kremlin had said the three-way format was already agreed and Europe had no role.
  • It is the fourth Trump-brokered round and the first since Geneva in February.
  • Zelenskyy compared the risk to the 1945 Yalta conference.

A Narayanganj group has held a monthly vigil for Taqi since 2013

Tanwir Muhammad Taqi was killed in 2013, and his father says the trial has still not begun, so a Narayanganj group keeps lighting candles on the eighth of each month.

A confessional statement was given in court in 2013, and the trial has still not started. That is the strange part of the Taqi murder case, and it is why a candle was lit again on 8 November.

Here is the case in short. On 6 March 2013, Tanvir Muhammad Taqi left his home on Shaista Khan Road in Narayanganj and went missing. Police recovered his body from the Kumudini canal on 8 March.

Later that year, on 12 November, a man named Sultan Shaukat Bhramar went before a court. He is described as an associate of Azmeri Osman. Under Section 164 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, he gave a confessional statement saying Taqi had been abducted and killed under Osman's leadership.

So why does the vigil still happen? Because the court process has not moved the way the family wants.

Narayanganj Cultural Alliance has marked the eighth of every month since the murder, demanding that the trial begin and that identified suspects be arrested. The latest one, at Narayanganj Central Shaheed Minar, marked 164 months. That is 13 years and 8 months.

Taqi's father, Rafiur Rabbi, asked why the trial has not begun and why the charge sheet has not been submitted. He alleged that Sheikh Hasina kept the case stalled for 11 and a half years to protect Osman's family. Osman is the nephew of former Awami League lawmaker Shamim Osman.

Here's the catch. Rabbi said the judicial system has not changed despite the change of government. He alleged that some members of the current ruling party are protecting members of the Osman family and their businesses, and he asked whether they are obstructing justice for money. Those are his claims, made at the vigil.

He called for the judiciary to be separated from the executive. "We want an independent judicial system," he said.

Abdullah Al Amin, lawmaker for Narayanganj-4, spoke too. "When perpetrators are not brought to justice, they become the rulers of the state," he said, adding that 13 years without justice for Taqi was not normal.

A charge sheet is the formal document in which investigators set out the case against the accused. Without one, a trial generally cannot start. The father has asked why it has not been submitted, and the vigil will come around again on 8 December.

Key facts

  • Taqi went missing on 6 March 2013 and his body was recovered from the Kumudini canal on 8 March.
  • A confessional statement was given in court on 12 November 2013 naming Osman's leadership.
  • Narayanganj Cultural Alliance holds a vigil on the eighth of every month.
  • Taqi's father asked why the trial has not begun and no charge sheet has been submitted.

Modi says armed Maoists are gone, but warns of "dimagi Naxals"

At a Diwali event with CRPF personnel in Bastar, the PM said armed Naxalites are gone but their ideological supporters are not.

How do you defeat an enemy the government says no longer carries a gun? Prime Minister Narendra Modi gave his answer on Sunday, November 8, 2026, while celebrating Diwali with CRPF personnel at a camp in Bastar, Chhattisgarh.

First, the background. Naxalism is a Maoist armed insurgency, and the CRPF is one of the central forces that fights it. The government declared the country free of Naxalism on March 31, 2026.

Here's the catch. Mr. Modi said the threat is not over. Armed Naxalites have disappeared from the forests, he said, but "dimagi Naxals", meaning ideological supporters, have taken control of several organisations.

He said they have infiltrated several political parties and are instigating people against the Constitution and democracy. "We must identify and defeat them," he said. The reported speech names no party or group. He had used the same phrase on Independence Day in August 2026.

Then he looked back. At one point, he said, over 230 districts across nearly 20 States were affected, and there were around 2,000 Naxalite attacks a year. That is roughly five a day.

He also said that between 2004 and 2013, more security personnel were killed each year than Naxalites were eliminated. He blamed previous governments, saying they treated Naxalism as an incurable disease.

So what changed? In his telling, the government hit the funding. He said the fake currency nexus was destroyed, weapons supply stopped and recruitment was curbed. He also said security forces were given freedom to take every necessary decision.

One number stood out. Mr. Modi said that over the past year, for the first time in 47 years, not a single CRPF personnel lost their life on duty.

All of this is the Prime Minister's account, in a speech that also took aim at political opponents. The report gives no independent figures to check it against.

That leaves one open question. If the enemy is now an idea rather than a fighter, who decides who counts as a "dimagi Naxal"?

A huge Amsterdam march called for tighter trade limits on Israel and an end to arms sales, while the Dutch prime minister says his government is already doing what it can.

More than 200,000 people marched through Amsterdam on Sunday, according to Amnesty International's Dutch branch, one of the organisers. Let each of them walk past you for one second and you would be standing there for more than 55 hours.

Here's the catch on that number. It comes from an organiser. Still, it was a very large crowd.

They called it the Red Line protest, and many wore red. A red line is a limit you say must not be crossed. The marchers want tighter trade restrictions on Israel and a halt to all arms sales.

One marcher, Mike van Dongen, said what is happening in Gaza is "inhumane". Of the Dutch authorities he said: "They don't dare take any serious steps."

Along a normally busy street closed to traffic, a man at a bus stop shouted "Free, free Palestine!" through a loudspeaker. Many marchers echoed him as they passed.

This was not the first. A similar protest took place in early October last year, after Red Line demonstrations in The Hague last June.

So why keep marching? Al Jazeera reports that the war has killed at least 74,000 Palestinians, including 21,000 children, since October 2023. A ceasefire took effect on October 10 last year, but Israeli attacks and killings have continued almost daily, it says.

What has the Dutch government done? Last year the foreign minister resigned after failing to secure new sanctions against Israel. A new minority coalition took power in February and banned imports from Israeli settlements, which the Dutch government and most of the world consider illegal. That caused a diplomatic spat with Israel.

Prime Minister Rob Jetten joined a march last year but was not at this one. On Friday he said: "Every single day and every single week, we do what we can to alleviate the terrible situation there." He added that his government is exerting greater political and diplomatic pressure on Israel to abide by its agreements on Gaza.

So the crowd and the government are arguing over one thing: where the line sits, and whether it has been reached. Sunday's march did not settle that.

Anthropic reportedly pays $3.4bn to take over another firm's generator orders

The Financial Times reports that Anthropic set out to buy electricity from a Texas supplier and ended up taking over its gas generator orders instead.

Anthropic went shopping for electricity. According to the Financial Times, it came back with someone else's generators.

The deal is worth about $3.4 billion and covers 1.7 gigawatts of heavy-duty gas-fired generators. Anthropic, the company behind the Claude AI models, got them from OnePWR Solutions, an equipment supplier in San Antonio, Texas. The two sides reached the agreement in late September.

So why buy the machines instead of the power? The answer is in the order book.

About two years earlier, OnePWR had ordered the generators from Caterpillar, the heavy-equipment maker. Then it needed financing to finish the purchase. Anthropic had planned to buy electricity from OnePWR. Instead, it took over the generator orders itself.

Think of a long queue at a bakery. The person near the front can't cover their big order, so the customer who was going to buy bread from them pays for the whole tray.

The deal also came with the engineering and construction know-how needed to install the generators. Anthropic did not buy OnePWR, and no OnePWR employees moved over.

Why does an AI company want generators at all? AI models run on data centres that use huge amounts of electricity, and gas generators can make that power right on site. Demand for Claude has pushed Anthropic to hunt for more computing and power, and AI companies are seeking this kind of equipment as demand for power rises.

Now the numbers. $3.4 billion for 1.7 gigawatts works out to about $2 per watt. So the generator capacity behind one 60-watt light bulb costs roughly $120.

The FT called it the largest known single purchase of power-generation equipment by an AI company. Here's the twist: it's small next to Anthropic's plans. Reuters reported in September that the company expected to spend more than $500 billion on AI infrastructure over the next decade, and this deal is under 1% of that.

One caution. Anthropic and OnePWR declined to comment to the FT, and Reuters said it could not immediately verify the report.

The reports also leave a hole: they don't say where the generators will go or when they'll start making electricity. For now, 1.7 gigawatts of gas generators have a reported new owner and no known address.

Key facts

  • Anthropic agreed to pay about $3.4 billion for 1.7 gigawatts of gas-fired generator commitments, the FT reported.
  • OnePWR Solutions had ordered the generators from Caterpillar about two years earlier and needed financing to finish the purchase.
  • Anthropic did not acquire OnePWR, and no OnePWR staff moved over.
  • Neither company has confirmed the deal, and the sites for the generators are unknown.

Webb telescope catches 104 magnified stars in the Dragon galaxy

A massive galaxy cluster magnified drifting stars across 6.5 billion light-years, offering clues to how galaxies age and what dark matter is made of.

José María Palencia led a team comparing space telescope images collected between 2022 and 2024 to look for changes in brightness. They focused on the Dragon galaxy, located about 6.5 billion light-years away.

The starlight began traveling before Earth even formed. Normally, an individual star that far away is far too faint to see. Yet Palencia and his team detected 104 magnified star events, more than doubling the previous record of 44.

How do you spot a single star across half the visible universe? Nature provided a cosmic magnifying glass. A massive galaxy cluster named Abell 370 sits between the Dragon and Earth, bending and focusing the background light with its gravity.

This is the clever bit. Think of a glass of water on a kitchen table focusing sunlight into sharp, bright lines on the counter. Astronomers call those lines of extreme magnification caustics.

Here is the catch. The galaxy cluster is lumpy rather than smooth, so its stars and dark matter break those lines into tiny ripples called microcaustics. As stars in the Dragon drift across these ripples, their light flares up to thousands of times its normal level before fading within days or weeks.

Only the most luminous stars can shine brightly enough during these crossings to be caught, which means they are all supergiants. The team found roughly equal numbers of hot blue supergiants and cool red supergiants. That one-to-one ratio suggests the Dragon is a relatively mature system that is still forming new stars.

Then came the puzzle. When the researchers mapped where these events happened, the pattern looked lopsided. Close to the main magnification line, the count dropped on one side while staying steady on the other.

The imbalance matches predictions for fuzzy dark matter, an idea where invisible mass is made of light, wavelike particles. Did the telescope catch our first hint of wavelike dark matter, or did the team simply misplace the gravitational line in their computer model?

Key facts

  • Astronomers using the James Webb Space Telescope identified 104 unique magnified stellar events in the Dragon galaxy, more than doubling the previous record of 44.
  • The flares occur when background stars drift across gravitational caustics created by the intervening galaxy cluster Abell 370, temporarily brightening by thousands of times.
  • The detected stars show an equal balance of blue and red supergiants, indicating a relatively mature galaxy that is still forming stars.
  • A drop in detections on one side of the magnification line matches predictions for wavelike dark matter, though a slight flaw in the gravitational model could also explain it.

How do you stop a throat growth that returns every time a surgeon cuts it out?

How a director passed as a Muslim to film Mecca in 1928

George Krugers changed his name and had himself circumcised to shoot the first documentary of the pilgrimage to Mecca.

A filmmaker named George Krugers had himself circumcised and adopted the name Abdul Wahid just to carry a movie camera into Mecca.

Mecca had been closed to non-Muslims for centuries. A few European travellers had slipped inside over the generations, but almost nobody had ever managed to bring moving film back out.

Krugers saw a rare opportunity in the Dutch East Indies, where he ran his own production company. By 1928, roughly 50,000 pilgrims from the colony made the long journey every year, yet curious audiences in the West had never seen the pilgrimage on screen.

Here is how he got inside. He consulted Agus Salim, a prominent Islamic leader, and secured a letter of recommendation to smooth his arrival. The Dutch consul in Jeddah backed the project, and local officials were notified.

Krugers boarded the steamship SS Madioen in February 1928, armed with a compact 35-millimetre Bell and Howell camera. He travelled alongside rural pilgrims, filming their journey across the ocean, their medical checks in Sabang, and their arrival at the port of Jeddah.

From the coast, the pilgrims set out across the desert toward Mecca on foot and camelback. Krugers followed them into the city, using a network of local informants who helped him find filming angles.

Even with official backing, pointing a camera at sacred ground was delicate work. Krugers kept his distance from the most sensitive spaces, filming the Kaaba from a high vantage point rather than stepping into the dense crowd.

His camera captured landmarks like the Zamzam Well, King Ibn Saud greeting crowds, and pilgrims performing the ritual run between the hills of Safa and Marwa. He followed them onward to the tent city of Mina and the plains of Arafat.

After returning to Java, Krugers edited the footage into a 72-minute silent feature across four reels, complete with 113 title cards. The Great Mecca Feast had its Dutch premiere on November 9, 1928, as the first documentary film of the Hajj.

You might expect such an audacious film to become a classic. Instead, the documentary faded into obscurity after a few showings, surviving quietly as the only work from Krugers that was not lost to time.

Key facts

  • The Great Mecca Feast was filmed by George Krugers and premiered in the Netherlands on November 9, 1928.
  • Because Mecca was closed to non-Muslims, Krugers was circumcised and adopted the name Abdul Wahid to pass as a Muslim.
  • By 1928, approximately 50,000 pilgrims from the Dutch East Indies made the annual journey to Mecca.
  • Shot on an Eyemo 35-millimetre camera, the 72-minute silent film is the only surviving work by Krugers.

Learn · Physics from first principles

The unsolved problems in physics

Where the laws of nature break down and why physics keeps a list of what it cannot explain.

Two everyday phenomena, how a solid block absorbs heat and how light travels across empty space, once seemed to hold the only remaining doubts in classical physics. In a 1900 speech, Lord Kelvin called them two clouds, yet resolving them took less than a decade and created both special relativity and quantum mechanics.

That rapid transformation shows what an open problem actually is. It is a boundary marker where our working descriptions of nature fail to explain what we observe.

Some of these gaps are theoretical, where formulas cannot account for experimental results, while others are experimental, where we cannot yet build tests to probe the ideas. Think of our two strongest frameworks as two languages that refuse to agree: the Standard Model governs subatomic particles, while general relativity explains gravity and large masses. Under extreme conditions, like the Big Bang and the centers of black holes, their mathematics clashes and both theories break down completely.

Tracking these unresolved questions is a regular practice. In 1984, mathematical physicist Barry Simon proposed 15 open problems on quantum operators, and by 2000, researchers had solved about 5 of them. Simon introduced a second list of 15 problems that year, and physicists eventually solved at least 3 more.

Other lists move much more slowly. At the Strings 2000 conference, members proposed 10 millennium problems in theoretical physics, and not one has been conclusively solved as of 2025. Today, major questions remain unanswered beyond the Standard Model, including the absolute mass of neutrinos, matter-antimatter asymmetry, and the identity of dark matter and dark energy.

So what happens when the very small and the very heavy collide at a single point in space? That is the question the next lesson answers: how do physicists plan to merge the Standard Model and general relativity into a single workable rule?

Key facts

  • In 1900, Lord Kelvin identified two unresolved issues in classical physics that were solved within a decade by special relativity and quantum mechanics.
  • The Standard Model is mathematically inconsistent with general relativity, causing both theories to break down at the Big Bang and at the centers of black holes.
  • At the Strings 2000 conference, physicists proposed 10 millennium problems in theoretical physics, none of which has been conclusively solved as of 2025.

The slow work beneath the headlines

A day’s events make more sense when you ask what has been building underneath them.

A headline tells you what happened. The harder question is what had to be true for it to happen. Someone had to claim a seat at a table. A system had to depend on a single point of supply. A rule had to hold, or fail. Those quieter arrangements are easy to miss until an event brings them into view.

One pattern today is that authority and trust do not arrive together. France says European officials will attend Ukraine talks after the Kremlin said they had no role. A Narayanganj group still holds its monthly vigil for Taqi, whose father says his trial has not begun. These are very different stories. Both leave you asking who gets heard, and what happens while an answer is delayed.

Another pattern is how much can rest on something out of sight. A strike on one gas field cut the supply to Kurdistan’s power plants. In experiments, teams of AI agents reached their goals while breaking rules. The systems did what they were built to do, until a point of strain showed what else their design allowed. That is when a technical detail becomes a public question.

News gives you the visible moment: the meeting, the strike, the result. The slower story is how a decision gains legitimacy, how a service becomes dependent, and how people learn whether a system can be trusted. Those processes keep moving between headlines.

A 20-minute gunfight, then a flattened Hebron neighbourhood

After two gunmen killed two Israeli soldiers near Hebron, Israeli forces demolished homes in the district the gunmen were said to come from, and local officials say about 1,500 people were evicted.

The gunfight lasted 20 minutes. The fallout, according to the Hebron municipality, left about 1,500 people evicted from their homes by the next afternoon.

Here is what happened. On Friday, two gunmen attacked an Israeli military checkpoint outside Hebron, a West Bank city of 250,000 people. Two Israeli soldiers were killed and three were injured. Both gunmen were killed too.

Then came the response. Israeli forces moved into Ein Deir Baha, the district the two gunmen were alleged to come from. The municipality says 35 hectares, or 86 acres, were destroyed or marked for destruction.

Videos sent by a Hebron resident showed every house flattened across several blocks. In one, soldiers spray a black cross on Palestinian houses. In another, a bulldozer pulls down a stone wall on a hillside.

So who is losing their homes? A municipality spokesperson said about 300 families had been evicted. Some went to friends and relatives. Others were sheltered in community halls and children's play areas the municipality opened up.

The whole city was also sealed off, with checkpoints on every point of entry.

The political side matters here. Israel votes in four days. Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu went to the attack site and said Israel would "act to destroy this place". Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich filmed himself in body armour in front of a building being demolished, and called for a campaign "exactly like in Gaza and Lebanon".

Local people call this collective punishment, meaning a whole group is punished for what a few did. Issa Amro, a Hebron civil rights activist, said his sister's family was among those driven out. "They are not at all connected to what happened yesterday," he said.

Wafa, a Palestinian news agency, reported Israeli plans to displace a further 2,500 people. The Israeli army was asked for comment on the operations and the videos, and the Guardian report does not include a reply.

Amro's own question is still open. He said he does not know whether this is "only election propaganda" or a serious plan made for after the vote.

Key facts

Why would oil get more expensive because of a fight that might happen? That question is behind oil hitting $117, a price it had not reached since April.

Here's the trick. Oil traders pay for what they think tomorrow looks like, as well as for what is happening today. The worry this time is a flare-up in hostilities involving Iran, which is a major oil producer. If you fear supply could get squeezed, you pay more now.

And it doesn't stay in the oil market. The jump in energy prices has pushed up US inflation expectations, which means what people think prices will do next. Energy goes into moving and making almost everything, so pricier oil usually turns up in other prices too.

What set off the fear, and whether the hostilities will actually flare up, the early reporting doesn't say. For now, $117 is what a worry costs.

Astronomers think they found the night Van Gogh painted The Starry Night

A new study uses star shapes, weather records and cicadas to argue The Starry Night shows the pre-dawn sky of July 22, 1889.

One detail in The Starry Night did not add up: the moon. Van Gogh painted a thin crescent, but the sky outside his asylum window in late June 1889 held a fatter, waning gibbous moon.

That was the problem with the old answer. Art historian Albert Boime, working in the 1980s, matched the painting to June 19, 1889, using Venus and a cluster of stars he read as the constellation Aries. The date came from letters van Gogh sent his brother.

To explain the moon, Boime said van Gogh took some artistic license. He pointed to scraped paint on the crescent as a sign the painter may have drawn a fuller moon, then changed his mind.

Here's the catch. A new study in Scientific Reports says the stars were misread.

Elijah Jeong-hyun Kim of Kyung Hee University in South Korea says an observational astronomer sees the Hyades in Taurus, a V-shaped cluster. His team's shape analysis matched five central stars in the painting to the Hyades, and matched them even better to a sketch van Gogh made after finishing the painting.

Put the Hyades, Venus near the horizon and a crescent moon together, and the team lands on the pre-dawn sky of July 22, 1889. Wait, that fixes the moon without any artistic license.

But there's a new puzzle. Most scholars think van Gogh had a seizure around mid-July that kept him from painting for a while, which would rule out a late July date.

So the team went back to his letters. He wrote that the seizure struck while he painted on a windy day. Weather records show only intermittent wind from July 15 to 26, then strong mistral gusts from July 27. That could leave room for a painting on the 22nd.

Then came the cicadas. Van Gogh mentioned sweltering heat and cicadas in letters about his summer paintings. The researchers say cicada weather means above 86 degrees Fahrenheit. June had four such days, and July had eighteen.

None of this proves the date, and the researchers say as much. It does suggest that night was the focus of what he observed. Whether a painter would wait for the sky to match his canvas, or the other way round, is still anyone's guess.

AI designed molecular scissors that nature never made, and they cut

David Baker's lab used AI to design protein-cutting enzymes, and some work, though the cysteine ones were less active than the yeast enzyme they drew on.

Nobel laureate David Baker's lab at the University of Washington has unveiled two kinds of AI-designed proteases. Those are enzymes that act like molecular scissors, cutting other proteins at specific sites.

In test tubes, the designer scissors chopped up proteins linked to Alzheimer's and ALS. In cells, they worked like switches for inflammation-related proteins, turning them on only after a precise snip.

Why is this so hard? Proteins are strings of amino acids that fold into 3D shapes, and getting the shape right is only half the battle. An enzyme also has a catalytic core, and its parts must move, align and interact at just the right moment.

Here's how the team did it. They took inspiration from enzymes in a crayfish's digestive system and in yeast, then used their design tool RFdiffusion2 to build new protein backbones around each enzyme's catalytic core. AI picked the amino acids, and AlphaFold3 predicted the structures. Most looked nothing like known proteins.

Now the scorecard. Thirteen of the cysteine proteases worked in a test tube, which is 19 percent, and all were less active than the original yeast enzyme. Among 135 zinc protease candidates, 36 percent cut their targets.

The best designs made the reaction roughly 100 million times faster than when the target, amyloid-beta, broke down on its own. That protein forms toxic clumps in Alzheimer's disease.

Why would medicine care? Antibodies latch onto one target at a time, so they need a high dose. Think of a protease as a pair of scissors instead of a clip. Baker said you need only a small amount because each scissor molecule can cut many, many targets.

Here's the catch. The low success rates expose an uncomfortable truth: current AI tools still struggle to make more complex molecular machines.

So the lab built RFdiffusion3, which zooms in on individual atoms rather than mainly working at the level of amino acids. That gives scientists finer control over what they build.

Will atom-level design lift those hit rates enough to match nature's enzymes? Anqi Chen, a study author, said proteases are just the beginning. Nobody can say yet how far the beginning goes.

  • 36 percent of 135 zinc protease candidates cut their targets; 13 cysteine proteases worked (19 percent).
  • The best designs sped up breakdown of amyloid-beta roughly 100 million times.

The army that fought a war by calling itself volunteers

In 1950 China sent regular troops into the Korean War under the name "volunteers" so it would not have to officially be at war with the United States.

How do you send 250,000 soldiers into a war without declaring war? In October 1950, China's answer was to change the army's name.

The first wave, from 25 October to 5 November, included six corps totalling 250,000 men. On paper, they were not China's army. They were the Chinese People's Volunteers.

Here's the catch. Every unit had been transferred from the People's Liberation Army, China's regular army, on the orders of Chairman Mao Zedong. The "volunteer" label was a costume, and it was built to prevent an official war with the United States.

Why bother? The United Nations forces in Korea were under US command. China wanted to avoid open war with the US and the other UN members. Calling its soldiers volunteers made it look like individuals had simply turned up on the battlefield.

The name has its own small mystery. Some scholars say the first choice was "support army", and that Huang Yanpei, a vice premier, warned it would read as direct aggression against the US. Newer studies say Premier Zhou Enlai had already written "volunteer army" on a manuscript on 7 July 1950.

China had also warned the world. On 20 August, Zhou said Korea is China's neighbor, and diplomats passed on that China would intervene. US President Harry Truman called it a bald attempt to blackmail the UN and dismissed it.

Inside China, the decision was not easy. Many leaders, senior military ones included, resisted confronting the US. Peng Dehuai argued that if US troops conquered Korea and reached the Yalu River, they might cross it and invade China. The Politburo agreed to intervene.

Did the disguise work? For a while. It deceived US intelligence and the UN about the size and nature of the Chinese forces. They later worked out the volunteers were the PLA's North East Frontier Force, yet kept using the name to keep the war from escalating.

Here's the last twist. The volunteers entered Korea on 19 October 1950 and did not completely withdraw until October 1958. That is eight years, and about 3 million Chinese civilian and military personnel served in Korea during the war.

  • Its units were all transferred from the PLA; the volunteer name was meant to prevent an official war with the US.
  • The first wave of six corps totalled 250,000 men.

Open problems: how physics keeps a list of what it can't explain

Lesson 15 of 15: the best physicists write down their ignorance, and the lists have a surprising track record.

In 1900, Lord Kelvin gave a speech saying physics had just two problems left. He called them "two clouds" hanging over the 20th century. Both clouds turned out to be the whole future.

Start with something you already know from life. When a theory works almost everywhere, the places where it fails are the interesting bit. A rule that explains nearly everything but chokes on one case is telling you the rule is incomplete. Physicists call those cases open problems.

Some open problems are theoretical: our best theories cannot explain something we observe. Others are experimental: we have an idea, but nobody knows how to build the test. Kelvin's clouds were of the first kind. One was the missing evidence for the luminiferous aether, a supposed substance that light was thought to travel through. The other was the equipartition theorem, which failed to predict the heat capacity of solids, meaning how much heat a solid soaks up as it warms.

Here's the catch. Those small clouds were not small. Within a decade, special relativity settled the first one and quantum mechanics settled the second. Two problems, two brand new branches of physics. That is the clever bit of the whole lesson: a nagging gap is often where the next theory is hiding.

So how are we doing today? Let's do the arithmetic on some famous lists. In 1984, Barry Simon proposed 15 open problems on quantum operators, and by 2000 about 5 were solved, which leaves about 10 still open after 16 years. He released a second list of 15 in 2000, and at least 3 had fallen. Roger Penrose's 1982 list on general relativity still had about 14 unsolved in 2017. Progress is real, and it is slow.

The big ones are bigger. The Standard Model, our best theory of particles, is mathematically inconsistent with general relativity, our best theory of gravity. Both break down in extreme places, like the Big Bang and the centers of black holes. Add the nature of dark matter and dark energy, the absolute mass of neutrinos, and why there is more matter than antimatter, and you have a long list. Vitaly Ginzburg added three great problems of his own: the arrow of time, the interpretations of quantum mechanics, and whether life can arise from physics alone.

That is the end of the course, so there is no lesson 16 to answer the next question. The next question is yours: which of these gaps could be the next cloud that turns into a whole new science? If you want to dig in, Welch Labs, 3Blue1Brown, PBS Space Time and Veritasium are good places to go deeper.

  • In 1900 Lord Kelvin named two "clouds" over physics: the aether and the heat capacity of solids. Both were solved within a decade.
  • Barry Simon's 1984 list had 15 problems, with about 5 solved by 2000.
  • The Standard Model is inconsistent with general relativity, and both break down at singularities like the Big Bang.
  • The 10 problems proposed at Strings 2000 had none conclusively solved as of 2025.

Riyadh airport hit twice in two days as oil summit nears

A Houthi strike killed 12 at Riyadh's main airport, it was hit again the next day, and the attacks are now shaking oil flows and prices.

Twelve people were killed at Riyadh's King Khalid International Airport on Saturday, and hundreds more were injured. On Sunday, the same airport was hit again.

Yemen's Houthis, an armed group, claimed both attacks. On Sunday they said they had targeted the airport several times, along with Saudi energy facilities.

The dead include Azza Ramzy, a 28-year-old Egyptian flight attendant, and a Syrian woman named Buthaina Ahmad Oweida. An Indian national later died from injuries.

Sunday's strike was smaller in what we know, not necessarily in danger. Saudi authorities said an "unidentified projectile" struck Terminal 4, and air defenses destroyed a cruise missile near the airport. The civil aviation authority said the attack "resulted in injuries" but gave no number.

Here's the catch: the Houthis are saying more is coming. A spokesman told travelers and airlines to stay away from Saudi airports and to "take our warning seriously". He also claimed Saudi Arabia "bears responsibility for any failed interceptions".

So why does an airport matter to your petrol price? The repeated attacks have temporarily halted oil flows and driven up global oil prices.

The timing makes it sharper. The attacks came just before the World Petroleum Congress, a major oil summit scheduled for this week.

The bosses of Shell, ExxonMobil, BP and TotalEnergies were due to attend. Given the attacks, it is unclear whether they will take the risk.

One more reaction is worth noticing. The United Arab Emirates issued a statement of solidarity with Saudi Arabia, backing "all measures aimed at safeguarding the kingdom's security and stability". The Guardian calls that significant, given the major rupture in relations between the two this year.

Watch the summit this week to see which oil chiefs actually turn up.

Key facts

  • 12 people were killed in Saturday's attack on Riyadh's King Khalid International Airport, and hundreds were injured.
  • The airport was hit again on Sunday; a projectile struck Terminal 4 and a cruise missile was intercepted.
  • The Houthis claimed responsibility for both attacks.
  • The attacks have temporarily halted oil flows and raised global oil prices ahead of the World Petroleum Congress.

Bangladesh's home minister skips the first Bimstec home ministers' meeting

India hosts the first meeting of Bimstec home ministers on security, but Bangladesh's home minister is sending an official instead.

A first-ever meeting of seven countries' home ministers is happening in New Delhi tomorrow, 13 October. Bangladesh's home minister, Salahuddin Ahmed, will not be in the room.

He has deputed a senior home ministry official to go instead.

So what is the meeting for? The agenda, as laid out by India's Home Minister Amit Shah, covers terrorism, transnational organised crime, narcotics, radicalisation, human trafficking, illicit financial flows and cyber threats.

Here's the catch with a list like that: none of these problems stop at a border. Drug routes, trafficking networks and online scams cross countries easily, so one country's police cannot fix them alone.

That is the idea behind the meeting. It is expected to review regional security cooperation and look at ways to strengthen coordination in law enforcement, intelligence sharing and efforts against terrorism and transnational crime.

Who is at the table? Bimstec, short for the Bay of Bengal Initiative for Multi-Sectoral Technical and Economic Cooperation, was established in 1997. Its members are Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, Myanmar, Nepal, Sri Lanka and Thailand.

Shah called the meeting "historic" in a post on X. He said it reflected the commitment of member countries to building a safer, stronger and more secure region, and that India was honoured to host.

Notice the tension. A meeting built on sharing intelligence and coordinating across borders opens with one member sending a lower-ranking representative.

Bangladesh is still taking part, just not at minister level. Whether that changes at later meetings is not something the source says.

Key facts

  • The first Bimstec home ministers' meeting is in New Delhi on 13 October.
  • Bangladesh's Home Minister Salahuddin Ahmed will not attend and has deputed a senior home ministry official.
  • Agenda includes terrorism, narcotics, radicalisation, human trafficking, illicit financial flows and cyber threats.
  • Bimstec was established in 1997 and has seven members.

Trump's Russian diesel deal starts with less than a day's fuel

Trump's Treasury licensed Russian diesel sales, angering Ukraine and some Republicans, for a first shipment the US would use up in under a day.

How much diesel does America burn in one day to keep its trucks, trains and other transport moving? About 2.94 million barrels. Hold on to that number.

On Friday, October 9, President Donald Trump announced a deal with Russian President Vladimir Putin. Russia would supply diesel to US and global markets: more than 300,000 metric tons immediately, 500,000 tons in November, then 1 million tons "immediately thereafter."

That needed a workaround. Congress banned Russian energy imports in 2022, the year Russia launched its full-scale invasion of Ukraine. So the US Treasury issued a special license the same day, allowing Russian diesel to be sold and shipped in until April 2027.

Why bother? Diesel prices are high, partly because the war in Iran has cut fuel from Gulf producers, and the US midterm elections are on November 3.

Here's the catch. That first 300,000 tons is about 2.25 million barrels. Remember the opening number? It's less than one day of US transport diesel. And it's a promise, not a delivery: Russia banned most of its own diesel exports in July after attacks on its refineries.

So what was the deal really for? At a meeting in Miami, Trump's envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner told Ukrainian officials it could help bring Russia back to peace talks, according to three people familiar with the meeting. Kushner also passed on Trump's request that Ukraine stop striking Russian refineries.

Ukraine's president, Volodymyr Zelenskyy, called the deal a "gift to Putin." Trump's answer on Saturday: Ukrainians needed to "get a new president."

Then, on Sunday, Trump announced an immediate ceasefire on attacks against energy sites by both sides. Neither side confirmed it. On Monday, Zelenskyy said Russia had since hit a power plant in Kharkiv, a gas production facility and a civilian gas station, while Ukraine had held back. The Kremlin said only that "contacts are ongoing."

The pushback isn't only foreign. Seven senators, including Republicans Susan Collins, Lisa Murkowski, Thom Tillis and John Curtis, urged Trump to "immediately reverse course," arguing the imports break US law. Trump called the deal "the right choice for the American people."

So watch the tankers. Nobody yet knows when the diesel will arrive or which ships can carry it. Until then, the license is issued, the ceasefire is unconfirmed, and the diesel exists only as a promise.

Key facts

  • Trump announced on October 9 that Russia would supply more than 300,000 metric tons of diesel immediately, with more promised later.
  • That first 300,000 tons, about 2.25 million barrels, is less than one day of US transport-sector diesel use.
  • Trump announced an energy-strike ceasefire on Sunday, but neither Russia nor Ukraine confirmed it, and Zelenskyy said Russian strikes continued.
  • Seven senators, including four Republicans, urged Trump to reverse course, arguing the imports violate US law.

The selling spread fast. On Monday the Nasdaq Composite dropped 0.44% and U.S. memory maker Micron slid 3.9%. Then Asia opened: SoftBank sank 8.03%, Tokyo Electron fell 6.6% and Advantest lost 5.38%.

So why the plunge? Vey-Sern Ling of UBP said investors are jittery and looking for excuses to take profit on AI stocks that have outperformed this year, especially memory stocks. Taking profit just means selling something that has gone up, to lock in the gain.

Wool put it this way: "I read that more as a sign of how vulnerable AI sentiment is at this moment." He warned that volatility could persist as investors reassess chip demand and how long chipmakers can keep their pricing power.

There is a second worry in the background. The chief executives of Intel and Broadcom have warned that access to capital could become a major headwind for the AI boom, even though demand for computing infrastructure still outstrips supply.

What to watch: this week brings a busy run of corporate earnings and U.S. inflation data. Those could give fresh clues on interest rates, which often move in the opposite direction to growth stocks.

To crack cuttlefish camouflage, scientists first made them glow red

Researchers have engineered the first cephalopods with a new gene, a step toward watching a cuttlefish's brain paint its skin.

"Sometimes I think we've experienced every type of failure possible," says Tessa Montague, a neuroscientist at Columbia University. Her opponent was a cuttlefish egg.

First, the animal. Cuttlefish are relatives of squid and octopuses, and they are famous for disappearing. Their skin is packed with pigment-filled structures, and tiny muscles control how much of each pigment shows. They can even change the texture of their skin to look like rock or algae.

And they are fast. A report last month said cuttlefish can change their appearance in less than 200 milliseconds, using millions of these pigment structures run by the nervous system. That's a fifth of a second, about the length of a blink.

Here's the puzzle. Cuttlefish are thought to be colorblind, yet they match all sorts of surroundings. So what is the brain doing between seeing the seafloor and copying it onto the skin?

Nobody has pinned that down yet. Montague wants to watch it happen. Her plan is to give cuttlefish neurons a gene that makes them glow whenever they fire, so you could see which brain cells light up as the animal builds a pattern.

"Cuttlefish can actually tell us what they see," she said, meaning the patterns on their skin. Think of the skin as a screen showing what the brain is working on. The missing piece is a view of the brain itself.

Here's the catch. Adding a gene means getting inside a fertilized egg, and that meant peeling away dozens of layers of jelly. The team built pincers that grip the egg with a precise amount of force, plus a needle angled to pierce an embryo that is lens-shaped and nearly invisible.

Then the hatchlings had to survive, which was its own fight.

It worked. The team has produced cuttlefish that glow red under fluorescent light, described as the first cephalopods engineered to contain a new gene.

The red glow proves a new gene can get in. Neurons that light up as they fire are a further step, and the reporting gives no date for when that will arrive.

Until it does, how a colorblind animal paints the world onto its own body stays a secret the cuttlefish is keeping.

  • Tessa Montague of Columbia wants to give cuttlefish neurons a gene that makes them fluoresce when they fire.

Gusty Spence announced a loyalist ceasefire at Fernhill House in 1994, one step on the road to the Good Friday Agreement.

Most people hear "Protestants versus Catholics" and file the Troubles under religious war. It was not one.

Learn · Physics from first principles

Relativity: gravity as the shape of spacetime

Lesson 11 of 15 in physics from first principles: why Einstein said a falling apple is not being pulled.

Here is the strangest claim in this whole course so far: according to general relativity, a falling apple is not being pulled by anything. It is falling because that is how objects move when no force is acting on them.

Start with something you have seen a thousand times. You drop a ball and it speeds up on the way down. For about 200 years, the answer to why came from Isaac Newton's mechanics: gravity is a force, and it tugs the ball. Albert Einstein replaced that picture in two steps. Special relativity came in 1905, and general relativity in 1915.

Special relativity comes first, and it is about the structure of spacetime, which treats space and time as one thing instead of two. It rests on two postulates that cannot both be true in classical mechanics, yet the theory matches experiments better than classical mechanics does. It also has odd consequences: whether two events are simultaneous depends on who is looking, and clocks and lengths change with motion. Here's the catch: it only works when gravity is absent.

So how do you add gravity? Einstein began with a simple idea called the equivalence principle. Being at rest in a gravitational field, such as standing on the surface of the Earth, is physically identical to being in accelerated motion. No experiment can tell the two apart.

Now a worked example. A dropped ball gains about 9.8 metres per second of speed every second. After 3 seconds it is moving at about 29.4 metres per second. Newton says the Earth's force does that. Einstein says the ball is moving freely, and the speeding up is just what free motion looks like here. That raised a problem. Objects in free fall accelerate relative to each other, and neither classical mechanics nor special relativity allows freely moving objects to do that.

His fix was to propose that spacetime itself is curved. Working with the mathematician Marcel Grossmann, he found that the idea fit into a geometry developed in the 1800s. By 1915, about eight years after he began in 1907, he had the Einstein field equations, which tie the curvature of spacetime to the mass, energy and momentum inside it.

At first the theory seemed to offer little beyond minor corrections to Newton, and most of its claims were on an astronomical scale. Around 1960 it became central to physics and astronomy, and discoveries such as quasars in 1963, pulsars in 1967 and the first black hole candidates in 1981 were explained by it. That brings up a question: relativity handles the very large, so what happens when physics shrinks to the very small? That is where the next lesson goes.

Key facts

  • Special relativity was published in 1905 and general relativity in 1915.
  • General relativity began with the equivalence principle: standing on Earth is physically identical to accelerated motion.
  • Einstein proposed that spacetime is curved, and the 1915 field equations relate that curvature to mass, energy and momentum.
  • Around 1960 general relativity became central to physics and astronomy.

Saying it is not the same as it happening

A few quiet patterns run under today's headlines, and one question that fits all of them.

Read the whole paper in one sitting and a quiet pattern shows up. Saying a thing and the thing happening keep coming apart. Trump announced an energy ceasefire, and Ukraine says a power plant was struck right after. India's top judge says firecracker bans only work if people agree.

That is really a story about who sets the rules. Rules on paper are cheap. The ones that hold are the ones people go along with, or the ones backed by something heavier, like the threat of a blacklist hanging over sailors asked to cross the Strait of Hormuz.

The second pattern is fragility. A lot of the world runs through narrow pipes: a strait, a port, an airport. Squeeze one and the effect travels far from where it happened, usually arriving as a price.

Then there is how the news itself works. News is made of events: a strike, a fire, a death. The things underneath move slowly and rarely arrive with a date. Squeeze a slow number into a headline and it gets misread, as with the 73% wildlife decline, where half of studied populations are shrinking and the other half are growing or steady.

So here is a question that works on any headline: who has to go along with this for it to be real?

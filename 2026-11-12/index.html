Cover story

A woman with a death sentence says she is going home

Sheikh Hasina, Bangladesh's ex-prime minister, says she will return in December even though she faces being hanged.

She faces being hanged. And Sheikh Hasina says she would rather die in her own country than stay away.

Hasina served twice as prime minister of Bangladesh. More than two years ago, large street protests forced her to resign and flee.

Since then she has been living in India. Bangladesh asked India to hand her over, and India refused.

Here's the catch. To go home, she needs a way in.

Hasina says she asked for a new passport so she could return. The government in Dhaka turned the request down.

She has not backed off. The former leader announced she will come home in December, no matter what.

So what happens if she does? That is the question Al Jazeera's Inside Story put to three guests: Shahab Enam Khan of Jahangirnagar University, Sanjay Kumar Bhardwaj of Jawaharlal Nehru University, and Taqbir Huda, a human rights lawyer at Oxford.

The source gives us the question, not their answers. What it does lay out is a standoff with three locked doors. Dhaka will not issue the passport, India will not send her back, and she says she is coming anyway.

That is the part to watch. December is the date she has named, and whether she shows up, and how, would tell us whether this is a plan or a statement.

Key facts

  • Hasina faces a death sentence and says she will return to Bangladesh in December.
  • She fled after large street protests more than two years ago and lives in India.
  • India refused Bangladesh's request to hand her over.
  • Dhaka turned down her request for a new passport, she says.

Garment owners weigh shutting 210 Ashulia factories with no end date

Owners are considering an indefinite closure of 210 factories in Ashulia and nearby areas, a step under which workers are not entitled to wages, with a final call due Saturday.

What happens to a worker's pay when an owner closes a factory with no end date? Under Section 13(1) of the Labour Act, the answer is blunt: workers are not entitled to wages for the period of closure.

That rule is now at the centre of a big decision. Garment owners are considering shutting 210 factories in Ashulia and other unrest-prone areas indefinitely. Industry leaders reached an initial decision at an emergency meeting yesterday, and all members will take the final decision on Saturday.

So why now? Owners say workers are entering factories but refusing to work, or leaving after marking attendance. Yesterday, 143 factories in Ashulia, Jamgora and Hemayetpur suspended operations.

A BGMEA leader, who asked not to be named, said the number stood at 50 until 11:30am yesterday and then kept rising as the day went on.

Here's the catch. Mohammad Rashed, a vice-president of BKMEA, said: "Some factories have been unable to operate for 11 days. How will they pay workers' wages?" He said an earlier government request to keep factories open had failed to resolve the situation.

That is where Section 13(1) comes in. Employers can use it to declare a factory closed indefinitely if workers remain unwilling to work or unrest makes operations impossible. Some owners have already issued closure notices under it.

The affected belt includes names like Ha-Meem, New Age, Ananta Garment, Starling, Envoy, Islam and Dekko. BGMEA sources say it employs around 300,000 workers.

Closing is not free for owners either. Shovon Islam of Sparrow Group said subcontracting production elsewhere might not be feasible because of order commitments and compliance requirements. Factories could end up paying for air shipments or risk losing orders.

Some places have calmed. Unrest has eased in Gazipur, Mirpur and Narayanganj since the announcement that the new wages would take effect in December. Tensions persist in parts of Ashulia and Savar.

Saturday is the date to watch. If members back the closure, the clause that owners have already started using would apply to far more factories at once.

Key facts

  • Owners are considering indefinite closure of 210 factories in Ashulia and other unrest-prone areas; final decision at a members' meeting on Saturday.
  • 143 factories in Ashulia, Jamgora and Hemayetpur suspended operations on 11 November.
  • Under Section 13(1) of the Labour Act, workers are not entitled to wages during an indefinite closure.
  • The affected belt employs around 300,000 workers, according to BGMEA sources.

A day after the Islamabad High Court ordered access, two PTI lawyers sat with the jailed ex-PM and his wife for about three hours.

First, who is who. Imran Khan is a former prime minister and founder of the PTI party. He has been in prison since August 5, 2023, and his wife was arrested on January 17, 2025, in a separate case. Both were sentenced and are appealing.

So why now? On Tuesday, the Islamabad High Court, hearing those appeals, directed authorities to facilitate meetings with his legal team. It said a meeting should not be less than an hour.

Asked why this one ran so long, Khosa laughed and said, "Ask those who allowed the meeting."

Here's the catch. The court already allows Imran meetings on Tuesdays and Thursdays with family, lawyers and associates. Despite multiple court directives, those meetings have been largely restricted.

Inside, Safdar said, legal and medical issues were discussed in detail. He said he would keep representing both in the main appeals in the Al Qadir Trust case, while Khosa would help on Bushra's petition about her eye complications.

Imran's own eyes are part of this. Hours before the meeting, he was taken to the Pakistan Institute of Medical Sciences for a check-up and follow-up eye treatment. The hospital said his health readings were "normal" and he was "stable".

Khosa painted a mixed picture. He said Imran's blood pressure fluctuated to a "terrifying extent", but that he was "completely active and his will was very strong".

Khosa said details of Imran's health, and what he saw himself, would be released later. That release is the thing to watch. So far the public has a hospital saying "normal" and a lawyer saying "terrifying".

Russia and Ukraine agree a huge prisoner swap, but not peace

Russia and Ukraine agreed a prisoner exchange of more than 1,000 people each in Abu Dhabi, but the talks left their territorial dispute unresolved.

Two countries that cannot agree on where a border should be have just agreed on something else: to swap more than 1,000 prisoners each.

That is the main result from two days of talks in Abu Dhabi, the first US-brokered talks between Russia and Ukraine since February. The Guardian reports it as the "largest prisoner exchange" of the war, which is nearly five years old.

Jared Kushner, one of Donald Trump's envoys, said the swap would "reunite families, build trust and create momentum towards peace". Think of it as the one thing both sides could say yes to.

Here's the catch. On the big question, land, there was little movement.

Russia wants control of all the Donbas, including areas Kyiv still holds, plus the parts of two southern regions it does not yet occupy. Vladimir Medinsky, who led Moscow's delegation, said "every inch of our land will be returned to Russia, one way or another." He said Russia would be satisfied only "when there is peace on its terms".

Ukraine's David Arakhamia said the Russian team arrived with "very, very strict directives". Still, he reported "really big progress" on security guarantees for Ukraine after the war, and said there was good momentum toward a solution before the new year.

Medinsky also wants something bigger than Ukraine. He called for a new security system in Europe, "a new Yalta and Potsdam", and described Ukraine as "just a cyst that burst".

That sounds like Russia's 2021 demands to Nato, which included halting enlargement and limiting forces and missiles in eastern Europe. The west called them a non-starter, and weeks later Russia invaded. European governments sent envoys to Abu Dhabi, though they are not believed to have taken part directly.

Next, the US envoys go to Moscow on Thursday to meet Vladimir Putin. Then they head to Dublin to meet Volodymyr Zelenskyy and European partners. What Putin says to those proposals is the next thing to watch.

Key facts

  • Russia and Ukraine agreed a prisoner exchange expected to involve more than 1,000 prisoners from each side.
  • The Abu Dhabi talks were the first US-brokered talks since February.
  • Russia still demands all of the Donbas and the parts of two southern regions it does not yet occupy.
  • US envoys meet Putin in Moscow on Thursday, then Zelenskyy in Dublin.

Nvidia's board is reportedly preparing to oust co-founder Jensen Huang

The Wall Street Journal says Nvidia's board may remove Jensen Huang after an affair that got tangled up in claims about Chinese spying, and the shares fell more than 7 percent.

More than 7 percent. That's how far Nvidia's shares fell in after-hours trading after a single news report. Put another way, roughly one dollar in every fourteen that investors had in the company disappeared on paper, before the stock market even opened again.

The report came from the Wall Street Journal. It says Nvidia's board is preparing to oust its chief executive, Jensen Huang, and plans to meet as soon as Wednesday to decide his future.

Wait, what? Huang co-founded Nvidia, the company whose chips power much of the world's artificial intelligence. Can a company really fire the person who started it?

It can. A company's board of directors has the power to remove the CEO, and that includes founders.

So what happened? Headlines across several outlets point to an extramarital affair. The reports we have don't say who the other person is or when it came out.

Here's the twist that turns gossip into geopolitics. A Reuters headline says Huang got entangled in "a China chip and spy scandal". The South China Morning Post reports allegations that Huang was targeted by Chinese intelligence. Beijing denies it.

Why would that matter so much? In Washington, the tech race with China is a major political fight, and the US limits sales of advanced chips to China. When a top chip boss is linked, even by allegation, to Chinese spies, politicians take notice.

According to Chinese experts quoted by the South China Morning Post, China hawks in Washington could weaponise the allegations. One of its pieces asks whether this could become another "spy balloon" saga, a reference to the Chinese balloon that drifted over the US in 2023 and soured relations.

The internet, meanwhile, has done what it does. The art site Hyperallergic calls the affair "meme gold".

Here's the catch. Most of this rests on one newspaper's report, passed along second-hand, and we haven't seen a response from Nvidia.

The thing to watch is the board meeting: whether it happens, what it decides, and who would run Nvidia if Huang goes.

A new method finds the M87 black hole's ring without the usual guesswork

A new imaging method, built on measurements that calibration errors can't corrupt, recovers the ring-shaped shadow of the black hole at the heart of M87.

Some researchers looked at the famous black hole picture and saw no ring at all. That is the odd part of this story.

The picture shows M87*, the supermassive black hole at the centre of the galaxy Messier 87. It was made by the Event Horizon Telescope, a global network of radio observatories linked so they act like one Earth-sized dish. Per the source, the image appeared as a ring.

Here's the catch. Even an Earth-sized dish built this way is mostly gaps, because the telescopes only sample a sparse set of views. Turning that into a picture is an under-constrained puzzle, and the answer can shift with calibration, post-processing and settings the analyst picks.

So different teams got different pictures. Some outside analyses even challenged the ring. One found that if you widen the field of view past 120 microarcseconds, a single elongated bright spot is favoured over a ring.

Now a team of Samuel Lai, with Nithyanandan Thyagarajan, O. Ivy Wong and Foivos Diakogiannis, has tried a different route. They published it in Nature on 11 November 2026.

Their trick is to use quantities called closure invariants. Think of a group photo where every camera is slightly miscalibrated: you ignore each camera's own errors and keep only the relationships that survive them. Closure invariants carry the shape of the source and are unaffected by errors in wavefront and instrument calibration.

They paired that with an independent imaging algorithm, and the method avoids prescriptive assumptions about what shape the source should have. Then they ran it on the M87* data.

The result, in the authors' words, unequivocally reproduces a ring. Its angular diameter is 42.2 plus or minus 1.5 microarcseconds. With nominal scaling parameters, that corresponds to a black hole mass of 6.5 plus or minus 0.5 billion suns, viewed about 20 degrees from the approaching jet.

That is the clever bit. The ring did not come from the method wanting a ring. It came out of measurements that cannot be fooled by the calibration errors that muddied earlier arguments.

So the open question is how other teams, including those who saw no ring, will respond to a result that sidesteps their objections.

Key facts

  • Published in Nature on 11 November 2026 by Samuel Lai and colleagues.
  • The method uses closure invariants, which are unaffected by instrument calibration errors.
  • The recovered ring has an angular diameter of 42.2 ± 1.5 microarcseconds.
  • The implied black hole mass is 6.5 ± 0.5 billion solar masses, with nominal scaling parameters.

11,382. That is how many operations an AI-run lab carried out in roughly eight days to write and read a message in DNA. Spread out, that is about one every minute, day and night.

Who did all that work? Software agents steering robot arms. The humans came by now and then to restock supplies and empty the trash.

The lab is called AutoDNA, built by a team at Shanghai Jiao Tong University in China. A scientist describes a task in plain language. A team of agents, each powered by a large language model, works out the procedure and writes the code to run the equipment.

Why bother? Much of molecular biology is repetitive, and one tiny slip can wreck hours, days or even weeks of work. The long hours and sheer boredom take a toll too.

Here's the catch. Plenty of AI already helps science, but mostly on computers. James Zou at Stanford, who was not involved, notes that liquids, chemicals and biological materials bring new challenges.

So the team did not retrofit a lab made for people. They built one around AI from the start. An AI coordinator guides robotic arms that operate over 20 types of common lab equipment and fix small hiccups on their own.

Did it work? In one test, the system tuned a DNA copying method called PCR over several rounds and reached a yield of nearly 98 percent, similar to human experts. In another, it found a candidate mutation in KRAS, a cancer-driving gene, in a colorectal cancer sample, then built the short DNA strands needed to confirm it.

Then came the message test. All 78 DNA strands needed to encode the message were synthesized and decoded. Across the whole read-write cycle, which took around 200 hours, the precision was similar to expert scientists.

And the humans? When supplies ran low, the team wrote, "the system paused the experiment and alerted the technician for replenishment." The AI ran the experiment, and the person was the one it called when the shelves ran bare.

The 1956 invasion that left Britain and France needing America's permission

A war over a shipping shortcut ended with the canal shut and Britain and France shown they could not act without the US.

The Suez Crisis was a British, French and Israeli invasion of Egypt. Israel invaded on 29 October.

So why were Britain and France involved? Because of a canal. The Suez Canal opened in 1869 and gave ships the shortest ocean route between the Mediterranean Sea and the Indian Ocean.

Britain had bought in. In 1875 Egypt sold its shares to the British government, a 44-percent stake for 4 million pounds. By 1882 the UK had taken de facto control of the country and the canal.

Then came oil. Western Europe imported two million barrels a day from the Middle East, and 1,200,000 of them, or 60 percent, came by tanker through the canal. The pipelines were prone to instability, so British leaders preferred the sea route.

Here's the catch. In 1951 Egypt cancelled the treaty that let Britain keep its Suez base, and Britain refused to leave. In 1952 a military coup overthrew King Farouk, with Gamal Abdul Nasser among its leaders.

Now the result. Israel's four-month occupation of the Gaza Strip and Sinai Peninsula let it sail through the Straits of Tiran. Nasser's standing grew, and the British and French were humiliated on the world stage.

The deeper lesson was about power. The crisis showed that the UK and France could no longer run independent foreign policies without US consent. The UN set up an emergency force to police the Egypt-Israel border, and Canada's Lester B. Pearson won the Nobel Peace Prize for his diplomatic efforts.

One last fact. The canal the whole invasion revolved around stayed closed from October 1956 to March 1957.

  • Israel invaded Egypt on 29 October 1956 as part of a British-French-Israeli invasion.
  • The Suez Canal was closed from October 1956 to March 1957.
  • The crisis showed the UK and France could not act without US consent.

A chatbot can tell you the cup will fall without ever touching one

Chatbots and humans can predict the same outcome for a cup on a table edge, but they get there in very different ways.

Chatbots write poems, brainstorm and solve complex problems. Their designers drew on the human brain, building artificial neural networks inspired by how neurons work. So it is easy to assume they think like us.

They don't. Similar results do not necessarily mean similar processes.

Start with a cup at the edge of a counter. Reach too fast or grip too loosely and it smashes. For you this is routine, yet it leans on a quiet skill: guessing what happens next.

Here's the trick. One influential family of theories, called predictive processing, says the brain generates expectations, compares them with what comes in, and adjusts when they don't match.

Say you reach for an empty cup you believe is full. Your hand lifts with too much force, and the cup shoots up feeling almost weightless. That mismatch corrects your movement and updates your sense of its weight.

A chatbot predicts something else entirely: the next word or fragment of text. Give it "A hand bumps a cup near the edge of a table. The cup..." and it may finish with "tips over the edge and falls to the floor." It learned that from patterns in enormous amounts of data, without ever handling a cup.

Here's the catch. Describing a plausible outcome does not mean the chatbot has learned why it happens.

Enter world models, a family of AI approaches that try to predict how situations evolve and how actions change them. They can learn from videos, simulations or recordings of what robots see and do. A home robot might learn that gripping too tightly could break the cup.

Even so, matching our prediction tells us nothing about what knowledge sits underneath. To you, a cup can be graspable, breakable, yours, mine, too hot or almost falling.

Studies suggest infants only a few months old already have basic expectations about how objects behave. The prediction that a cup will fall is a small part of what you understand about it.

Key facts

  • Chatbots built on large language models learn to predict the next word or fragment of text.
  • Predictive processing theories say the brain compares expectations with incoming information and adjusts after mismatches.
  • World models aim to predict how situations evolve and can learn from videos, simulations or robot recordings.
  • Studies suggest infants a few months old already have basic expectations about physical objects.

Learn · Physics from first principles

Where physics runs out of answers

An open problem shows where an explanation stops working, or where a proposed answer still needs a test.

A solid object takes heat to warm up. That sounds simple enough. But what happens when a theory gives the wrong answer for how much heat it takes?

Physics builds explanations that can be checked against what happens. An open problem appears when an explanation cannot account for an observation, or when researchers cannot yet make the experiment needed to test an idea. The gap tells you what the theory still has to explain.

Here is a worked example. In 1900, Lord Kelvin named two problems. One concerned the lack of evidence for a proposed aether; the other concerned a theory that failed to predict the heat capacity of solids. Within a decade, special relativity resolved the first problem and quantum mechanics the second. Two problems led to two different answers.

Today's gaps include a deeper mismatch. The Standard Model and general relativity are two theories used in physics, but their mathematical frameworks do not fit together. Under extreme conditions, including those associated with the Big Bang and the centers of black holes beyond their event horizons, both break down.

There are other questions too: What is the absolute mass of neutrinos? What are dark matter and dark energy? Naming these questions does not answer them. It tells researchers exactly where an answer is missing.

That is the lasting idea of this course: a theory earns its place by explaining what we can observe, while its unanswered questions show where to look next. What experiment could help decide which new explanation is right?

Key facts

  • Open problems can be theoretical or experimental.
  • Kelvin identified two problems in 1900; special relativity and quantum mechanics resolved them within a decade.
  • The Standard Model's mathematical framework is inconsistent with general relativity.
  • The nature of dark matter and dark energy remains an open question.

The rules beneath the headlines

Sudden events can expose who holds power, who carries risk and whose account gets believed.

A housing protest became a nationwide work stoppage in Spain. That jump is striking because home and work can look like separate problems from a distance. For the person trying to keep both, they meet every day. A dispute can spread when enough people recognize the same pressure in different parts of their lives.

Then there is the question of who can afford a pause. In Ashulia, factory owners are weighing indefinite closures that would leave workers without wages. The decision sits with one group; the immediate loss of pay would fall on another. That gap helps explain why a fight over work can become a fight over the basic ability to get through the week.

Trust has its own weak points. Official and private air monitors told different stories on Delhi's Diwali night. A ruling on same-sex criminal laws exposed a colonial-era tie in Trinidad and Tobago's courts. In each case, understanding the event means asking who gets to establish the account or apply the rule, and how that authority came to be accepted.

News gives us the moment when something happens. The slower story is how people came to depend on a wage, a measurement or a rule, and what happens when it fails them. Those arrangements are easy to miss while they work quietly. When a headline brings one into view, what has been shaping people's choices all along?

Trump waives the two biggest teeth of a Russia sanctions law he signed weeks ago

Two days before a deadline, Trump waived the new law's tariffs on Russian goods and on buyers of Russian oil and gas, while keeping older sanctions.

Up to 500%. That is how much extra a new US law told the president to charge on Russian goods coming into the country. On a $100 item, that is up to $500 in duties.

Late Friday, Trump signed an order waiving that requirement. He wrote that he had sent Congress a certification that the waiver "is in the national interests of the United States."

Here's the backstory. The House passed the bill on Sept. 16, and Trump signed it two days later. Its primary backer was the late Lindsey Graham, a Republican senator from South Carolina, and the law carries his name.

The law demanded two big things within 30 days of enactment. One was those duties of up to 500% on Russian goods. The other was tariffs of up to 100% on countries among the top five buyers of Russian crude oil or gas, including China and India.

Trump's order waives both, two days before the deadline. Here's the catch: it keeps the existing sanctions on Russia from earlier executive orders. The law also allowed sanctions on Russian leaders, officials and financial institutions, and wider sanctions on Iran.

So why now? A week earlier, the administration temporarily waived sanctions on Russian diesel exports through April 2027, to ease a supply crunch caused by the war in Iran. High fuel prices are a sensitive issue for voters before the midterm elections on Nov. 3.

Reactions followed. Kirill Dmitriev, Putin's economy envoy, called it "an important and truly historic moment" and said US-Russia economic dialogue would continue.

Sen. Richard Blumenthal, a Connecticut Democrat who also drafted the law, condemned the waiver. He said Trump stands on the side of "a bloodthirsty thug."

Ukraine's president, Volodymyr Zelenskyy, spoke in Brussels and saw a pattern forming. "Sanctions will be lifted after gas," he said. "They will be lifted on something else." He named the banking system, certain individuals and the chemical industry.

Whether his list turns out to be a forecast or a fear is the question nobody can answer yet.

Key facts

  • Trump waived the Graham Act's duties of up to 500% on Russian goods and tariffs of up to 100% on top buyers of Russian oil and gas.
  • The order came two days before the law's deadline and keeps existing Russia sanctions.
  • The House passed the bill Sept. 16 and Trump signed it two days later.
  • A week earlier, Russian diesel export sanctions were waived through April 2027.

PM turns up unannounced at 13 Dhaka sites and sets cleanliness deadlines

On Saturday Prime Minister Tarique Rahman toured 13 Dhaka hospitals, schools and government quarters unannounced, set two cleanliness deadlines, and one doctor was suspended.

Associate Professor Dr Rafiqul Islam was smoking in his untidy room at Bangladesh Shishu Hospital and Institute when Prime Minister Tarique Rahman found him.

The prime minister immediately told the hospital chairman to take action against the doctor. The doctor was then suspended.

That was one stop out of 13 on Saturday, 17 October. According to his Deputy Press Secretary Hasan Shiplu, the tour ran from 9:10am to 1:41pm. That is about four and a half hours, or a little over 20 minutes a stop on average.

Here's the catch: these were surprise visits, meant to assess the quality of public services, cleanliness and overall management.

He started at Kuwait-Bangladesh Friendship Government Hospital in Uttara. From there he went on to schools and colleges, the National Heart Foundation at Mirpur, Shaheed Suhrawardy Medical College and Hospital, and government staff quarters.

At each place he looked at cleanliness, service quality and management, and spoke to officials. He also spoke to students and members of the public.

So what happens when he doesn't like what he sees? He sets a clock. At the Uttara hospital he gave the authorities seven days to improve the environment and cleanliness arrangements.

At Azimpur Government Officers' and Employees' Residential Quarters he gave two weeks. He expressed dissatisfaction with poor cleanliness and management at several locations.

Not every stop was a scolding. At Dhaka Residential Model College, his old college, he urged students to help build a clean Bangladesh and plant more trees.

He also visited a footpath at Mirpur-10 that has been cleared of hawkers, with Dhaka North City Corporation Administrator Shafiqul Islam Khan Milton present.

Count forward from Saturday and the hospital's week ends on 24 October, and the Azimpur quarters' two weeks end on 31 October. Those are the dates to watch, because a deadline only means something if someone comes back to check it.

Putin says his border zone inside Ukraine is not up for negotiation

Putin says Russia is building a 660 km "security zone" inside Ukraine along the border, and he will not put it into peace talks.

Vladimir Putin has now put a strip of Ukraine that Russia has never even claimed as its own beyond the reach of any peace deal. On October 16, the Russian president said his forces were building a "security zone" inside Ukraine along the border with Russia. He said he would not make it a subject of peace negotiations.

Why does that matter? Some quick background. Russia launched its full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022 and has claimed certain Ukrainian regions as its own. Sumy and Kharkiv, which sit right on the Russian border, are not among them. The zone would take in land in both.

So where did this come from? Putin points to August 2024, when Ukrainian troops crossed into Russia's Kursk region. He said Russian forces were given two jobs: push Ukraine's troops out of Russian territory, and set up a zone in the land next door. He said the second job was going successfully.

Here's what's new. Putin ordered a buffer zone like this back in May 2025. Until now, though, he had not said how big it would be, or that it was off the table in talks.

The Kremlin filled in the size. Spokesman Dmitry Peskov said the planned zone runs 660 km, or 410 miles, along the border, and that Russia already controls 70% of it. Do the arithmetic and that is about 460 km Russia says it holds, with roughly 200 km to go.

Here's the catch. That figure is length only. Peskov did not say how far the zone would reach into Ukraine, and a narrow strip and a deep one are very different things on the ground.

Think of a negotiation as a pile of things each side could trade. By declaring the zone non-negotiable, Putin is pulling a slice of Ukrainian land out of the pile before any deal is struck.

He spoke at a military command post, in uniform, in televised remarks. He also claimed Russia had yet to capture just over 10% of Ukraine's Donetsk region and 16.5% of Zaporizhzhia. None of these figures, including the 70%, has been independently verified.

The reports so far carry no Ukrainian response to the declaration and no timetable. The biggest blank is the simplest one: how deep into Sumy and Kharkiv does a zone Russia says it will not bargain over actually go? The Kremlin has not said.

Key facts

  • Putin said on October 16, 2026 that Russia is building a security zone inside Ukraine along the border and will not include it in peace talks.
  • Peskov said the planned zone runs 660 km (410 miles) along the border and Russia controls 70% of it.
  • The zone would cover land in Ukraine's Sumy and Kharkiv regions, which Russia has not claimed.
  • The Kremlin has not said how deep into Ukraine the zone would reach.

Anthropic reportedly offers Blackstone over 10% to help fund Google chips

Anthropic has reportedly offered Blackstone a yield above 10% to lend into a $60 billion package that pays for Google-designed AI chips, about 3 points more than similarly rated borrowers pay.

Why would the company behind the Claude AI assistant offer a lender more than 10% a year when borrowers with a similar credit rating pay about 7%?

According to the Financial Times, that's what Anthropic has offered the investment firm Blackstone. Reuters passed the report on October 16 and said it could not immediately verify it.

The money is for chips, and a lot of them. The package is called Project North Star. It totals $60 billion and would support about 1.5 gigawatts of Google-designed tensor processing units, or TPUs, the chips Google builds for AI work.

Here's the interesting part. The debt would be backed by Anthropic's lease payments for those chips over five years. Lenders get paid out of that stream of payments.

So why the high price? The $60 billion comes in layers. About $42 billion is senior debt, guaranteed by Broadcom, which works with Google on the chips. The other $18 billion is junior debt.

Think of a queue. If money ever runs short, senior lenders get paid first, and junior lenders wait behind them. Standing at the back of the line costs extra.

Blackstone's funds would take $9 billion, which is half of the junior slice, at a 10% coupon. Discounts and fees would push its total yield above that. A 10% coupon on $9 billion works out to $900 million a year.

The credit rating matters here too. In June, the rating agency KBRA put Anthropic one notch below investment grade, the line many cautious investors use to decide what they're allowed to buy. The new junior debt is expected to get a similar rating, and Anthropic wants to get above that line.

The price has also climbed. About four months earlier, Anthropic borrowed roughly $4.4 billion at 8.5% through a similar deal called Project Big Sky. The FT says North Star is expected to be the largest chip-financing deal to date.

The reports describe an offer, not a closed deal. Blackstone declined to comment, and Anthropic did not immediately respond.

The next thing to watch is the other $9 billion of junior debt, which is expected to go to other investors in the coming months, possibly at a lower yield. If buyers accept less, lenders are getting more comfortable with AI chip debt. If they don't, the 10% offer looks like the going rate.

Key facts

  • Project North Star is a proposed $60 billion package to fund about 1.5 gigawatts of Google-designed TPUs.
  • Blackstone funds would invest $9 billion in junior debt at a 10% coupon, with the total yield above 10%.
  • The debt is backed by Anthropic's chip lease payments over five years; about $42 billion of senior debt is guaranteed by Broadcom.
  • Similarly rated borrowers pay about 7%; Anthropic's earlier Project Big Sky deal paid 8.5%.

Egypt's leader signaled peace in 1970, then war came in 1973

The 1973 war began after peace signals went nowhere, and it ended up pushing both sides toward the same trade Egypt had floated earlier: land for recognition.

In December 1970, Egyptian President Anwar Sadat told The New York Times he was ready "to recognize the rights of Israel as an independent state." The price was a total Israeli withdrawal from the Sinai Peninsula.

Less than three years later, on 6 October 1973, Egypt and Syria attacked Israel. So what went wrong in between?

Start in 1967. In the Six-Day War, Israel captured Egypt's Sinai, the Gaza Strip, roughly half of Syria's Golan Heights and the West Bank. Days after, on 19 June, Israel's government voted to return the Sinai and the Golan in exchange for permanent peace.

Here's the catch. That decision was not made public, and it was not conveyed to any Arab state. Meanwhile, at a summit in Khartoum that September, eight Arab states passed the "three nos": no peace, no recognition, no negotiation with Israel.

Both sides, in other words, were talking past each other. Fighting continued on a limited scale and escalated into the War of Attrition, an attempt to wear Israel down over time.

Sadat tried again in February 1971, proposing that Israel pull back from the Suez Canal and Sinai. Egypt also accepted much of a similar plan from Swedish diplomat Gunnar Jarring. That was the first time an Arab government had publicly said it was ready to sign a peace agreement with Israel.

Then came the surprise. The 1973 attack landed on Yom Kippur, the Jewish holy day, which fell on the 10th day of Ramadan. Egyptian forces crossed the Suez Canal, and Syrian forces gained ground in the Golan.

Israel halted the Egyptians after three days and pushed most Syrians back. Later its forces exploited the failed Egyptian advance to breach the canal. A UN ceasefire on 22 October collapsed the next day, and a second one on 25 October ended the war.

The United States and Soviet Union both rushed supplies to their allies, which heightened tensions between them.

Here's the clever twist. The Arab world, humiliated in 1967, felt psychologically vindicated. Israel concluded its military dominance was no longer certain.

That shift fed the 1978 Camp David Accords. Israel returned the Sinai to Egypt, and the peace treaty made Egypt the first Arab country to recognize Israel. It was the same trade Sadat had floated in 1970.

Learn · Physics from first principles

Open problems: how physics keeps a list of what it cannot explain

The last lesson is about the gaps. Physicists write them down, and sometimes a gap closes fast.

Here is a strange habit of physicists: they write lists of what they cannot explain. Isaac Newton did it in his book Opticks, first published in 1704, with 31 questions about light and matter. Three centuries on, the habit has not stopped.

Start with something you already know from daily life: a theory is only as good as the things it can predict. When it fails to explain something we actually see, or when we cannot build a test for it, that failure is called an open problem. Some are theoretical, meaning our best theories cannot explain what we observe. Others are experimental, meaning nobody has worked out how to build the test.

Here is a case where a list paid off. In 1900, Lord Kelvin gave a speech naming two problems in classical physics, which he called "two clouds" over the 20th century. One was missing evidence for the luminiferous aether. The other was that the equipartition theorem failed to predict the heat capacity of solids, which is how much heat a solid soaks up as it warms.

Now the arithmetic. Two clouds, and both were gone within a decade. Special relativity dealt with the first and quantum mechanics with the second. Wait, what? Two small gaps in 1900 turned into two whole new branches of physics. That is the clever bit: the gap was not a flaw to patch, it was a doorway.

Not every list clears so fast. Barry Simon proposed 15 open problems on quantum operators in 1984, and by 2000 about 5 were solved, leaving about 10 after 16 years. His second list of 15, released in 2000, had at least 3 solved. The 10 millennium problems proposed at the Strings 2000 conference are tougher: none had been conclusively solved as of 2025.

Here's the catch for today's physics. The mathematics of the Standard Model, our current theory of particles, is inconsistent with general relativity, our theory of gravity. Both break down in extreme places like the Big Bang and the centers of black holes. Other open questions include the absolute mass of neutrinos, why there is more matter than antimatter, and what dark matter and dark energy are.

Vitaly Ginzburg, a Nobel laureate, kept such a list from 1971 to 2005, ending with 30 problems. He added three great ones: the arrow of time, how to interpret quantum mechanics, and whether life can arise from physics alone. So here is the question that closes this course and opens the next: which of today's clouds will turn out to be a doorway?

Key facts

  • In 1900 Lord Kelvin named two "clouds" over physics, and both were solved within a decade by special relativity and quantum mechanics.
  • The Standard Model is mathematically inconsistent with general relativity, and both break down at the Big Bang and inside black holes.
  • Barry Simon's 1984 list of 15 problems had about 5 solved by 2000.
  • None of the 10 Strings 2000 millennium problems had been conclusively solved as of 2025.

The rules keep bending, and the pipes stay put

What a day of headlines says about who sets the terms and what quietly holds everything up.

Read a whole day of news at once and the loudest stories turn out to be the least informative ones. The signal sits underneath, in a few slow patterns that keep showing up in different clothes.

Here's the news itself as a clue. Events arrive as single days: a waiver, a missile, a firing. We notice the fire and not the missing safety approval that came before it.

After a midterm drubbing, Trump makes Stephen Miller his chief of staff

Trump has handed the top staff job to Stephen Miller, an adviser widely seen as an architect of his mass deportation drive, days after his party's midterm loss.

Trump's Republican Party took a drubbing in the midterms this week. His answer on Friday was to promote a man widely seen as an architect of his most controversial policies.

That man is Stephen Miller, 41. On Monday he takes over as White House chief of staff from Susie Wiles, which means running the president's staff day to day.

On Truth Social, Trump called Miller "one of my most trusted advisors". So who is he, and how did he get here?

Long before Trump, Miller was a conservative activist. At Santa Monica High School he railed on local radio against what he described as liberal school policies. At Duke he led the Duke Conservative Union, then moved to Washington to work as an aide to Republican members of Congress and Senator Jeff Sessions.

He joined Trump's first campaign in January 2016. "I think he helped Trump find his voice," Steve Bannon told the AP in 2017.

Here's the catch. His influence is tied to some of the most fought-over policies of Trump's first term. A 2017 order blocked citizens of seven Muslim-majority countries from entering the US for 90 days. And the 2018 "Zero Tolerance" border policy, which Miller is credited with shaping, called for arresting nearly every adult crossing without papers.

Children could not be held in criminal detention with their parents, so families were separated. Trump later changed the policy by executive order after outrage.

Back in office in January 2025, Miller became deputy chief of staff for policy and homeland security adviser. In May 2025 he told Fox News the goal was a minimum of 3,000 ICE arrests every day. Do the sum and that is more than a million a year.

Democratic lawmakers have long accused Miller of white nationalist rhetoric. In 2019 the Southern Poverty Law Center released emails it said showed him supporting white nationalist websites.

Now watch January. Democrats take control of the House and the Senate then, and they are widely expected to launch investigations into Miller's role in the White House.

Key facts

  • Trump named Stephen Miller chief of staff on Friday, replacing Susie Wiles, who hands over on Monday.
  • The move follows the Republican midterm defeat; Democrats take the House and Senate in January.
  • Miller is widely seen as an architect of Trump's mass deportation campaign.
  • In May 2025 he said the goal was a minimum of 3,000 ICE arrests a day.

A three-nation defence pact is about to get its first boss

Pakistan, Saudi Arabia and Turkiye signed a pact to treat an attack on one as an attack on all, and its first secretary general is due in Riyadh soon.

Who runs an alliance built on the idea that an attack on one member is an attack on all? For now, a retired general who has not quite started the job.

His name is Nauman Mahmood, a retired lieutenant general. He was appointed on Sep 23 as the first secretary general of the Makkah Defence Alliance, which links Pakistan, Saudi Arabia and Turkiye. Pakistan's Foreign Office said on Friday he is expected to take up his duties in Riyadh soon.

Here is the alliance in one line. Under the Makkah Joint Defence Agreement, signed on Aug 7, the three countries agree to treat an armed attack on one as an attack on all. Think of three neighbours who promise that a break-in at one house counts as a break-in at all three.

The paperwork is nearly done. The Foreign Office said late last month that Pakistan and Saudi Arabia had finished ratifying the deal, each following its own constitutional and legal procedures. On Thursday, Turkiye's parliament approved a bill ratifying it, which paves the way for the pact to come into force.

So why the attention now? Saudi Arabia faces attacks by Yemen's Houthi rebels, including a deadly strike that closed Riyadh's international airport for two weeks last month.

In mid-October, the military's spokesperson, Lieutenant General Ahmed Sharif Chaudhry, said Pakistan is providing "whatever support is needed" to defend Saudi Arabia.

Here's the catch. A promise to defend each other can sound like a threat to someone else, and Chaudhry addressed that directly. He said all members are "absolutely clear" that the alliance "has no offensive intent; it is purely defensive".

Who decides when the three sit down together? Foreign Office spokesperson Sajjad Haider Khan said the members would jointly decide on convening a meeting of the alliance.

There is also an older layer. Islamabad already has the Strategic Mutual Defence Agreement with Riyadh, and the Makkah pact is the second one, with Turkiye added.

Watch for two things: Mahmood arriving in Riyadh, and the pact formally coming into force after Turkiye's vote. The Foreign Office has said only "soon" about the first.

Key facts

  • Nauman Mahmood was appointed on Sep 23 as the first secretary general of the Makkah Defence Alliance and is expected in Riyadh soon.
  • The Makkah Joint Defence Agreement, signed on Aug 7, treats an armed attack on one member as an attack on all.
  • Turkiye's parliament approved a bill ratifying the pact on Thursday.
  • Pakistan's military says the alliance is purely defensive.

Here's the catch: this is not a one-off. The UK Maritime Trade Operations Center, which tracks attacks on ships, counted more than 35 incidents in the Strait of Hormuz in October. That is the highest monthly number since the war began. At least five ships have been attacked so far in November.

What is Hormuz? It is the narrow waterway at the mouth of the Persian Gulf, and oil tankers have to pass through it to leave.

The Wall Street Journal reported that Iran is trying everything to stop that flow. Its report cites bomb-laden undersea drones, rocket-propelled grenades and crews attaching limpet mines to ships.

So why now? Oddly, shipping had been recovering. US Central Command said last month it had helped Gulf countries export 1.5 billion barrels of oil through Hormuz since the summer.

On Friday, Iran's Revolutionary Guards said two more tankers were on fire in the strait, according to state media. Iranian media also said air defenses downed a US drone over Qeshm Island, days after US strikes there.

A statement attributed to Iran's Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei warned the war would widen if the US resumes attacks. So the question hanging over the strait is whether this week's fires are the peak or the start.

Then the selling travelled. Markets open in different time zones, so a bad night in New York lands on Asia the next morning. Japan's Nikkei sank 6% and South Korea's Kospi tumbled more than 6.5%.

So what set it off? The Guardian ties it to the Democrats' decisive midterms win. Susannah Streeter of Wealth Club said the build-out of datacentres, the warehouses of computers that run AI, looks set to slow.

Here's the catch. That slowdown risks "shredding the spending spree underpinning the boom", she said, just as investors had become "super nervous" about what all that spending will earn back. Two worries, colliding.

Is the AI boom over, then? Mohit Kumar of Jefferies does not think so. He said he does not see Democrats giving up on this growth engine, since datacentres create jobs. Chris Beauchamp of IG warned there is a risk of much more selling to come.

Now zoom out. After all that, the Nasdaq is still up 6.35% since January, though more than 10% below its peak. The FTSE 100 has gained almost 7.5% this year.

How do you rescue a telescope when nothing you own can reach it? That is the puzzle NASA now has with Hubble.

Hubble has been in orbit since 1990, and it is slowly losing height because of atmospheric drag. It has gyroscopes to point at different parts of the sky, but no propulsion of its own. It cannot lift itself.

Lifting it used to be the shuttle's job. The last boost came in 2002, when Columbia visited, one of five shuttle missions to the telescope. The shuttles have since been retired, and NASA has no spacecraft that can do the task.

So how long is the clock? NASA estimates a 10 per cent chance of re-entry by September 2032, and 50 per cent by December 2033. Some parts are dense enough that they would probably survive the fall.

NASA has asked the commercial sector for ideas, due by 30 November. Raise Hubble to at least 600 kilometres, or bring it down safely into the ocean. A company would have to supply everything: the launch vehicle, the spacecraft and, if needed, the crew.

Here's the catch. NASA asked in 2022 too, reportedly got eight submissions, and went ahead with none. And this year a commercial mission to boost the Swift observatory, which was decaying in a similar way, failed because of a steering problem.

Still, scientists are pushing. Thomas Haworth, who sits on a panel advising NASA, expected talk of squeezing out the last science. Instead, he says, a boost was higher on the agenda. Planning a mission takes years, he says, so "You need to decide now."

Why the fuss? Stephen Wilkins says Hubble does high-resolution ultraviolet imaging better than anything available. NASA aims to launch an Ultraviolet Explorer in 2030, but with a tenth of the resolution. The Habitable Worlds Observatory, which could match Hubble, may launch in the 2040s.

That could mean a decade or more of lost science. Wilkins adds a second reason: attempting a repair keeps alive skills that future space work will need.

Watch 30 November. If any company turns in a plan NASA believes in, the decision Haworth says must come soon gets a real option behind it.

How one assassination in 1914 turned into a world war

A single shot in the Balkans set off a chain of old promises between empires, and within weeks much of Europe was at war.

In the decade after it ended, many people hoped World War I would be "the war to end all wars." The destruction and the death toll were immense.

It wasn't. The League of Nations was set up to keep the peace, but its failure to manage the instability that followed helped bring on World War II in 1939. And the first war had already killed 15 to 22 million people.

So how did it start? On 28 June 1914, a Bosnian Serb named Gavrilo Princip assassinated Franz Ferdinand, the heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne.

Here's the catch. Europe was already tied together by promises. Think of a group chat where everyone has sworn to back a friend: one message goes out, and suddenly everyone is in the argument.

Austria-Hungary blamed Serbia and declared war on 28 July, exactly one month after the shooting. Russia mobilised in Serbia's defence. In early August, Germany declared war on Russia and France, and the United Kingdom joined after Germany invaded Belgium.

Those ties were built over decades. Bismarck, who had helped create the German Empire, wanted to isolate France, so he arranged agreements among Germany, Austria-Hungary and Russia. After Wilhelm II pushed him out in 1890 and the Reinsurance Treaty with Russia was not renewed, France got an opening. It signed an alliance with Russia in 1894, then an Entente Cordiale with Britain in 1904.

Germany planned to beat France fast. Its advance was halted in September 1914, and the Western Front hardened into a near-continuous line of trenches. Even huge battles like Verdun, the Somme and Passchendaele failed to break it.

The fighting also spread. The Ottoman Empire joined the Central Powers in November 1914. That is the thread running to today's date in history: in 1917, British forces captured Gaza after the Ottoman garrison retreated.

The war ended with the Armistice of 11 November 1918. The war to end all wars had finished, and the failure of the peace-keeping body built afterward contributed to the next one.

Key facts

  • Gavrilo Princip assassinated Franz Ferdinand on 28 June 1914; Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia on 28 July.
  • Alliances pulled Russia, Germany, France and the United Kingdom in within weeks.
  • World War I caused 15 to 22 million deaths.
  • British forces captured Gaza in 1917 after the Ottoman garrison retreated.

Learn · Physics from first principles

The open problems in physics

Our best rules for gravity and matter still clash, leaving major mysteries wide open.

You might assume that physics has already sorted out how the physical world fits together. Every day, you drop keys and gravity pulls them down, while the solid floor stops them from falling through the floorboards. Behind that smooth routine sits an awkward truth: our best description of gravity and our best description of matter do not agree with each other.

Unsolved problems in physics fall into two camps. Some are experimental, where we struggle to build tests sensitive enough to investigate phenomena like dark matter or the absolute mass of neutrinos. Others are theoretical, where the mathematics inside our equations breaks down entirely.

That mathematical breakdown happens because general relativity handles gravity across space, while the Standard Model governs particles. Think of a group chat where two people send messages using incompatible codes: each person sounds sensible alone, but the chat cannot combine their sentences into a single meaning. Under extreme conditions, like the Big Bang or the center of a black hole beyond its event horizon, both rulebooks collapse into mathematical nonsense.

So how fast do physicists actually solve these puzzles? Consider a concrete scorecard from 1984, when mathematical physicist Barry Simon wrote down 15 open problems about quantum operators. By the year 2000, researchers had solved about 5 of them, meaning 10 remained open, which shows that solving just one third of the list took 16 years.

Earlier generations had faster luck: in 1900, Lord Kelvin named just two problem clouds hanging over physics, and both were solved within a decade by special relativity and quantum mechanics. Today, the deepest questions take far longer. When physicists at the Strings 2000 conference proposed 10 millennium problems in theoretical physics, not a single one had been conclusively solved 25 years later in 2025.

That leaves our understanding of the universe split between two rules that work in daily life but fail together at the edges of reality. That brings us to the question our next lesson answers: what kind of new framework could finally unite gravity and matter into one?

Key facts

  • The Standard Model is mathematically inconsistent with general relativity, causing both theories to break down at singularities like the Big Bang and black holes.
  • In 1984, Barry Simon listed 15 open problems on quantum operators, and about 5 were solved by 2000.
  • None of the 10 millennium problems in theoretical physics proposed at the Strings 2000 conference have been conclusively solved as of 2025.

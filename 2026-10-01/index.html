Swiss glaciers lose an estimated 5.5% of their ice in 2026

An estimated 5.5% of Switzerland's remaining glacier ice disappeared in 2026 after early heatwaves accelerated summer melting.

Swiss glaciers lost an estimated 5.5% of their remaining ice volume in 2026, according to a report by Glacier Monitoring Switzerland. Until recently, glaciologists in the Alps viewed a 2% loss in a single year as extreme. More than 5% of the country's glacier ice disappeared over the past 12 months after intense summer heatwaves. Dr Matthias Huss, the director of Glacier Monitoring Switzerland, described the loss as "really just disastrous."

The melt began after a mild winter left peaks with less snow cover than usual. Heatwaves in May and June stripped that layer away, which exposed the darker glacier ice beneath. Bare ice reflects less sunlight and absorbs more solar energy. At measurement sites 3,500 metres above sea level, researchers found that no snow survived the end of summer. At some glaciers, ice levels dropped by four metres at the highest points, where several metres of winter snow normally lie.

"Ten years ago, I wouldn't have thought that it's possible to lose glaciers so quickly," said Dr Fabien Maussion, a glaciologist at the University of Bristol. At high elevations, snow compacts into ice over time to replenish what melts lower down the mountain. Without summer snow, glaciers lose mass continuously. Some smaller glaciers, such as Plattalva in eastern Switzerland, have already disappeared.

Between July and September, Swiss glaciers released an estimated 2.2 trillion litres of meltwater. Runoff temporarily cushioned water supplies for irrigation and hydropower. Yet the frozen supply diminished rapidly. The River Rhine reached record lows in places during the summer, which disrupted shipping routes and raised transport costs.

Dr Huss noted that runoff during heatwaves consists of water stored decades or centuries ago. Because that supply fails to regenerate without new snow, smaller glaciers will produce less meltwater during future dry spells. Dr Huss stated that sharp reductions in carbon emissions can still preserve ice in the Himalayas and polar regions, where most of the world's ice resides.

Key facts

  • Swiss glaciers lost an estimated 5.5% of their ice volume in 2026.
  • Glaciers released 2.2 trillion litres of meltwater between July and September 2026.
  • Measurement sites at 3,500 metres above sea level recorded no snow by the end of summer.
  • Dr Matthias Huss, director of Glacier Monitoring Switzerland, reported up to four metres of ice loss at the highest elevations of some glaciers.

Bangladesh remittances fell to 2.77 billion dollars in September

Inflows reached $2.77 billion in September, the lowest monthly total in 11 months, according to Bangladesh Bank data.

Read

Expatriates sent home $2.77 billion to Bangladesh in September, according to updated Bangladesh Bank data released on Thursday. The monthly total is the lowest in 11 months, and it is 3% higher than the $2.69 billion received in September last year.

The September total slowed from the pace recorded earlier in the year. Remittances exceeded $3 billion a month for six consecutive months through May. Receipts fell below that level in June to $2.82 billion. Inflows were $2.86 billion in July and $2.97 billion in August.

Total inflows were higher across the first quarter of FY27. Bangladesh received $8.59 billion from July to September. That is an increase of 13.3% from the $7.59 billion recorded during the same period a year earlier.

In the previous fiscal year, remittances reached a record $35.59 billion. The country received $30.33 billion in FY25.

The highest monthly inflow in the record run came in March, when expatriates sent $3.75 billion. May followed with $3.44 billion, and December recorded $3.22 billion.

Key facts

  • Expatriates sent $2.77 billion to Bangladesh in September, an 11-month low.
  • First-quarter remittances for FY27 rose 13.3% to $8.59 billion from $7.59 billion a year earlier.
  • Bangladesh Bank released the updated figures on Thursday.

Middle East crude exports return to pre-war volumes through pipelines and transfers

Crude shipments reached 16.5m barrels a day in September, though supplies of refined fuels such as diesel remain scarce.

Crude oil exports from the Middle East returned to pre-war volumes in September as producers rerouted shipments around Iranian disruption in the strait of Hormuz. At least 16.5m barrels per day left the region last month, according to data from Kpler, a commodity intelligence firm. The figure equals the pre-war average, excluding Iran, and is 10.5m barrels per day higher than the average for March.

Exporters have used pipelines and offshore tanker transfers since the conflict began on 28 February with US and Israeli strikes on Iran. Pipelines across Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates now transport about 40% of the region's crude, compared with 17% before the war. In late September, Saudi Arabia restarted its east-west pipeline after drone attacks. The line enabled exports to resume from the Red Sea port of Yanbu.

Tankers that enter the strait of Hormuz follow altered routines. Most crude transported through the waterway travels on shuttle vessels that sail with satellite transponders turned off. Crews transfer the oil to different tankers in open water off the coasts of Oman and Fujairah. In August, more than 70% of crude that moved through the strait changed ships. Almost no Gulf crude changed tankers in the Gulf of Oman before the war.

Supplies of refined fuels remain constrained. Kpler recorded a seven-day average of 677,000 barrels per day of refined products leaving the strait as of Monday, down from 3.6m barrels per day before the war. That shortfall has lifted fuel costs. The average diesel price in the UK reached a record 199.18p a litre on Monday, and forecourts in some areas reported prices above £2.

Physical attacks continue along the waterway. On Tuesday, projectiles struck three Liberian-flagged tankers in the strait of Hormuz. Richard Meade, the editor in chief of Lloyd's List, stated that oil flows recovered because market participants accepted higher costs and operational complexity, while the underlying threat remains. Crude prices rose again on Thursday.

Key facts

  • Middle East crude exports reached at least 16.5m barrels per day in September, equalling the pre-war average excluding Iran.
  • Pipelines through Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates carry about 40% of regional crude, up from 17% before the war.
  • Three Liberian-flagged tankers were hit by projectiles in the strait of Hormuz on Tuesday.

A quarter of mathematics preprints on arXiv acknowledge artificial intelligence

Preprint data shows artificial intelligence citations rose from 1 per cent to 25 per cent in one year.

Read

Twenty-five per cent of mathematical papers published on the arXiv preprint server in August acknowledged some use of artificial intelligence, according to an analysis of the repository. The proportion is up from 1 per cent recorded the year before. Proofs can be verified through the Lean programming language, which breaks arguments down into elemental logical statements checked mechanically by a computer.

Terence Tao, a mathematician at the University of California, Los Angeles, warned in a social media post that solving problems with artificial intelligence is akin to using up a non-renewable resource. Tao said that identifying problems that lead to new techniques is a key part of research. "The indiscriminate use of powerful solution-extraction tools can achieve the immediate short-term goal of solving problems at hand, but at the cost of sustaining the ecosystem for the next wave of progress, or in understanding the progress already obtained," Tao said. He proposed ringfencing specific classes of problems for full analysis and understanding.

Alessandro Della Corte at the University of Camerino in Italy has organized an online declaration for researchers who choose to eschew artificial intelligence in their work. "I don't think it's ideal if all mathematicians on the planet use the same technology as an inescapable interface between themselves and their discipline," Della Corte said. He proposed that a minority of 5 to 10 per cent of researchers remain free of the software. Della Corte added that he will use results produced by artificial intelligence if a human author takes responsibility in an understandable paper.

Daniel Litt at the University of Toronto stated that the profession is entering a new era. "I think we are at the beginning of an incredible, wonderful explosion of mathematics, and if we value human understanding, there will be more need for human mathematicians than ever before," Litt wrote. Future mathematical work will center on understanding and communication.

Key facts

  • 25 per cent of mathematics papers on arXiv in August acknowledged artificial intelligence, up from 1 per cent the previous year.
  • Terence Tao at the University of California, Los Angeles, compared artificial intelligence problem-solving to depleting a non-renewable resource.
  • Alessandro Della Corte at the University of Camerino proposed that 5 to 10 per cent of mathematicians remain free of artificial intelligence.

SpaceX launches four astronauts to space station on eight-hour flight

Jessica Watkins leads the Crew-13 mission from Florida on a six-month expedition to the orbital laboratory.

Four astronauts launched from Florida on Thursday on a six-month mission to the International Space Station. A SpaceX Falcon 9 rocket and Dragon capsule lifted off from Cape Canaveral space force station in the late morning after clouds cleared. Commander Jessica Watkins leads the crew. She is the first Black woman to lead a crew to orbit.

The flight follows a three-week delay to repair the capsule's leaking fuel system. The transit to the space station is scheduled to take eight hours, the fastest flight to date for SpaceX. "Thanks for an amazing ride," Watkins radioed after reaching orbit. The four astronauts will reach the station well before bedtime.

Watkins is a geologist who worked on Nasa's Mars rover Curiosity a decade ago, and she completed an earlier stay at the space station in 2022. Her three crewmates are on their first spaceflights. Luke Delaney is a retired marine, and Joshua Kutryk is a colonel in the Royal Canadian air force. Sergey Teteryatnikov is a former Russian submariner. They will replace four astronauts who have lived aboard the station since February.

The launch is the 13th crewed flight SpaceX has conducted for Nasa, excluding a test flight in 2020. The astronauts named the flight Crew-13 to honor Apollo 13, the harrowing 1970 lunar mission that ended with the crew's safe return. Ahead of liftoff, the crew traveled to the launch pad in black Teslas with license plates that read "Lucky 13". "We really wanted to honor their legacy," Watkins said.

Kutryk will become the fourth man to become a father while orbiting Earth. His wife, Heather, is due to give birth to their third child in late November. The Canadian Space Agency has promised support for the family, and Nasa plans to keep Kutryk connected to his wife during the mission. In 2004, the agency set up a radio link for astronaut Mike Fincke during his daughter's birth.

Key facts

  • Jessica Watkins is the first Black woman to lead a crew to orbit.
  • The mission is the 13th crew SpaceX has launched for Nasa.
  • The transit to the space station is scheduled to take eight hours.

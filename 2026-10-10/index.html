Navi Pillay wins Nobel Peace Prize as US sanctions court

The South African judge receives the award for a long career in international law despite pushback from the US and Israel.

Picture a grand stone building in Nuremberg, Germany. South African judge Navi Pillay is 85 years old. She sits on the International Court of Justice. On Friday, the Norwegian Nobel Committee awarded her the 2026 Nobel Peace Prize. She learned the news while inside the German courthouse.

The committee chair is Jørgen Watne Frydnes. He said Pillay received the prize for promoting peace and international law. Pillay grew up in Durban, South Africa. She defended activists during apartheid. She later became the first non-white woman on her country's high court. She spent four years as president of the UN tribunal for the 1994 genocide in Rwanda. She now hears the case where Myanmar is accused of genocide against the Rohingya people. Many Rohingya refugees live in camps in Bangladesh.

Tension surrounds the award. Pillay chaired a UN panel from 2021 to 2025. The panel investigated the Palestinian territories. The group concluded that Israel committed genocidal acts in Gaza. It also found that Hamas committed war crimes. Israel rejected the findings and called the Nobel award a weapon to legitimise prejudice.

US President Donald Trump reposted a statement on social media. The statement called Pillay a hater of America and Israel. The US government then announced sweeping sanctions on the International Criminal Court. Sanctions work like financial walls. A government blocks a foreign group from using bank accounts to cut off their money. The US said it wanted to restrict the court's resources.

Pillay joked that she might share the prize with Trump if the crowd applauded more. She dedicated the award to survivors of international crimes. This dispute exposes the fragile system of global justice. It asks if international courts can judge powerful states, or if powerful states will simply defund the courts.

Key facts

  • Navi Pillay is 85 years old.
  • Pillay was told of the award at the Palace of Justice in Nuremberg, Germany.
  • Pillay was the first non-white woman appointed to South Africa's high court.
  • The US announced sweeping sanctions on the International Criminal Court following the award.

Bangladesh asks India to lift land port export restrictions

Dhaka wants anti-dumping duties removed and trade privileges kept after it leaves the least developed country group.

Imagine a line of freight trucks idling at a border crossing between Bangladesh and India, waiting for clearance on a dusty access road. Ayesha Akhter, an additional secretary at the commerce ministry, led a 10-member delegation to New Delhi. They met Indian officials for the two-day 16th meeting of the Joint Working Group on Trade.

Bangladesh asked India to lift restrictions on Bangladeshi exports at Indian land ports. According to an official release in Dhaka, the delegation requested the removal of anti-dumping duties on Bangladeshi jute and PET film. Anti-dumping duties are extra taxes a government puts on foreign goods that it believes are priced below fair market value. They act like a penalty fee to protect local manufacturers.

Dhaka wants to keep its trade privileges under the South Asian Free Trade Area agreement after it graduates from the least developed country category. To help goods enter the Indian market, Bangladesh asked India to simplify certification rules under the Bureau of Indian Standards. The delegation requested better infrastructure at Indian customs stations, asking for better access roads and warehouses.

Bangladesh pushed for greater access for its freight trucks travelling through Indian territory to reach Nepal and Bhutan. India responded with its own requests. New Delhi proposed removing limits on the export of cotton yarn to Bangladesh through specific land ports. Indian officials asked Dhaka to ease barriers affecting exports from northeastern Indian states.

The two sides discussed improving the Akhaura-Agartala trade route and resolving issues at border haats. They proposed starting a forum for chief executives to connect businesses directly. The talks expose a regional trade system where poor physical infrastructure and rigid customs rules slow the movement of goods.

Partial nuclear test ban treaty goes into effect in 1963

The 1963 agreement stopped atmospheric and underwater nuclear explosions to reduce global radioactive fallout.

Picture Bikini Atoll, a coral reef in the Pacific Ocean, where a sudden flash turns the sky white and drops radioactive ash on nearby fishing boats. This happened in 1954 when the United States, a major military power, detonated the Castle Bravo device. The explosion yielded 15 megatons of TNT. It spread radioactive particles over more than 11,000 square kilometers and sickened Japanese fishermen. A Soviet test sent similar particles over Japan the same year.

Public anxiety grew over this radioactive fallout and the power of new hydrogen bombs. The United States and the Soviet Union, the two main nuclear powers, met in Moscow along with Britain. They signed the Partial Test Ban Treaty on 5 August 1963. The agreement went into effect on 10 October 1963.

The treaty banned test detonations of nuclear weapons in the atmosphere and under water. It also prohibited tests in outer space. A treaty is a formal contract between governments. This contract allowed underground tests. Negotiators abandoned a complete ban because they disagreed on how to detect hidden explosions and verify compliance.

Between 1951 and 1958, the US conducted 166 atmospheric tests. The Soviet Union carried out 82, and Britain conducted 21. Following the new rules, the concentration of radioactive particles in the air dropped substantially. France and China did not sign the agreement. They continued atmospheric testing until 1974 and 1980.

Over time, 123 other states joined the treaty. The shift to underground detonations removed the visual terror of mushroom clouds from the sky. Countries simply continued to build weapons out of sight. How does a global system control the spread of arms when the testing happens entirely out of view?

Learn · Physics from first principles

Physics from first principles, lesson 9: thermodynamics and entropy

The laws governing heat and energy apply directly to the behavior of black holes.

A hot cup of tea cools down until it matches the room. This everyday observation is the basis of thermodynamics, the study of heat. In physics, black hole thermodynamics is a set of relationships between the properties of black holes. These relationships are a direct match to the classical laws of heat. The connection begins by replacing entropy with the area of the black hole horizon. It continues by replacing temperature with surface gravity.

James Bardeen and Stephen Hawking, physicists studying general relativity, proposed the laws of black hole mechanics in 1973. For a stationary black hole, the surface gravity is constant on the event horizon. This is the zeroth law of thermodynamics, where temperature becomes equal across a system at rest. Without quantum effects, the temperature of a black hole must be exactly 0. The third law states that a black hole with a surface gravity of 0 cannot be formed.

Because a black hole has a temperature, it must emit radiation. This emitted energy is Hawking radiation. The area of the horizon is a non-decreasing function of time. In one concrete example, scientists analyzed gravitational waves from the event GW250114. The data confirmed that the surface area of a black hole cannot decrease. This rule is the second law of thermodynamics for black holes.

Gerard 't Hooft and Leonard Susskind, physicists working in the early 1990s, generalized this relationship between surface area and entropy. They applied the rule to all of spacetime to form the holographic principle. The principle states that the area surrounding any volume in spacetime limits the information content of that volume. The number of degrees of freedom in any space has a strict boundary.

Until 1995, no person was able to calculate black hole entropy based on statistical mechanics. Earlier theorems suggested that black holes could have only a single quantum state. Then, Andrew Strominger and Cumrun Vafa, researchers working in string theory, calculated the entropy of a specific black hole. Their result agreed with established formulas. For a standard Schwarzschild black hole, the relationship between large-scale properties and quantum states is unknown. How will string theory solve this remaining problem?

Key facts

  • James Bardeen and Stephen Hawking proposed the laws of black hole mechanics in 1973.
  • Gravitational waves from event GW250114 confirmed that a black hole's surface area cannot decrease.
  • Andrew Strominger and Cumrun Vafa calculated black hole entropy using string theory in 1995.

The Day, Connected

The slow processes beneath the noise

A look at the hidden patterns shaping the world beyond today's headlines.

We spend our days watching loud events: the sweeping sanctions and the sudden firings. The news often captures only the immediate surface of an event. Underneath, a different world takes shape through slow, quiet processes.

One deep pattern is how established rules break down, often driven by the very governments who wrote them. We see this when the US sanctions the International Criminal Court to stop the prosecution of Americans, or when a president suspends Russian sanctions to lower domestic fuel prices before an election. The mechanisms that once held the line are traded away for short-term wins.

Another pattern is the hidden fragility in our new tools. We build massive systems without knowing the final cost, and the reality starts to show the friction. We see it when AI coding agents produce so much software that human reviewers become a severe bottleneck, and when a shortage of memory chips for AI data centers drives up the price of cheap smartphones. The efficiency we chased created new, heavy burdens.

These slow processes are much harder to spot than a street protest or a downed passenger plane. They move completely beneath the surface. They quietly change the borders of daily life and alter how we live long before any alarms sound. When you read a headline today, ask: what slow, quiet process is driving this loud event?

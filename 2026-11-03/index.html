Iran offers to reopen Hormuz in two days, if the US acts first

Qatar is trying to shrink a seven-day deal to two days, but neither side trusts the other.

A Qatari gas tanker bound for Pakistan was given direct clearance and safe-passage approval by Tehran. Hours later, on Friday, unidentified missiles hit it near the strait of Hormuz.

That is the backdrop in Doha, where indirect talks ran into a third day on Sunday. Indirect means the US and Iran are not across the table from each other. Qatar carries the messages, and Iran's foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, says there are no plans for direct talks.

So what is being haggled over? Qatar is trying to negotiate the release of more than $6bn in Iranian assets. That could lead to the strait reopening and nuclear talks starting again.

Here's the catch. Neither side trusts the other. So Qatar wants to squeeze the order of events: the US lifts sanctions, Iran reopens the strait, then nuclear talks begin. Iran has agreed to cut that timetable from seven days to two.

Araghchi spelled out the price to Al Jazeera. Iran would open the strait within two days of the US lifting its blockade on oil ships, ending its block on Iranian assets, and ending sanctions on Iranian oil exports.

The mixed signals keep coming. Secretary of state Marco Rubio said the US is open to a diplomatic solution. Iran's security chief, Mohsen Rezaei, said he got a letter from vice-president JD Vance seeking to revive talks, noted a more positive "tone", and has not replied.

Donald Trump said the talks were "going fine but you know they're not very reliable". He also said the US still had to "take care of" one or two targets.

Now the awkward bit. The plan would restart nuclear talks, yet Iran's deputy parliament speaker, Ali Nikzad, said Iran will not negotiate on the nuclear issue "under any circumstances".

The clock matters too. The talks are running up against the US midterm elections on Tuesday. The UAE envoy, Anwar Gargash, said Gulf states are "braced for the worst" and do not want a neighbour like Iran caught up in a prolonged crisis.

Washington has not yet answered Iran's proposal. Until it does, nobody can say whether two days is short enough for anyone to take the first step.

Key facts

  • Indirect talks in Doha ran into a third day on Sunday, with Qatar trying to free more than $6bn in Iranian assets.
  • Iran agreed to cut the timetable for lifting sanctions, reopening the strait and starting nuclear talks from seven days to two.
  • Unidentified missiles hit a Qatari LNG tanker near the strait on Friday, hours after Tehran approved its passage.
  • Gulf officials are waiting for Washington's response and say they are braced for the worst.

Wage protests halt production at 690 garment factories

Production stopped at at least 690 garment factories employing more than 17 lakh workers on 2 November as wage protests kept workers away.

Production stopped at at least 690 garment factories on Monday, 2 November, and more than 17 lakh workers are employed at them. That puts the country's biggest export industry on pause across almost all of its main hubs on the same day.

Why? The two manufacturers' associations, BGMEA and BKMEA, gave three reasons: workers protesting for higher wages, workers refusing to return to work, and fears of possible attacks.

Here's the detail that shows how it actually happened. BGMEA vice-president Mijanur Rahman told The Business Standard that 670 member factories suspended production because workers had not come back by 11:30am. The workers emptied the floors first, and the owners' decision came after.

So where did it hit? Pretty much everywhere clothes get sewn. Gazipur and Mymensingh alone had 382 closed units employing around 9 lakh workers, which is more than half the factories and more than half the workers in the whole shutdown.

Savar's Ashulia and Zirabo areas lost 223 factories. Mirpur lost 65.

Knitwear was hit too. BKMEA reported around 20 factories shut in Narayanganj, affecting more than 50,000 workers. Its executive president, Fazlee Shamim Ehsan, said owners "were forced to suspend production at relatively large factories" there.

Do the maths and you can see what "relatively large" means: 50,000 workers across about 20 factories works out to roughly 2,500 people per factory. Workers from several factories also blocked the Dhaka-Narayanganj Link Road.

One wrinkle: the totals don't quite match. One report gives 679 factories and another gives 690. The gap may come down to different cut-off times, or to BGMEA and BKMEA members being counted separately.

Other outlets' headlines go further. Reuters and The Economic Times say the protests turned violent, Al Jazeera calls it the third day, one headline says a garment wage board has been formed, and another says factories were reported reopening. None of those details could be checked against full reports, so treat them as unconfirmed.

That leaves the biggest question unanswered in public: what wage are workers actually asking for, and what would bring 17 lakh of them back to the lines?

  • More than 17 lakh workers are employed at the affected factories
  • Gazipur and Mymensingh had 382 closed units with around 9 lakh workers
  • BGMEA says 670 member factories stopped because workers had not returned by 11:30am

Repeated bombs hit a Ukrainian aid center, killing at least 26

Russian glide bombs hit a school used as an aid hub in Komyshuvakha, killing at least 26 people, officials say, in a second strike aimed at the shelter.

According to Ukraine's president, people still living in the village of Komyshuvakha went to the same place every week to collect humanitarian aid. On Monday, that place was bombed.

The place was a local school that served as an aid hub. At least 26 people were killed and five more were injured, officials say.

The village sits in the Zaporizhzhia region of southern Ukraine, about 35 kilometers east of Zaporizhzhia city and a dozen kilometers from the frontline. Russian planes reportedly dropped more than 10 glide bombs. That is an ordinary bomb fitted so it can glide toward its target after release.

Here's the part that matters. President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said one bomb came first, then nine more hit the shelter where people were taking cover.

That pattern has a name: a double-tap. A location is hit, then hit again, aiming to catch the first responders who arrive. Ukrainian emergency services said two members of a rescue crew were among the dead.

Zelenskyy called the attacks "utterly horrific" and said Russia could not have been unaware that residents received aid there weekly. The material I have includes no Russian comment on this strike.

So why does the timing stand out? The strikes came less than 24 hours after a six-day local humanitarian ceasefire around Oleshky, in neighboring Kherson, had expired.

Zelenskyy said that truce could have led to further steps toward a deal. After Monday, he said Russia had sent "a completely opposite signal." Ukraine's General Staff said Russia used the pause to rearm and rotate troops. The Kremlin called that "normal practice" and said Ukraine could have regrouped too.

Meanwhile, US envoy Steve Witkoff said US, Russian and Ukrainian officials will hold working-level talks in Abu Dhabi on November 10 and 11. He said humanitarian efforts are on the agenda. Watch whether anything about protecting places like this one comes out of that room.

Stocks rose a year after all 11 midterms since 1982. Will they again?

Markets are nervous before Tuesday's midterms, but history says the election itself rarely hurts stocks; the Fed, earnings and AI matter more.

Eleven midterm elections, from 1982 through 2022. After every single one, the S&P 500 was higher a year later.

That's odd, because markets are sweating ahead of Tuesday's vote. The S&P 500 is an index of big US companies, and CNBC looked at how it behaved after elections from 1980 through 2024.

A year after midterms, it had gained 13.7% on average. Across all elections, returns a year later were positive 91% of the time. Put $100 in, and the average midterm gave you about $114 back.

Here's the catch. The short term is shakier: one day after, the average gain was just 0.2%. Ryan Detrick of Carson Group put it this way: "Once you are through it, it's a big relief and markets tend to do pretty well."

So which result do markets like? Divided government, with one party holding the White House and the other holding at least one house of Congress. After 15 elections with no single party in charge, the index averaged 15% a year later and rose in 14 of them. After single-party control, it averaged 11.5%.

Detrick's explanation: with a huge red or blue wave, "you have too much spending one way and too much power."

Is the election doing any of this? Probably less than it looks like. After the 2000 election the S&P 500 fell 22.1% because of the dotcom bust. After 2020 it rose 38.3% as markets rebounded from the COVID selloff.

Société Générale says Federal Reserve policy and corporate earnings matter more. In 2018, the Fed raised rates four times and the index ended the year down 6.2%, even though it was up 11.7% a year after that election.

So what is the swing factor now? AI. Detrick says that as long as the AI wave keeps moving and the economy keeps growing, the bull market is probably still alive. But a Democratic sweep would almost certainly pressure the AI infrastructure buildout.

Bonds are skittish too, with yields rising globally and a mini debt crisis in France. The war with Iran could also worsen. Deutsche Bank's Jim Reid wrote that "seasonality isn't destiny," and those worries won't vanish by themselves.

Watch Tuesday's results. History favors a split Washington; a Democratic sweep would almost certainly pressure the AI buildout.

Key facts

  • The S&P 500 was up a year after all 11 midterms from 1982 through 2022.
  • After 15 elections with divided government, the index averaged a 15% gain a year later, versus 11.5% for single-party control.
  • Société Générale says Fed policy and earnings matter more than elections.
  • A Democratic sweep would almost certainly pressure the AI infrastructure buildout.

Oil and falling house prices are squeezing Australia's economy at once

Australia's central bank is expected to raise rates again on Tuesday, and bank economists say the slowdown will be deeper and last longer.

In early July, a barrel of Brent crude, the global oil benchmark, cost about US$72. In recent days it has been back above US$110, which is more than half as much again.

That jump is a big reason Australia's major banks now expect a "sharper, longer slowdown". The Reserve Bank, the country's central bank, is widely expected to raise interest rates on Tuesday for the fifth time, taking the cash rate to its highest since 2008.

Wait, why raise rates when the economy is already weakening? Because prices are still climbing. Higher rates make borrowing dearer, people spend less, and price pressure eases. The medicine works by slowing things down on purpose.

Belinda Allen, head of Australian economics at CBA, now sees real GDP growth falling to just 1% by the middle of next year, down from her earlier 1.8%. She had expected "signs of economic recovery" by the New Year. Now she sees the economy "bottoming deeper and later".

Her main reason is oil. "Most people thought we would see a resolution to the Middle East conflict by now," she said. "Alas, that has not been the case."

Here's the catch. Oil has company. AMP's chief economist Shane Oliver says home prices could fall as much as 15% before levelling off late next year, and Sydney is already down 10% from its peak.

Why would that reach beyond owners? Oliver points to the "wealth effect": worried homeowners pull back on spending. Weaker housing also hurts homebuilding.

Then there are jobs. NAB's Gareth Spence says unemployment may need to rise from 4.7% to as high as 5.4% by the end of next year to get inflation under control. CBA sees a peak of 5.1%. Either way, the plan to beat inflation without losing post-Covid job gains looks unlikely to work.

A hair-thin probe is now listening to single brain cells in people

Neuropixels probes record hundreds of neurons at once, and after years in animals, they are now being tried briefly in human brains.

In a bright surgical theatre in central London, neurosurgeon Ahmed Toma bores a small hole into a woman's skull. She is there for a routine fix: a shunt to drain excess fluid from inside her head.

But she has also agreed to something extra. Her surgeons briefly place a probe in her brain, one about a centimetre long and thinner than a human hair.

It is called Neuropixels, and it is built to record the electrical activity of hundreds of neurons at once.

So what do they hear? Mihaly Kollo, a research scientist at the Francis Crick Institute, plays repeating words such as 'blind spot', 'parking spot' and 'piggy bank'. He wants to see how the brain handles the jump from one sound to the next. As the words ring out, some make waves sweep across the probe's output. That is a clear sign it is picking up activity.

Why is this a big deal? Because people have mostly been studied in two blunt ways. Devices that sit on the brain's surface measure the activity of whole populations. The Utah array, a tiny bed of needles, reaches single cells but has just 96 recording channels.

Think of it the way neurosurgeon Pierre Bourdillon does. The old way is one microphone at a busy intersection, trying to pick out conversations. Neuropixels is a microphone clipped to each speaker's collar.

Here's the catch. Other single-cell methods, like calcium imaging, can be too slow for some neurons, or need genetically encoded sensors. That rules them out in humans.

Animals got there first. Neuropixels debuted in 2017, and Bourdillon says the revolution began in animals 10 to 15 years ago. Now, he says, the first teams are using it in humans.

Back in London, neuroscientist Matteo Carandini is pushing the technology in mice. His team records tens of thousands of neurons, sometimes for months. Their newest version, Opto 1, adds optogenetics, which switches neurons on and off with light.

That gap is the reframe. In mice, the probes can listen for months. In the woman on the operating table, they listen briefly, while surgeons do their routine work.

Key facts

  • Neuropixels probes are about a centimetre long, thinner than a hair, and record hundreds of neurons at once.
  • They debuted in 2017 and are now being used briefly in human neurosurgery in London.
  • The Utah array, the older single-cell tool for people, has 96 recording channels.

The plant that made America's bomb triggers is now a wildlife refuge

Rocky Flats once made the plutonium cores for US warheads; cleanup erased the site's history while the pollution stayed.

Where would you go to see the place that made the triggers for America's nuclear bombs? Today, the answer is a wildlife refuge where elk can be hunted.

That place is Rocky Flats, eighteen miles west of Denver, Colorado. Over nearly forty years it produced 60,000 to 70,000 plutonium pits, the parts of a warhead that set off the explosion.

Here's why it mattered. The weapons system had plenty of backup, but Rocky Flats was the only facility that could do assembly-line machining of those plutonium devices. Historian Jason Krupar calls it a chokepoint, so when things failed there, the ripples crossed the whole nuclear weapons network.

And things failed. Machined plutonium is highly flammable, and the plant had hundreds of fires, including major ones in 1957 and 1969. The 1969 fire cost the Atomic Energy Commission more than $65 million in cleanup and burned plutonium, and it shifted local opinion about a plant that communities had once been proud of.

Then came the raid. In 1989, FBI and EPA officials searched the plant and found sixty-two pounds of plutonium in the ventilation system. There was also evidence that more than 2,600 pounds of plutonium and other radioactive material had simply been lost over the plant's life.

Rocky Flats shut down a couple of years later. Decommissioning meant demolishing hundreds of buildings, removing more than a million cubic meters of waste and more than twenty-one tons of weapons-grade material, and treating sixteen million gallons of water.

Here's the catch. The history went with the buildings. The World War II Manhattan Project has its own national historical park, but the Cold War's weapons sites have hardly been memorialized, and the monuments that exist are often about cleanup, not production.

Former workers, community members and historians found no ally in the Department of Energy when they tried to save pieces of the plant's story. A museum has never been built, so the Rocky Flats Cold War Museum remains virtual.

What's there now is a 400-acre industrial core, a restricted Superfund site, surrounded by a 5,237-acre national wildlife refuge. The refuge's trail map labels the core "Department of Energy Legacy Site", and the environmental consequences of the nuclear past remain.

So where do you go to see it? A trail map with one label on it.

Key facts

  • Rocky Flats made 60,000 to 70,000 plutonium pits over nearly forty years.
  • A 1989 FBI and EPA raid found sixty-two pounds of plutonium in the ventilation system.
  • Today a 400-acre restricted core sits inside a 5,237-acre wildlife refuge.
  • The Rocky Flats Cold War Museum exists only online.

Learn · Physics from first principles

Why physics still has open problems

Our best descriptions of nature work well, but they fail to agree under extreme conditions.

When you drop an apple or feel the warmth of the sun, the physical world seems completely figured out. But what happens when our best theories cannot explain what we observe?

These puzzles are called open problems, and they come in two flavors. Some are theoretical, where existing formulas fail to explain an observed result, while others are experimental, where we struggle to build tests to investigate a phenomenon. In both cases, the science is simply not finished.

Here is the deepest split. The mathematical framework of the Standard Model, which describes particles, is inconsistent with general relativity, which governs gravity. Think of a kitchen where two cooks follow contradictory recipes; under extreme conditions like the Big Bang or inside black holes beyond their event horizons, both descriptions break down.

To see how progress actually works, consider a concrete count. In 1984, mathematical physicist Barry Simon published 15 open problems on quantum operators. By 2000, researchers had solved about 5 of them, meaning 15 minus 5 left 10 problems unsolved before Simon issued a second list of 15.

Other lists take much longer to clear. During the Strings 2000 conference, members proposed 10 millennium problems in theoretical physics, and as of 2025, zero of those 10 have been conclusively solved. Nobel laureate Ginzburg even grouped 30 open questions by 2005, singling out three great problems: the arrow of time, interpretations of quantum mechanics, and life arising from physics alone.

Wait, does that mean our current theories are wrong? They are simply incomplete frameworks that stop working when gravity and matter meet at extreme points. That leaves the question our next lesson answers: how can physicists resolve the clash between general relativity and the Standard Model?

Key facts

  • Theoretical open problems happen when current theories cannot explain observations, while experimental open problems involve challenges in testing them.
  • The Standard Model is mathematically inconsistent with general relativity, causing both theories to break down at gravitational singularities like the Big Bang and black holes.
  • In 1984, mathematical physicist Barry Simon proposed 15 open problems on quantum operators, and about 5 were solved by 2000.

What holds the headlines together

Sudden events expose the trust, rules and pressure that were there all along.

A headline tells you what changed. It rarely tells you what was already strained. Iran has offered to reopen Hormuz in two days if the US acts first, while neither side trusts the other. You can shorten the proposed deal. You cannot shorten the time it takes for one side to believe the other will keep it.

Rules have a quieter way of shaping events. The Supreme Court has let California count disputed ballots, after a federal voter list left eligible ballots in question. Who appears on a list sounds like paperwork until it meets someone trying to vote. The ruling makes news; the work of deciding whom a system recognizes goes on.

Other systems ask for trust each time they speak. Hackers sent fake nuclear warnings through emergency apps in Ukraine and Russia. The false alert is an event. The slower problem is whether you can believe the next message. A warning works because people expect it to come from the right place, and that expectation can be damaged.

Pressure has its own slow clock. Wage protests kept workers away and halted production at at least 690 garment factories in Bangladesh. The stoppage is the news. Beneath it is a pay dispute involving the people who keep production going. Headlines catch the moment something gives way; they offer only a glimpse of what was bearing the weight. When you read the next one, what had to be trusted, maintained or endured for it to happen?

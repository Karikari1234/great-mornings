Israel's president gives the first shot at government to the smaller bloc

Netanyahu's bloc won more seats, but President Herzog picked opposition leader Gadi Eisenkot to try forming a government first.

Benjamin Netanyahu's bloc won more seats than Gadi Eisenkot's. Eisenkot still got the first call.

The numbers: Netanyahu's Likud and its far-right and ultra-Orthodox allies took 53 seats in the 120-seat Knesset, Israel's parliament. Eisenkot's Yashar party and three other Jewish opposition parties took 48. You need 61 to govern, so neither bloc reaches it.

So why does the smaller bloc go first? Because in Israel the president decides. President Isaac Herzog has broad discretion to pick whoever they believe has the best chance of building a coalition, and does not have to choose the leader of the biggest party.

His office said Eisenkot received 67 recommendations for prime minister from incoming members, and Netanyahu 46.

Here's the catch. Add 53 and 48 and you get 101. The other 19 seats went to two Arab-led alliances, the United Arab List and the Joint List, after a surge in Arab turnout. That makes them potential kingmakers, yet all but one of the Jewish parties pledged before the vote not to work with them.

The results point to three routes. A unity government with both men in it. A right-wing coalition with Netanyahu and defectors from Eisenkot's bloc. Or an Eisenkot-led government backed by the Arab parties, which Eisenkot and every member of his bloc bar one have refused before. A deal with the ultra-Orthodox parties would also be difficult.

There is a precedent for the first route. In 2020, after three deadlocked elections, Netanyahu and Benny Gantz agreed to swap the leadership between them.

Herzog told the parties that, to avoid another election, "not every commitment and promise can be fulfilled". That is a polite way of saying: some pledges may have to break. Between 2019 and 2022, Israel held five elections as Netanyahu and his rivals kept failing to co-operate.

Eisenkot, a former military chief, wrote on X: "Now is the time to form a national emergency government. I will do everything to ensure we are worthy."

Eisenkot has 28 days, with a possible 14-day extension. Watch which promise bends first.

Key facts

  • Netanyahu's bloc won 53 seats, Eisenkot's bloc 48, in a 120-seat Knesset.
  • Two Arab-led alliances together won 19 seats.
  • Eisenkot got 67 recommendations to Netanyahu's 46.
  • Eisenkot has 28 days, with a possible 14-day extension.

Garment workers want 30,000 taka; the wage board picked only a date

The wage board says a new pay scale starts in December but has not named a figure, while protests have stopped hundreds of factories.

What does a wage board agree on when it hasn't agreed on a wage?

That is where the garment fight stands. On Wednesday, the statutory Minimum Wage Board agreed that a revised pay scale will take effect from December. What it has not done is say what that pay scale will be.

Keep that gap in mind. The whole story sits inside it.

Start with what workers want. The monthly minimum is 12,500 taka now. They want 30,000 taka, about US$237. That is 2.4 times the current figure, or an extra 17,500 taka every month.

To push for it, tens of thousands of workers have marched in recent days through Dhaka, Gazipur, Ashulia and Narayanganj. In several hubs the protests turned violent, with factories vandalised, roads blocked and clashes with police. Hundreds of plants suspended operations.

So why does a pay dispute look like an economic emergency? Because of how much rides on this one industry. Garments bring in 80 per cent of Bangladesh's export earnings. Of every 100 taka the country earns selling abroad, 80 come from clothes.

And the buyers are big. The halted factories supply H&M, Zara and Walmart, and Bangladesh is the world's second-largest clothing exporter after China. A long shutdown here can ripple into the supply chains of brands far away.

Here's the catch. The board works as a bargaining room. Factory owners and worker representatives negotiate inside it, and those talks are still going. A December start date tells workers a new scale is coming. It says nothing about whether the figure lands near 30,000 or near 12,500.

Plenty is still unknown. The reporting so far has no response from owners, the government or the brands, no word on injuries or arrests, and no sense of how long the halt will last or what it will cost.

So the question from the top still stands. The board has picked a month. The number it writes next to that month is the thing this whole fight is about.

Key facts

  • Workers want the monthly minimum wage raised to 30,000 taka (US$237) from 12,500 taka.
  • The Minimum Wage Board agreed a revised scale takes effect from December but has not set a figure.
  • Unrest forced hundreds of plants supplying H&M, Zara and Walmart to suspend operations.
  • Garments bring in 80 per cent of Bangladesh's export earnings.

Delhi's air is bad again as Diwali begins

Delhi's air has plummeted again as Diwali arrives, and India's Supreme Court says traffic is a big part of why.

Diwali is the festival of lights, a five-day celebration of light winning over darkness. This year, it begins with Delhi's air quality having plummeted again.

That is a grim way to start a party. So what is going on with the air?

Part of the answer is the festival itself. People set off firecrackers and fireworks, and pollution regularly spikes during Diwali. This year it runs from November 6 to 10, with the main day on November 8.

Here's the catch. The fireworks land on air that was already bad. Delhi's air quality has plummeted again as winter nears.

And the smoke has another source. India's Supreme Court says vehicle emissions are a big cause of Delhi's pollution. It is reviewing how the city tackles the problem, and it implored people to celebrate responsibly.

Why does this keep happening? Delhi battles dangerously polluted air every winter. The emergency restrictions kick in only after pollution has already reached dangerous levels.

Think of a smoke alarm that rings only once the room is full of smoke. It does its job, but late.

Years of government action, emergency rules and public anger have not changed the pattern. DW asks the obvious question: why is there no lasting solution to a crisis that arrives on schedule?

Nobody in the reporting has a clear answer yet.

Qatar's plan trades nuclear inspections for an open Strait of Hormuz

Qatar is trying to line up a swap: UN inspectors into Iran's bombed nuclear sites, in return for a lifted US blockade and a reopened strait.

Of the 10.23 million barrels of Gulf crude that passed through the strait of Hormuz each day in October, about 42% went to China. That is more than 4 million barrels a day heading to one buyer. India took about 13%.

So when Qatar, the lead mediator in the US-Iran conflict, puts that waterway into a deal, a lot of the world is watching. The deal is about nuclear inspections.

Qatar wants Iran to let International Atomic Energy Agency inspectors, the UN's nuclear watchdog, visit its bombed nuclear sites. That includes the ones housing its stockpile of highly enriched uranium.

Here's the offer. The US would immediately lift its naval blockade on Iran in the strait and release a portion of $8bn in frozen assets. On the second day, Iran would open the strait, along a route it has already negotiated with Oman.

Iran would not have to promise the uranium leaves the country. Other options are on the table, such as down-blending it, which means reducing its uranium content.

Here's the catch. Iran has other conditions: an end to the war in Lebanon, US forces pulling back from its coastline, some sanctions lifted, and war reparations. The order of these steps has been at the heart of the mediation. Think of a queue where everyone wants someone else to step forward first.

Inspectors are a sore point. Iran says it has lost faith in Rafael Grossi, who heads the inspectorate, and claims he failed to criticise the US for bombing its nuclear sites. It also fears what inspectors gather could be handed to the Americans and Israelis as targeting data.

Meanwhile, Trump is still weighing a new assault on Iranian nuclear sites. The Guardian says those threats look weaker inside Iran after JD Vance said the war itself was critical to the Republican collapse in the midterms. Qatar's emir spoke to Trump, his office said on Friday, though it did not reveal what was said.

The strait is already selective. An Iranian lawmaker said ships from China, India, Qatar and Iraq are allowed through. Indian and Chinese vessels pay transit fees, while Qatari ships pass free.

Oman has a longer-term idea: shipping companies would pay for services, never tolls. But the first question is simpler. Will Tehran let inspectors in, when it says it no longer trusts the man in charge of them?

Key facts

  • Qatar is pushing Iran to allow IAEA inspectors into its bombed nuclear sites.
  • The US would lift its naval blockade and release part of $8bn in frozen assets; Iran would open the strait on day two.
  • Iran has other preconditions, including ending the war in Lebanon, sanctions relief and reparations.
  • Of 10.23m barrels a day through the strait in October, about 42% went to China and 13% to India.

About 4 minutes of hard daily effort is linked to far lower dementia risk

A large study found that short bursts of vigorous everyday movement went with a 48 per cent lower dementia risk, though it cannot prove cause.

3.7 minutes. That is about one pop song, and it is the daily amount of hard effort that went with a 48 per cent lower dementia risk in a large study.

The effort was not a gym session. Think rushing for the bus, carrying heavy shopping or climbing stairs. Researchers call this vigorous lifestyle activity, and it hides in ordinary days.

Here is how they found it. Nicholas Koemel at the University of Sydney and colleagues used data from 22,618 adults, mostly in their early 60s, from the UK Biobank study. None had dementia when they wore wrist trackers for seven days between 2013 and 2015.

Then came the waiting. The team followed people for around eight years, until a dementia diagnosis, a death, or November 2022. In that time, 170 people were diagnosed.

Now the comparison. Of people who did no exercise at all, 2.5 per cent developed dementia. Among those with a few daily bouts of vigorous lifestyle activity but no set exercise time, it was 0.71 per cent. The lowest rate, 0.59 per cent, was in people who also exercised on purpose.

Brain structure pointed the same way. More of this activity was linked to larger grey matter volumes in several brain regions, and to shrinking of white matter hyperintensities, which are areas of abnormal brain tissue.

Here's the catch. The study is observational, so it cannot show the bursts changed anyone's risk. Early, undiagnosed dementia might have left some people less able to move that way, and the tracker only captured one week, years before diagnosis.

So why might it work? The researchers speculate that even tiny hard efforts could lower blood pressure and reduce arterial stiffness, which cuts the chance of vascular brain injury. Fast posture adjustments may also engage the brain's cerebellar and sensorimotor networks.

Nobody is telling you to sprint up every staircase tomorrow. Koemel says people should increase activity gradually, because sudden shifts could raise the risk of accidents or injuries. His line: "A little can go a long way here."

The open question is whether the bursts protect the brain or simply mark the people whose brains were already healthy. This study cannot settle that.

  • 22,618 UK Biobank adults, mostly early 60s, wore trackers for seven days and were followed for around eight years
  • 170 developed dementia during the study
  • 3.7 minutes a day of vigorous routine movement was associated with 48 per cent lower dementia risk
  • The study is observational and cannot prove cause

Is AI really intelligent? Five experts gave five different answers

Experts in linguistics, computing and cognitive science disagree on whether AI is intelligent, partly because "intelligence" is a vague word.

Can a machine be intelligent if nobody agrees what intelligence is? That is the snag under a question Undark put to five experts in linguistics, computer science, cognitive science and psychology.

Their answers were very different. The one thing they shared was that "intelligence" is vague and poorly suited to the conversation. Benjamin K. Bergen, a cognitive science professor at the University of California, San Diego, put it simply: "Psychologists disagree on what it is."

Start with the sceptic. Emily M. Bender, a linguistics professor at the University of Washington, says we probably cannot build systems with human-like cognition, and definitely not through language modeling, the approach behind LLMs. Her reason: a word pairs form (its spelling or sound) with meaning, and language models manipulate form without reference to meaning.

Here's the catch. When we read text, we imagine a mind behind it, and we do it instinctively. So the only evidence that LLMs "think" comes from our own effort to make sense of what they write, which makes judging them hard.

Melanie Mitchell of the Santa Fe Institute questions a different line. When companies like DeepMind and OpenAI define artificial general intelligence, she says, they talk only about cognitive tasks and leave out physical ones. ChatGPT is not going to fix your roof.

But can you really separate the two? Say an AI tells you to press the red button on the left side of your dishwasher door. If there are two red buttons, you are the one using intelligence to work out which.

She adds social intelligence: you may not know how to fix your car, but you know how to find someone who does. She does not know why AI is so bad at that. Maybe the training is wrong, or maybe AI lacks the right kind of participation in the social world.

Anima Anandkumar, a professor at Caltech, picks a plainer definition: the ability to learn from data and adapt to your surroundings. By that standard, she says, many AI systems already do both. Chatbots, for example, adapt through human feedback, which is called reinforcement learning.

See what happened? Same machines, different verdicts, because the meaning of the word moved. And Anandkumar was already looking past the chatbots. Language models mirror a lot of human intelligence, she said, but she wanted to talk about nature's intelligence.

  • Emily M. Bender says language models manipulate form without reference to meaning.
  • Anima Anandkumar defines intelligence as learning from data and adapting to surroundings, and says many AI systems do both.

The plant that made America's bomb triggers is now a wildlife refuge

Rocky Flats made the plutonium cores for US warheads for nearly forty years, and today its trail map calls part of it a "Legacy Site" next to a herd of elk.

Here is a place you can now visit to see elk. It sits eighteen miles west of Denver, and for nearly forty years it made 60,000 to 70,000 plutonium pits, the triggering parts of nuclear warheads.

It was called Rocky Flats, and it was once secret. Think of a warhead as a firework with a fuse. The pit is the fuse, the part that sets off the nuclear explosion.

Here's the catch. The nuclear weapons system had a lot of backup built in, but Rocky Flats was the only facility that could machine those plutonium devices on an assembly line. When it had trouble, the trouble rippled through the whole network. Historian Jason Krupar calls it a chokepoint.

And it had trouble. Machined plutonium is highly flammable, and the plant saw hundreds of fires, with major ones in 1957 and 1969. The 1969 fire cost the Atomic Energy Commission more than $65 million in cleanup and lost plutonium. It also shifted local opinion, which had started out proud of the plant and eager for federal money.

Then came 1989. FBI and EPA officials raided the plant. They found sixty-two pounds of plutonium in the ventilation system, and evidence that more than 2,600 pounds of plutonium and other radioactive material had simply been lost over the plant's life. It shut down a couple of years later.

The cleanup was huge. Hundreds of buildings came down, more than a million cubic meters of waste was removed, and sixteen million gallons of water were treated.

So where did the history go? Former workers, locals and historians wanted to save pieces of it, but the Department of Energy was no ally. A long effort to build a museum has not happened, and the Rocky Flats Cold War Museum exists only online.

Today a 400-acre core, a restricted Superfund site, sits inside a 5,237-acre national wildlife refuge. The refuge trail map labels the core a "Department of Energy Legacy Site." The elk can be hunted from September through December.

The article's point is that Cold War bomb-making has barely been memorialized, and the monuments that exist are often about cleanup, not production. The land looks restored. The pollution, the article says, is still there.

  • A 1989 FBI and EPA raid found sixty-two pounds of plutonium in the ventilation system.

Learn · Physics from first principles

Open problems: what physics still cannot explain

Lesson 15 of 15: the unanswered questions are not a failure of physics. They are the map of where it goes next.

Drop a spoon and it falls. Physics explains that so well that it is easy to think the subject is finished. It is not, and the people who work in it keep written lists of exactly what they cannot yet explain.

Those lists come in two flavors. Some problems are theoretical: our best theories cannot account for something we have seen. Others are experimental: we have an idea, but nobody has worked out how to build a test for it, or how to look closely enough at a strange effect.

Here's the big one. Our best theory of particles, the Standard Model, has math that does not fit with general relativity, our theory of gravity. Push either one to extremes, like the Big Bang or the centers of black holes, and both break down. Beyond the Standard Model sit more open questions: what dark matter and dark energy are, why there is more matter than antimatter, and what the absolute mass of neutrinos is.

Think of a list like a to-do list that nobody has crossed off yet. Isaac Newton started the habit: his book Opticks, first edition 1704, ended up with a list of 31 questions about light and matter. In 1900, Lord Kelvin said physics had just "two clouds": no evidence for the luminiferous aether, and a failure to predict the heat capacity of solids. Both clouds cleared within a decade, through special relativity and quantum mechanics.

Wait, that's the clever bit. The cloud that looked small turned into whole new physics. So a short list of puzzles is not a sign of a nearly finished subject. It can be a sign of where a revolution is hiding.

Now a worked example with numbers. In 1984, Barry Simon proposed 15 open problems on quantum operators. By 2000, about 5 were solved, so roughly one in three had fallen in 16 years. He then released a second list of 15 in 2000, and at least 3 of those were solved. Progress is real, but slow, and it comes problem by problem. Penrose's 1982 list on general relativity still had about 14 unsolved in 2017, and the 10 millennium problems of theoretical physics from the Strings 2000 conference had none conclusively solved as of 2025.

Vitaly Ginzburg, a Nobel laureate, kept such a list from 1971 to 2005 and ended with 30 problems. He also named three great ones: why time has an arrow, how to interpret quantum mechanics, and whether life can arise from physics alone. This is the last lesson, so that last question is yours to carry out the door: if the laws are all there is, how do they build something that asks questions?

Key facts

  • In 1900 Lord Kelvin named "two clouds" over physics, and both were solved within a decade by special relativity and quantum mechanics.
  • Barry Simon's 1984 list of 15 problems had about 5 solved by 2000.
  • The Standard Model's math is inconsistent with general relativity, and both break down at singularities like the Big Bang and inside black holes.
  • Vitaly Ginzburg's list from 1971 to 2005 ended with 30 problems, plus three great ones.

When fast promises hit slow walls

Headlines chase sudden leaps, but real power still belongs to the quiet friction of trust and physical space.

An approved buyer for the world's most guarded computer chips turned out to be an empty office. That strange gap between official paperwork and physical reality is the quiet puzzle sitting under almost every headline today.

Think of a busy kitchen where the front counter keeps taking orders while the stove is already jammed full. The news naturally tracks the loud moves, like sudden strikes and high-level summits, but those events are only ripples on the surface. Beneath them, two slower rules decide what actually holds: trust is fragile, and stubborn physical limits always set the real pace.

Here is the catch. You can see that drag across the board: voters blocked data centers in 46 of 47 ballots, investors fled sliding tech shares for Warren Buffett's holding company, and Israel's president gave the first try at government to a smaller bloc. When pressure builds, paper claims break first, and power snaps right back to whoever controls physical space or cold cash.

Daily headlines will always rush after the newest announcement, but the actual outcome is already being shaped by the slow machinery underneath. That is why big promises stumble so quickly: they treat the world as a blank screen, forgetting the stubborn weight of people, pipes, and power lines.

Cover story

Garment workers were promised new pay, but nobody has the number

After five days of protests and hundreds of shut factories, the wage board agreed to a revised pay structure, but the new minimum won't be announced until December.

What do you do when your fight for a raise ends in a yes, and the yes has no number in it?

That is where garment workers landed this week. After five days of protests, the six-member wage board agreed on Wednesday to a revised pay structure. But it did not discuss the size of any increase at its first meeting, said workers' representative Babul Akter.

So the yes did not settle it. On Thursday, workers in Ashulia reported to work, walked out and blocked a major highway, demanding a clear announcement of the increase. More than 100 factories there suspended operations, a police superintendent said.

Look at the ask. They want a minimum of 30,000 taka a month, up from 12,500 now. That is a 140 percent jump: every 10 taka becomes 24.

Wait, didn't they just get a raise? They did. In November 2023 the minimum went up 56.25 percent, from 8,000 taka to 12,500. Workers say rising food, housing and transport costs have since eaten away what that money buys.

Here's why a wage fight can shake so much. The industry employs about 4 million people, most of them women from rural Bangladesh, AP reports. And out of every 10 taka of export earnings, more than 8 come from garments.

That weight showed this week. Hundreds of factories shut, disrupting production for spring and summer export orders. H&M told Reuters some factories had temporarily closed, but that it had not seen major supply-chain disruptions. So far, the reports don't show how many orders, if any, will be late.

It has cost people, too. Police used tear gas and sound grenades in clashes with workers on Wednesday in Savar and Gazipur. In Fatullah, dozens were injured, according to media reports cited by AP.

Still, work had largely resumed by Thursday. More than 95 percent of factories in Narayanganj were running again, the industrial police chief said, while isolated demonstrations continued in Gazipur and Ashulia.

Going back isn't always up to the worker. Monowara, a 35-year-old Gazipur garment worker, said she was ready to return, but her factory told workers it would stay closed until Sunday.

Watch December. That is when the board says it will announce the new minimum, taking effect in January. Until then the yes has no number in it, and nobody can say whether the protests will stop.

Key facts

  • The wage board agreed to a revised pay structure on November 4, 2026, but has not decided the amount.
  • Workers want a 30,000-taka monthly minimum, up from 12,500 taka.
  • The new minimum is due to be announced in December and take effect in January.
  • Garments generate more than 80 percent of Bangladesh's export earnings.

Garment workers showed up, then refused to work. 130 factories shut anyway

Street clashes have eased, but about 130 garment factories stayed idle on 5 November while tax investigators chase the money allegedly behind the unrest.

On 5 November, workers turned up at some factories in Ashulia, Gazipur and Narayanganj. Then they refused to join production.

That was enough to stop the line. Owners were compelled to declare holidays, and Industrial Police data showed production suspended at around 130 factories. By the police count, that was 70 in Savar, around 50 in Gazipur and eight in Narayanganj.

So a factory can go quiet without a single brick thrown. Think of it as a strike with the doors left open.

Still, things are calmer. Gazi Jashim Uddin, an additional inspector general of Industrial Police, said there were no major road blockades, large gatherings or vandalism, unlike previous days. The improvement followed assurances that the new wage structure would be implemented by December.

Here's the catch. There were still attempts to block roads, and crude bombs were thrown from motorcycles in Gazipur. Uddin said workers are unlikely to be the ones throwing them, and that others are clearly involved.

BGMEA president Mahmud Hasan Khan Babu put it carefully: the situation has improved somewhat, but we cannot say it has returned to normal.

Meanwhile, a second front has opened, and it is about money. Two intelligence wings of the National Board of Revenue, the tax authority, are scrutinising the transactions of about 225 businesses and individuals for allegedly fuelling the unrest.

Sources said banks were asked to freeze the accounts of more than 100 individuals, including former Awami League leaders and influential businesspeople. Around 125 companies are also under scrutiny, and an official said the number is likely to increase.

What are they accused of? Intelligence officials say at least 25 groups were activated through Facebook, Telegram and WhatsApp, with money moving by cash, mobile financial services and cryptocurrencies. They also allege around 300 activists were brought to garment hubs to mingle with workers.

These are allegations. SM Mannan Kochi, a former BGMEA president who was named, denied them and said the government is trying to cover up its own failures by blaming others.

The date to watch is December, when the new wage structure is promised. Factories were set to stay closed on Saturday.

Key facts

  • NBR intelligence wings are scrutinising about 225 businesses and individuals for allegedly fuelling the unrest.

India exempts six minority groups from visa rules, then asks them to get a visa

A new Home Ministry order keeps an exemption for six non-Muslim communities from three countries, but now requires them to hold a visa to keep staying in India.

How can you be exempt from a visa rule and still be told to get a visa? That is the puzzle inside a new order from India's Home Ministry.

Start with the exemption. A notification of September 2, 2025 excused Hindus, Sikhs, Buddhists, Jains, Parsis and Christians from Afghanistan, Bangladesh and Pakistan from needing valid passports or visas to enter and stay. It applies to those who sought shelter in India because of religious persecution, or fear of it, and arrived on or before December 31, 2024.

Here's the catch. An amendment notified on Wednesday, November 4, 2026 keeps that exemption but adds a condition. In the notification's words, every such person "shall be required to obtain an appropriate category of visa after their entry into India for their continued stay in India."

In plain terms, how you got in is still forgiven. Staying on now needs paperwork. That includes people who entered without valid documents and people whose documents have since expired.

So why change it? The source says the amendment lines up the exempted categories with the Citizenship (Amendment) Act of 2019. That law fast-tracked citizenship for the same six communities, but only for those who entered before December 31, 2014. The new visa rule covers entries up to 2024, a full ten years later.

The order does more than this. Indian citizens travelling to Nepal or Bhutan, and Nepali and Bhutanese citizens entering or leaving India, must now show identity documents listed in a newly defined "Visa Manual". Indian citizens who use those borders to head to a third country must carry a valid passport.

Some groups stay as they were. Registered Sri Lankan Tamil refugees who took shelter in India up to January 9, 2015 keep their exemption. Tibetans on the specified entry permits stay exempt from visa and passport rules, though they must register and hold a Certificate of Registration.

Here's the last twist. The same visa requirement also reaches some Pakistani nationals who entered before December 31, 2009 intending to settle, certain Partition-era migrants who returned from Pakistan and settled in Kerala, and persons of Indian origin who later took Malaysian or Singaporean citizenship and cannot renew their passports. The visa rule is wider than the six communities.

Key facts

  • The Home Ministry amended the Immigration and Foreigners (Exemption) Order, 2025 on November 4, 2026.
  • Hindus, Sikhs, Buddhists, Jains, Parsis and Christians from Afghanistan, Bangladesh and Pakistan who entered by December 31, 2024 keep their exemption but must obtain an appropriate visa to continue staying.
  • The CAA 2019 cutoff for fast-tracked citizenship is December 31, 2014.
  • Some long-term Pakistani, Kerala-settled and Malaysian or Singaporean-citizen groups must also get a visa.

Finnish court jails two men over anchor dragged along Baltic seabed

A Finnish court found a ship's captain and a senior crew member guilty of deliberately dragging an anchor across the seabed and breaking two undersea telecom cables.

130 kilometers. That is how far a court says a cargo ship dragged its anchor along the bottom of the Gulf of Finland. It is a bit more than three marathons, run along the seabed.

On Thursday, the Helsinki District Court ruled it was no accident. It found the Russian captain of the Fitburg and a senior crew member guilty of "aggravated criminal damage," saying they dragged the anchor intentionally.

What got broken? Two undersea telecommunications cables, the kind that carry phone and internet traffic. These ones link Helsinki, Finland's capital, with Tallinn in Estonia.

The captain got three and a half years in jail. The crew member, an Azerbaijani citizen, got two years and 10 months.

Then comes the bill. The two were ordered to jointly pay around 1.9 million euros in damages, and the captain must pay about 85,000 euros more.

How did anyone catch it? Finnish authorities seized the ship on December 31 last year. Police said at the time it had been dragging its anchor for hours when it was found in Finland's exclusive economic zone, the stretch of sea a country controls for resources and the like.

The Fitburg and its 14 crew members were heading from St. Petersburg in Russia to the port of Haifa in Israel when it happened.

Here's the catch. Both men deny any wrongdoing, and they have challenged whether Finland has the jurisdiction, meaning the legal right, to try them at all.

This case sits inside a bigger pattern. Since Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, there has been a series of incidents involving cables and pipelines in the Baltic Sea, and Finland has stepped up efforts to protect its undersea infrastructure.

So the court has spoken, but the defendants are still contesting Finland's authority over the case. Whether that challenge changes anything is the question left hanging.

Key facts

  • A Helsinki court convicted the Fitburg's Russian captain and an Azerbaijani crew member of aggravated criminal damage.
  • The court said they intentionally dragged the anchor about 130 km, damaging two cables linking Helsinki and Tallinn.
  • Sentences: three and a half years, and two years and 10 months.
  • The defendants deny wrongdoing and challenge Finland's jurisdiction.

Scientists set a cold record in orbit by watching atoms drift

Researchers on China's Tiangong space station cooled rubidium atoms to an equivalent 9.7 trillionths of a kelvin above absolute zero, beating the previous reported low of 38.

You probably think a temperature is something you read off a thermometer. A new record for cold came from somewhere else: timing how slowly a tiny cloud of atoms drifted apart.

The number is 9.7 picokelvins, reached with rubidium atoms aboard China's Tiangong space station and reported in the journal Science on November 5. A picokelvin is one trillionth of a kelvin, and zero kelvin is absolute zero, minus 273.15 degrees Celsius, the bottom of the temperature scale.

Stretch one kelvin into a trillion seconds, about 31,700 years, and these atoms sit roughly 10 seconds from zero.

So how do you take a temperature that low? Colder atoms move more slowly. Think of a crowd let out of a room: the slower everyone walks, the longer the group takes to spread out. Release the atoms, time the spread, and you get what researchers call an equivalent temperature.

Here's the catch. Earth's gravity makes weakly trapped clouds sag, which limits both the cooling and the watching. Orbit offers near-weightlessness and a longer look.

The previous reported low, 38 picokelvins, came from a drop tower in Germany, a shaft where experiments free-fall, and the spreading cloud was watched for about two seconds. The Tiangong team watched for seven. Their figure is roughly a quarter of the old one, and the margin of error, plus 3.2 or minus 2.7, doesn't close the gap.

Orbit alone doesn't win it, though. NASA's Cold Atom Laboratory on the International Space Station got as low as 52 picokelvins.

So what did it take? A cloud of about 20,000 atoms, smaller than a grain of sand, held and cooled by lasers. Researcher Tang Li said more than 20 beams must focus on a cloud tens of micrometres across, with an alignment error of no more than five micrometres, far thinner than a human hair.

Peking University professor Xiaoji Zhou said the project took 15 years from planning to results in orbit.

Why bother? Researchers say this could lead to sharper atom interferometers, instruments that use the wave behaviour of atoms to measure gravity and acceleration, and to tests of the equivalence principle, the idea in Einstein's gravity that everything falls the same way.

No report says this run found minerals or gave a new Einstein test.

So when does a drifting speck of atoms become a working sensor? The reports give no timetable.

Key facts

  • Rubidium atoms aboard Tiangong reached an equivalent temperature of 9.7 picokelvins, with an uncertainty of plus 3.2 or minus 2.7.
  • The figure comes from how slowly the released atoms spread, watched for seven seconds, rather than a conventional temperature reading.
  • The previous reported low was 38 picokelvins in a German drop tower, with about two seconds of observation.
  • More precise gravity and acceleration sensors remain prospective, with no timetable reported.

OpenAI asks human scientists to check its new math proofs

After claiming major scientific breakthroughs, OpenAI is having independent researchers and verification software check its work.

Russell Impagliazzo helped create a famous computer science rule decades ago, and now he is leading a team to see if an artificial intelligence just broke it.

OpenAI announced three major scientific claims this week across mathematics, computer science, and physics. Its models reportedly proved Christopher Sogge's 1991 local smoothing conjecture, solved half of a Millennium Prize physics problem, and dismantled Impagliazzo's rule, known as the Exponential Time Hypothesis.

That hypothesis is the widely held belief that problems like 3-SAT cannot be solved meaningfully faster than brute force. If the machine really refuted it, computer scientists will have to rethink how quickly difficult calculations can be solved.

So why not celebrate immediately? OpenAI ran into trouble in the past after claiming an earlier model solved ten open math problems, only for researchers to find the system had mostly dredged up answers already sitting in the literature.

Here is the catch. This time, instead of dumping raw solutions on the internet and walking away, OpenAI shared its findings with outside experts called shepherds.

These shepherds sign no non-disclosure agreements, receive no pay, and get full credit for explaining the work. Nobel laureate David Gross is leading the physics group, which decided to hold back the machine-generated text and write the paper themselves.

The teams also turned to an automated referee. They posted the computer science and math proofs to GitHub alongside code written for Lean, a software program that checks formal mathematical reasoning.

Think of Lean like a strict spellchecker that verifies mathematical logic. It checks every single step of an argument, guaranteeing that the mathematical reasoning contains zero internal errors.

Yet even software has a blind spot. Lean guarantees that a proof matches a formal mathematical statement, but only human shepherds can confirm whether that statement actually answers the real question.

That leaves the ultimate test to human minds. Impagliazzo and his fellow shepherds must decide whether the machine truly solved his puzzle, or simply found a very neat way to ask the wrong question.

Hanford's B Reactor made plutonium and its own obstacle

Built to make plutonium for nuclear weapons, the reactor briefly stalled because its own reaction produced a neutron-absorbing byproduct.

Chien-Shiung Wu identified a substance that could stall a nuclear reactor. When the B Reactor at Hanford began running in 1944, that substance brought its initial operation to a halt. How could a machine make its own obstacle?

The reactor, near Richland, Washington, was part of the Manhattan Project, the US nuclear weapons program during World War II. Its job was to turn some of its natural uranium fuel into plutonium-239 for use in weapons.

Here is the trick. A nuclear reaction releases neutrons. Inside B Reactor, some struck uranium-238 and changed it into plutonium-239. The plutonium was then chemically separated from the fuel at Hanford's T Plant.

Why go through that trouble? The project also tried to separate uranium-235 from uranium-238, the dominant form in natural uranium. That was extremely complex. Once the reactor made plutonium, its different chemistry offered an easier way to separate it.

The staffing shows the difference between the routes. The Y12 uranium enrichment plant in Tennessee employed 22,000 people. B Reactor needed only a few dozen. It still required a leap in scale: an earlier pilot reactor had reached 4 megawatts, while B was designed for 250, more than 60 times as much.

Graphite helped sustain B's chain reaction, and water from the Columbia River carried away heat. The water passed around the uranium fuel, sat in basins, then returned to the river. The open cooling cycle contaminated the water.

Then came Wu's culprit. Xenon-135, a byproduct of the reaction, absorbed neutrons. With fewer neutrons available, the reactor's initial operation stopped. The problem was overcome by loading more uranium.

B was joined by two identical reactors, D in December 1944 and F in February 1945. Plutonium from the site went into the Trinity test and the bomb detonated above Nagasaki. It was also used in thousands of US warheads during the Cold War.

B Reactor shut down permanently in February 1968 and can now be toured by advance reservation. Its early stumble has its own place in the record: the world's first large-scale reactor was also the first to experience xenon poisoning.

Key facts

  • B Reactor was designed to operate at 250 megawatts.
  • It made plutonium-239 from natural uranium fuel.
  • Xenon-135 halted its initial operation by absorbing neutrons.
  • The reactor shut down permanently in February 1968.

Learn · Physics from first principles

Why physics still has open problems

An unanswered question can mean a theory cannot explain a result, or that nobody can yet run the experiment needed to test an answer.

You switch on a lamp and light reaches your eyes. That ordinary event gives physics something to explain. But what happens when an observation has no explanation, or an explanation has no experiment to test it?

Those are two different kinds of open problem. A theoretical problem begins when existing theories cannot explain an observation or experimental result. An experimental problem begins when researchers cannot yet make the test that would investigate a proposed answer.

Here is a worked example from physics history. In 1900, Lord Kelvin named two problems: no evidence for the proposed substance called the luminiferous aether, and a theory that failed to predict how much heat solids could hold. Within a decade, special relativity resolved the first problem and quantum mechanics resolved the second. Two problems called for two different changes in the ideas physicists used.

Today's questions include the absolute mass of neutrinos and the nature of dark matter and dark energy. Each name points to something physics still needs to determine. Giving a question a name is useful; it is not the same as answering it.

There is a deeper difficulty, too. The Standard Model and general relativity do not fit together mathematically, and both break down under extreme conditions such as those associated with the Big Bang and the centers of black holes. A theory can work well in its own domain and still leave a gap where you most want an answer.

So an open problem is also a guide to the next step: explain a result, test a proposed explanation, or find a way to make theories agree. When a proposed answer cannot yet be tested, how would you design an experiment that could settle it?

Key facts

  • Open problems can be theoretical or experimental.
  • Kelvin identified two problems in 1900; both were resolved within a decade.
  • The Standard Model and general relativity are mathematically incompatible under extreme conditions.
  • The absolute mass of neutrinos and the nature of dark matter and dark energy remain open questions.

A decision makes news in a moment. Making it real takes longer.

A promise can make news before anyone knows its size. Garment workers still lack the new minimum wage figure after a revised pay structure was agreed. That gap runs through this morning's paper.

Cover story

Hasina asks for a new passport, and the government says no

Ousted prime minister Sheikh Hasina has applied for a new passport and ID card from exile in India, and the government has refused and is pressing for extradition.

Sheikh Hasina, the prime minister forced out of Bangladesh in 2024, has asked for a new passport and a national identity card, Reuters reported in an exclusive. The government has turned her down.

On paper this is an argument about a small booklet. Here's why it matters a lot more than that.

A quick catch-up. Hasina ran the country for 15 straight years, from 2009, until a student-led uprising pushed her out on 5 August 2024. She fled to India, and her diplomatic passport was cancelled that same year.

Then things got heavier. In November 2025 the International Crimes Tribunal convicted her in absentia over the crackdown on the 2024 protests and sentenced her to death. Bangladesh has asked India to hand her over.

So here's the puzzle. The government wants Hasina back in Bangladesh. Several outlets read her passport request as a sign that she wants to come back too. So why is the answer no?

The difference is in how she would arrive. A passport lets you travel on your own terms: book a flight, land, walk through the airport. Extradition is the opposite. One government hands a person over to another.

Dhaka Tribune's headline puts the government's position in one line: it insists on extradition. Refusing the passport leaves extradition as the route the government says it wants.

Wait, a person sentenced to death is applying for travel papers? That's the strange part, and one podcast asked the question directly: why won't the government issue her a passport?

A lot is still unclear from what has been reported so far. We don't know when she applied, through which office, who in the government refused, or the exact wording of the refusal. India's position hasn't been reported either.

Even the headlines can't agree on where this is heading. Bloomberg called the request a "step toward return." Another outlet asked why Hasina "may never return" at all. If she ever comes back, will she come with a passport or under extradition?

Key facts

  • Reuters reported Sheikh Hasina is seeking a new passport and national identity card.
  • The government has denied the request.
  • Dhaka Tribune reports the government insists on extradition instead.
  • Outlets disagree on what it means: Bloomberg calls it a step toward return; StratNews Global asks why she may never return.

Bangladesh Bank doubles the no-approval limit for sending money abroad for medical care

Banks can now send up to $30,000 for treatment abroad without asking the central bank, and handle more school payments too, while paperwork on foreign cash coming in gets stricter.

Why would a central bank need to sign off on a family's payment for hospital treatment abroad? Until now, above $15,000, it did. That line has just moved.

Bangladesh Bank has doubled the limit to $30,000. Authorised dealer banks, the ones allowed to handle foreign currency, can release up to that amount, or its equivalent, for medical treatment abroad without asking the central bank first.

Think of it as a permission slip. Below the line, the bank says yes on its own. Above it, the bank has to go and get a signature.

Schooling got the same treatment. Previously, banks needed Bangladesh Bank's approval to release foreign currency for school-level education abroad. Now they can handle secondary and higher secondary-equivalent studies themselves.

There is a condition. The bank must verify that free education is unavailable.

A third change covers correspondence courses. Banks can remit money for regular academic studies by correspondence without prior approval. Before, that was only for certification and professional courses.

Here's the catch. The same circular tightens the other direction, for money coming in. Resident Bangladeshis who bring foreign currency into the country can only credit it to a Resident Foreign Currency Deposit account, a bank account that holds foreign money, if they declared it to Customs using Form FMJ.

So why now? Bangladesh Bank said it acted in view of the expansion of individual outward remittance facilities for private travel, medical treatment, overseas education and other permitted purposes. The stated aim is to ease outward remittances and make legitimate current-account transactions, the everyday payments for goods, services and similar needs, smoother.

One last detail. The circular says all other existing foreign exchange regulations and instructions stay exactly as they were.

Key facts

  • Limit for medical treatment remittance without prior approval rose from $15,000 to $30,000.
  • Banks can remit for secondary and higher secondary education abroad without approval if they verify free education is unavailable.
  • Foreign currency brought in by residents can go into RFCD accounts only if declared to Customs on Form FMJ.
  • All other foreign exchange rules remain unchanged.

Delhi allowed "green" fireworks for Diwali. The air still hit 58 times the safe limit

After Diwali fireworks, one Delhi neighbourhood's air hit more than 58 times the WHO daily limit, on top of a winter smog problem that has many sources.

This month, the Supreme Court allowed limited use of so-called "green" firecrackers during Diwali. They are designed to produce fewer emissions. Then Diwali came to Delhi.

On early Monday morning, around eight hours after the fireworks peaked, the air in the Mandir Marg area read 879 micrograms of PM 2.5 per cubic meter. That is more than 58 times the World Health Organization's recommended daily limit.

PM 2.5 means particles so tiny they can slip deep into your lungs. The source calls them cancer-causing. The reading came from a city government station, reported by the monitoring group IQAir.

Here's the catch. Restrictions like the court's are routinely flouted, and the city reverberated with explosions anyway.

So are fireworks the whole story? Not close. Delhi, home to more than 30 million people, already has a winter smog problem, and fireworks land on top of it.

India's environment ministry splits that winter pollution into slices. Vehicles account for 23%. Farm stubble fires and other biomass burning bring 20%. Industry, including coal-fired power plants, adds about nine percent.

The biggest slice is the odd one: 27% comes from secondary particles. Nobody emits those directly. Gases from vehicles, industry, power plants and burning react in the air and form them.

That's the clever, and annoying, bit. You can cut a smokestack and still get particles, because the haze is partly cooked in the sky.

Authorities have recently restricted traffic and construction to curb the smog. Diwali, though, is one night of bangs against a season of haze.

A 2024 study in The Lancet Planetary Health estimated that 3.8 million deaths in India between 2009 and 2019 were linked to air pollution.

Key facts

  • PM 2.5 reached 879 micrograms per cubic meter in Mandir Marg on Monday morning, per IQAir citing a city government station.
  • The Supreme Court allowed limited use of "green" firecrackers this month, but restrictions are routinely flouted.
  • Environment ministry shares of Delhi winter pollution: secondary particles 27%, vehicles 23%, biomass burning 20%, industry about 9%.
  • A 2024 Lancet Planetary Health study linked 3.8 million deaths in India between 2009 and 2019 to air pollution.

Bangladesh's inflation dipped to 8.27% in October as food eased and non-food costs rose faster.

Inflation went down in October. Prices did not.

That sounds like a riddle, but it is how the number works. Inflation is the speed at which prices climb, like a car's speedometer. Easing off the pedal slows the car. It does not send it backwards.

On 9 November, the Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics released the October figures. General inflation came in at 8.27%, down from 8.34% in September.

Here's the catch. That is a drop of just 0.07 percentage points, barely a nudge. Yet food prices alone moved a lot more than that.

Food inflation fell to 6.40% from 7.22% in September, down 0.82 percentage points in a month. That is the good news in the report.

So why did the overall number hardly budge? Because the non-food side went the opposite way. Non-food inflation rose to 9.89% from 9.30%, up 0.59 percentage points.

Two moves pulling against each other. One slowed, one sped up, and the headline figure landed almost where it started.

Make it human-sized. At 8.27%, something that cost 100 a year ago now costs about 108. Food has risen to about 106 on that scale. Non-food has risen to about 110.

Notice what that means. Non-food is now the faster-rising side, by a clear margin, at 9.89% against 6.40% for food.

And one last detail changes how the dip looks. In October last year, inflation was 8.17%. Even after easing, this October's 8.27% is 0.10 percentage points above it.

Key facts

  • General inflation eased to 8.27% in October from 8.34% in September.
  • Food inflation fell to 6.40% from 7.22%, down 0.82 percentage points.
  • Non-food inflation rose to 9.89% from 9.30%, up 0.59 percentage points.
  • October last year's inflation was 8.17%, so this October is 0.10 points higher.

The world admits it will overshoot 1.5 °C. Now comes the hard part

COP31 opens with the UN saying the 1.5 °C limit will be passed, so the argument moves to how to bring temperatures back down.

What do you do when the world's headline climate promise is going to be broken? That is the question hanging over COP31, the UN climate meeting that opens today in Antalya, Turkey.

The promise came from the 2015 Paris agreement: keep warming within 1.5 °C above pre-industrial temperatures. Last month the UN Environment Programme said that under all plausible emissions paths, the world will pass that mark. Its Limiting Overshoot report is the first UN report to say outright that overshoot is inevitable.

Here's the twist. The same report says the story does not end there. It lays out how to bring temperatures back below 1.5 °C this century.

Think of a bath that has already started to overflow. You have to turn the tap down to near zero, and you also have to bail water out. For the climate, bailing means removing hundreds of billions of tonnes of CO2 from the air, through removal technologies or nature-based approaches such as reforestation.

So why does this matter at COP? Clea Schumer of the World Resources Institute expects the idea of limiting the "magnitude and the duration" of overshoot to shape the talks. Last year's COP30 in Belém had some language on limiting overshoot, but CO2 removal mostly went unmentioned.

Here's the catch. Oliver Geden, a researcher in Berlin, says most governments are glad they no longer have to pretend we will make it to 1.5 °C. But on how to limit overshoot, he says, "nobody is talking about" it.

Steve Smith at the University of Oxford adds a number worth remembering: zero. No country has put clearly quantified CO2 removal targets in the climate commitments it sent to the UN. He says such targets would be necessary for removal at the scale needed.

CO2 removal has long been controversial, partly over fears it could distract from cutting emissions. Yet the Natural Resources Defense Council, once skeptical, now urges environmentalists to back it the way they backed renewable energy.

Johan Rockström, a climate scientist in Germany, calls this "a moment of self-critique" and says global policy has failed. He wants COP decisions made by majority vote, since agreements now need every country to agree, which lets a small number of states block what most want.

COP31 runs for 12 days. Most governments seem to agree overshoot is coming. Who will commit, in numbers, to pulling the carbon back out?

Key facts

  • The UN Environment Programme's Limiting Overshoot report, out last month, says overshooting 1.5 °C is now inevitable.
  • The report says hundreds of billions of tonnes of CO2 must be removed this century, alongside near-zero emissions.
  • No country has included clearly quantified CO2 removal targets in its commitments to the UN, says Steve Smith.
  • COP31 runs for 12 days in Antalya, Turkey.

The day white supremacists overthrew a city government in 1898

In 1898, white supremacists seized power in Wilmington and massacred black Americans, and the idea behind it had been written into American law for over a century.

Overthrowing a government sounds like something that happens to countries, not to a city. Yet on this day in 1898, white supremacists seized power in Wilmington and massacred black Americans.

Here's my honest limit. The material I have covers the belief behind the massacre, not the events of the day itself, so I won't invent details. What I can do is explain the idea that drove it.

White supremacy is the belief that white people are superior to those of other races, and it favors defending any power and privilege white people hold. It grew from 18th-century scientific racism, a doctrine now discredited.

So why does a belief matter this much? Because it did not stay a belief. It was put into effect through structures: the Atlantic slave trade, Jim Crow laws in the United States, apartheid in South Africa.

In the United States it was dominant before and after the Civil War, and it persisted for decades after Reconstruction, the years that followed the war. Wilmington happened inside that long stretch.

Look at how early the law joined in. The Naturalization Act of 1790 limited U.S. citizenship to whites only. That was 108 years before Wilmington.

In some parts of the country, people considered non-white were kept from voting, barred from government office, and shut out of most government jobs well into the second half of the 20th century.

That is why the term now stretches further than violence. In academic usage it also means a system in which white people hold structural advantages even when the law says everyone is formally equal.

  • The Naturalization Act of 1790 limited U.S. citizenship to whites only.

Learn · Physics from first principles

Open problems: how physics keeps a list of what it can't explain

In 1900 Lord Kelvin named two clouds over physics. Both turned into whole new theories.

Two clouds. That is how Lord Kelvin, in a speech in 1900, described what classical physics still could not handle. One was the lack of evidence for the luminiferous aether. The other was the failure of the equipartition theorem to predict the heat capacity of solids.

Start with something you already do. Ask "why" enough times about anything, and you reach a spot where the answer is "nobody knows yet." Physics has a habit of writing those spots down, and this last lesson is about that list.

Here's the twist on Kelvin. Both clouds were solved within a decade. The aether problem fell to special relativity, and the heat capacity problem fell to quantum mechanics. Two puzzles that looked small turned out to be doors to two big theories.

Now a worked example with real numbers. In 1984 the mathematical physicist Barry Simon proposed a list of 15 open problems on quantum operators. By 2000, about 5 were solved, so about 10 of the 15 were still open after 16 years.

In 2000 he released a second list of 15, and at least 3 of those have been solved since. That leaves at most 12 open. The lists do shrink, just slowly, and the list-makers keep going: Penrose wrote one in 1982, Bartnik one of 53 problems in 1988, and Vitaly Ginzburg kept one from 1971 to 2005 that ended at 30. Ginzburg also named three great problems: the arrow of time, the interpretations of quantum mechanics, and life arising from physics alone.

So what is on the list now? Some problems are theoretical, meaning our theories can't explain something we observe. Others are experimental, meaning nobody has yet built the experiment to test an idea. The big ones include the nature of dark matter and dark energy, the absolute mass of neutrinos, and why there is more matter than antimatter.

Here's the biggest catch. The mathematics of the Standard Model is inconsistent with general relativity, and both theories break down in extreme places, like the Big Bang and the centers of black holes beyond their event horizons. Kelvin's clouds looked small, and each one hid a new theory. So which of today's problems is the next cloud, and what will it turn into?

Key facts

  • In 1900 Lord Kelvin named two clouds over classical physics; both were solved within a decade by special relativity and quantum mechanics.
  • Barry Simon's 1984 list of 15 problems had about 5 solved by 2000; his second list of 15 had at least 3 solved.
  • The Standard Model's mathematics is inconsistent with general relativity.
  • The 10 millennium problems proposed at Strings 2000 had none conclusively solved as of 2025.

The Day, Connected

The news is the weather. The climate is underneath.

A closing page on what a pile of headlines says about the slow things moving beneath them.

Most of what landed this morning looks like events, but almost none of it started today. A headline is the part that breaks the surface. The slow thing under it has usually been building for months or years.

Start with who gets trusted. A court says a judge probably had no say over a border wall. A detector now checks an AI paper's ideas because its wording no longer proves who wrote it. A ceasefire body says Israel's flood steps were not enough. Everywhere, the question underneath is the same: whose word counts, and who gets to decide?

Then what is fragile. A crane drops a boiler in a Turkish steel plant. Hackers reach the software keeping French supermarket food cold. Delhi's air passes 58 times the safe limit even with "green" fireworks. The weak spot is rarely the dramatic one. It is the ordinary system everyone assumed would just keep working.

And old pressure keeps coming back. Hasina is refused a passport while the government pushes for extradition. Iran and Washington trade claims about whether a war is over. The UN admits 1.5 degrees will be passed. These are old arguments in new clothes, because the thing that caused them never got settled.

Here's the odd part about the news itself. It rewards the sudden, so it hands you the blast and skips the decades of corner-cutting, the vote and skips the years of bargaining. You see the wave and miss the tide.

So here is a question you can ask of any headline: what slow thing had to be true for this to happen today?

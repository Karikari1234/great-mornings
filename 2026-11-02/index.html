Six people will now decide what a garment worker earns

The government has formed a six-member wage board to set the garment industry's minimum wage, while workers protesting for Tk30,000 a month keep blocking roads.

Six people have been handed a question that workers are shouting about in the street: what is the lowest a garment worker can be paid each month?

The government formed the board on Sunday. Labour and Employment Minister Ariful Haque Choudhury announced it at a press briefing and urged the protesting workers to go back to work instead of taking to the streets.

The workers' demand is a minimum monthly wage of Tk30,000. On Sunday they blocked the Mirpur-10 intersection in Dhaka and several points of the Dhaka-Tangail Highway in Gazipur, disrupting traffic.

Here's the catch. In Gazipur, clashes between workers and police led authorities to close most factories in the affected areas.

So who sits on this board? Think of it as a table with three sides: a neutral chair, the employers and the workers.

The chairman is Senior District and Sessions Judge Sheikh Ashfakur Rahman. The neutral member is Prof Dr Mosleh Uddin Ahmed, from the Department of Public Administration at Dhaka University.

Each side then gets two seats. For employers, there is Asif Ayub of the Bangladesh Employers Federation and ABM Shamsuddin, a director of the BGMEA, the garment manufacturers' association.

For workers, there is Anwar Hossain, president of the Bangladesh Jatiyatabadi Sramik Dal, and Babul Akhtar, general secretary of the Bangladesh Garments and Industrial Workers Federation.

The board is set up under Section 138 of the Bangladesh Labour Act, 2006, amended in 2026. Its job, under Section 139, is to determine the minimum wage for garment workers.

Count the seats and you get two for employers, two for workers and two in the middle. The source does not say when the board must decide, or whether the protesters will accept the process. That is the thing to watch.

Bangladesh Bank shut its own website as a precaution, with no breach

Bangladesh Bank is preparing an AI-based cybersecurity policy for banks, after taking its own website offline over a warning, not an attack.

Bangladesh Bank switched off its own website on 22 October. Nobody had hacked it.

A day earlier, agencies issued a joint alert about serious vulnerabilities in PHP, a widely used programming language for web applications. The warning said attackers could use the flaws to run malicious code on affected servers.

The advice was to take precautions where security updates could not be applied promptly. The central bank took the site down, and its IT team watched the situation until the site came back at 8:00 am on 25 October.

Here's the catch: the bank's spokesperson, Arief Hossain Khan, told BSS that no bank website had been hacked and there was no risk to banking transactions. The shutdown was a fire drill for a fire that had not started.

Separately, the bank is set to introduce an AI-based cybersecurity policy, and it has formed a committee of government and bank officials, private sector representatives, university teachers and IT experts to write it.

Why AI? Because it cuts both ways. Security teams can use it to sift huge volumes of data and spot unusual transaction patterns. Attackers can use it to write convincing phishing messages, automate attacks and impersonate real people or organisations.

The policy is meant to guide banks and financial institutions on updating their IT, improving threat detection and tightening security. Muhammad Zakir Hasan, who runs the bank's ICT department, said the policy would come soon.

It builds on something already in motion. On 29 March 2026 the bank issued its Cybersecurity Framework, Version 1.0, covering banks, finance companies, mobile financial service providers and payment firms. They have until 31 December 2026 to put it in place.

Hasan also said Bangladeshi banks had not faced any related problems so far, and called cyber risk management a continuous process.

What the AI policy will actually require is still open. The source says its provisions depend on the committee's recommendations and the final text.

Key facts

  • Bangladesh Bank is set to introduce an AI-based cybersecurity policy and has formed a committee to draft it.
  • The bank took its website offline on 22 October after a PHP vulnerability alert and restored it at 8:00 am on 25 October.
  • The Cybersecurity Framework, Version 1.0 (2026), issued 29 March, must be implemented by 31 December 2026.
  • No bank website had been hacked, according to the bank's spokesperson.

130 million names removed from India's draft voter rolls, and the opposition wants the referee gone

Tens of thousands rallied in Delhi demanding the chief election commissioner resign over a voter-list revision that has removed more than 130 million names from draft rolls.

More than 130 million names have been removed from India's draft electoral rolls. That is the number at the centre of a fight that filled a Delhi ground with tens of thousands of people on Sunday.

The exercise is called the Special Intensive Revision, or SIR. The Election Commission says it clears out duplicate entries and the names of people who have died, moved away or are otherwise ineligible. Anyone wrongly dropped can seek to have their name restored.

Here's the catch. The opposition says the cuts are removing legitimate voters, and that this could help the governing BJP in future elections. The BJP and the Election Commission deny it.

So the opposition INDIA bloc, led by Rahul Gandhi's Congress party, held a rally at Ramlila Maidan, a big ground in central Delhi known for major political events. Protesters chanted "Save the vote, save the country" and "Vote thief, give up power."

Sonia Gandhi, Mallikarjun Kharge, Mamata Banerjee, Akhilesh Yadav, Mehbooba Mufti, Supriya Sule and Tejashwi Yadav also attended. Their target is Chief Election Commissioner Gyanesh Kumar. Kharge said the campaign would go on until Kumar resigns.

In September, The Indian Express reported that Kumar made key decisions on the revision despite repeated objections from the two other election commissioners. The poll panel said differences of opinion are normal in any institution and do not affect its functioning.

Senior BJP leader JP Nadda called the rally a "calculated conspiracy to plunge the nation into anarchy - in the name of democracy."

Delhi Police set conditions. According to local media, organisers had to end by 5pm, install CCTV cameras and keep protesters inside the grounds. The BBC described the atmosphere as largely peaceful.

Why not just vote him out? Because he is not elected. Under Indian law, the chief election commissioner can only be removed through a formal parliamentary process. On Saturday the alliance staged a symbolic impeachment, which had no legal standing.

That leaves the protests as pressure, not a mechanism. Kumar has not resigned, and the opposition says it will keep going.

A Ukraine truce held for six days with no violations, then ended anyway

A local ceasefire around Oleshky in Kherson region ended Sunday night with no reported violations, and Russia reportedly refused to extend it.

For six days, nobody reported a single violation of a ceasefire in Ukraine's Kherson region. Then it ended anyway, with no sign of an extension.

Here's the setup. The truce covered Oleshky, a Russian-occupied town, and the area around it. An estimated 2,000 civilians were in Oleshky when it began, and the town was besieged.

Reports describe it as the longest multi-day truce between Russia and Ukraine. Residents could leave for Ukrainian-held territory, leave for Russian-held territory, or stay with replenished food and medical supplies. Hundreds were evacuated in a rare joint operation between Kyiv and Moscow.

Who arranged it? Mediation by United States First Lady Melania Trump, with Pope Leo XIV involved. She wrote that residents got freedom to move, food, clean water and medicine. "Let Oleshky become the starting point for lasting peace," she said.

Here's the catch. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said Moscow viewed the operation positively. Yet a Ukrainian official told AFP that Russia reportedly refused an offer from Kyiv's Foreign Ministry to extend the truce.

So Moscow viewed it positively, and it still stopped. The ceasefire expired Sunday at 10:59pm Ukraine time.

In Kherson city, which Ukraine still controls, residents stayed calm. Oleksandr Knyha, who runs the city's music and drama theater, opened a concert by telling the audience, "Don't be afraid, keep holding on." He is from Oleshky and has not given up hope of returning home.

Governor Oleksandr Prokudin warned people to stay away from areas near the Dnipro River, where he said Russian strikes remain intense.

The war never paused elsewhere. Ukraine's Air Force said Russia launched 433 drones overnight, and 371 were destroyed or suppressed. Zelenskyy said attacks on power infrastructure in four regions cut electricity to hundreds of thousands of households.

A drone also hit the grounds of a vocational school in Vinnytsia region, killing three people, including a 13-year-old girl. Four others were wounded.

Ukraine hit back too. Russia's governor of Nizhny Novgorod said more than 100 Ukrainian drones were shot down there, and Russian media reported a blaze at the Kstovo oil refinery.

No extension is in sight. Whether another pause ever comes is the thing nobody can answer yet.

DeepSeek's prototype AI agent listens in on chats and fixes code unasked

Chinese start-up DeepSeek has shown an early, open-source "Super-agent" that spots coding problems in team chats and works on them with little human help, though its real-world quality is untested.

What if your AI coding helper didn't wait for you to ask? What if it sat in your team's group chat, noticed a problem, and came back with a fix?

That's the pitch from DeepSeek, a Chinese AI start-up. Its Harness team published a plan for a "Super-agent" on Saturday, October 31, along with open-source code for a research prototype. It's an early version, not a finished product.

Quick background. An AI agent is an AI model plus software tools, so it can actually do tasks and not just chat. A harness is the software wrapped around the model that helps it handle bigger, messier jobs.

Here's the problem DeepSeek is going after. When you run several agents at once, you end up babysitting them: handing out jobs and checking on their progress. Its manual puts the goal plainly: "Take as little human attention as possible, and give as much value as possible."

So how does it work? Think of a busy kitchen. A Router is the person at the pass, taking orders and handing them out. Workers are the cooks, each working on a separate dish at the same time. A Watchdog checks for broken equipment and for any cook who's getting nowhere.

The prototype is small. It works with one GitHub repository and listens to Feishu, a workplace chat app. When an authorized work request comes in, it starts the job in its own session and can post a pull request (a proposed code change) back in the same thread. A web panel lists anything that needs a person's attention.

Does it work? In DeepSeek's early tests, each run had about 300 messages, of which about 20 were work requests. That's roughly one real job in every 15 messages, with the rest being chatter it has to ignore. Half the tasks finished within 12.4 minutes, and 95 percent finished within 15.7.

Here's the catch. DeepSeek says the tests used made-up workloads in a simplified setup, and didn't measure how well each task was done. So we know it's quick. We don't know if it gets things right, or how it stacks up against Anthropic's Claude Code or OpenAI's coding tools.

DeepSeek also lists open problems: scaling up, long-term memory, security and privacy, including the risks of a cloud agent getting into your computer. It hasn't said when, or whether, this becomes a product.

One more thing. DeepSeek says an internal version is already running, helping fix problems, answering code reviews and building DeepSeek Harness itself. The agent that doesn't wait to be asked is already helping build its own toolkit.

Key facts

  • DeepSeek's Harness team published its Super-agent plan and an open-source prototype on October 31, 2026.
  • The design splits work between a Router that hands out tasks, Workers that run them in parallel, and a Watchdog that spots stalled work.
  • In early tests, median task time was 12.4 minutes, but DeepSeek says the tests used synthetic workloads and did not measure task quality.
  • DeepSeek has not said when, or whether, Super-agent will become a product.

Germany missed its winter gas storage target because storing didn't pay

Germany's gas stores were about 61% full on November 1, short of a roughly 70% target, because the Iran war made summer filling uneconomical, and the storage industry now urges an early debate on forcing suppliers to hold stocks.

Germany has a rule that says its gas stores should be well filled by November 1, ready for winter. This year they missed it. On November 1, 2026, they were about 61% full, against an average target of about 70%.

Here's the strange part. Under the law, missing that target brings no immediate sanctions, according to Tim Kehler of the German gas and water industry association. And the government says supply for this winter is secure anyway.

So why have a target at all? And why did the stores come up short?

First, what storage is for. Gas storage is a reserve you draw down when winter demand rises. Germany lost the Russian pipeline gas it had long relied on after Russia invaded Ukraine in 2022.

Here's the catch. Storing gas only appeals to traders if summer gas is cheaper than winter gas. Think of buying a winter coat in a summer sale. If the coat costs the same or more in July as in December, why buy it early and keep it in the cupboard?

This summer there was no sale. Reuters reported that the Iran war's effect on gas prices made filling storage uneconomical, and that this was the main reason levels are low.

So is Germany in trouble? Officials say no. Energy regulator chief Klaus Müller said hitting the targets was no longer essential, because Germany can also bring in gas through liquefied natural gas terminals. It gets pipeline gas from Norway and neighbouring European countries too. Müller did warn that gas has become considerably more expensive for customers.

Kehler offered a number that helps. German stores hold about 150 terawatt-hours of gas, which is more than was withdrawn over the previous winter.

The storage industry still isn't satisfied. "The failure to meet storage level targets shows that storage levels for coming winters must be guaranteed more rigorously," said Sebastian Heinermann, managing director of the storage association INES.

His fix: start talking early about a legal obligation for gas suppliers to hold stocks. He said the Economy Ministry was open to the idea.

A particle physicists ignored may explain why anything exists

A theory says a long-dismissed heavy neutrino briefly ruled the early universe and helped matter beat antimatter, though it is still only a theory.

What if the reason you exist is a particle physicists wrote off as background noise?

That is the claim of a new study from the University of Southampton and the Indian Institute of Technology Kanpur. It is published in Physical Review Letters, and it targets a very old puzzle.

Here is the puzzle. The early universe made matter and antimatter in nearly equal amounts. When the two meet, they annihilate and release energy. If the amounts had been perfectly equal, almost everything would have vanished, and there would be no stars, planets or people.

Something tipped the balance. The early universe ended up with slightly more matter than antimatter, and that small leftover is everything we see. Physicists are still trying to explain why.

So where does the forgotten particle come in? The team looked at a hypothetical heavy cousin of the neutrino, called a decoupled heavy neutrino. It barely interacts with anything, so earlier explanations left it out.

Here's the catch. It also decays incredibly slowly. Dr. Rishav Roshan says that as the cosmos cooled, the particle lived long enough to temporarily take over the universe's energy density and trigger a matter-dominated period.

Think of a temperature as a switch. Roshan says the particle shifted the temperature at which particles like electrons interacted with their antiparticles, with help from the Higgs boson. That switch shapes how the imbalance was built up.

Wait, what? Something once dismissed as irrelevant may have set the timing for the whole thing.

There may be a way to check. Dr. Sudipta Show says that shift in the early expansion would leave detectable imprints on primordial gravitational waves, which are ripples in space and time from the extremely early universe.

For now, this is theory only. If those ancient ripples ever show the predicted pattern, a particle nobody bothered with could turn out to be the reason there is anything here to ask the question.

Key facts

  • Researchers at the University of Southampton and IIT Kanpur propose that a decoupled heavy neutrino helped matter outnumber antimatter.
  • The particle decays so slowly that it may have temporarily dominated the early universe's energy density.
  • The shift could leave detectable imprints on primordial gravitational waves.

A DNA word processor has now been used inside a patient's body

Prime editing, a gene editor that works like search and replace, has been given to its first patient inside the body, in a trial for Wilson disease.

Much gene editing is messier than you would think. The classic CRISPR tool cuts through both strands of DNA, and much of what we call "editing" is actually cells scrambling to repair the break.

Sometimes they botch it. In one example, a single cut caused the loss of large portions of a chromosome, which one expert called "genome vandalism".

So scientists built gentler tools. Base editing swaps one DNA letter for another without deliberately breaking the double helix. The catch is that it can only make certain types of swaps.

Enter prime editing, introduced in 2019. Think of it as a word processor for DNA. It nicks just one strand, a guide molecule called pegRNA carries the blueprint of the edit, and a third component copies those instructions into DNA the cell can take in.

In tests, it could swap any DNA letter for another, add new genetic code, or delete short stretches with high precision. In theory, it could correct up to 89 percent of known genetic variants linked to disease.

Here's the catch. Prime editors are hefty molecular machines, and getting them into the right cells is tricky.

Prime Medicine, based in Cambridge, Massachusetts, first used it on blood stem cells taken from two patients with a rare immune disorder. Both patients made working germ-fighting cells within a month. But each patient needed a custom batch of their own edited cells, and cost was part of why the company shifted its focus.

Now it is trying the harder thing: editing inside the body. In a global trial for Wilson disease, the editor rewrites DNA directly in liver cells. In Wilson disease, a faulty gene stops copper being shuttled out of liver cells, and the copper builds up and damages the liver and nerves.

The first patient received an infusion earlier this month. The company's chief medical officer says the aim is a one-time curative treatment, ideally before irreversible damage occurs.

Whether it is safe and whether it works won't be known until next year. That is the answer to watch for.

The Maya story we were told began with a friar who burned their books

Old ideas about the Maya leaned on a missionary who destroyed their codices, and two archaeologists digging in Belize have been testing them against the ground.

For a long time, the common picture of the Maya went like this: peaceful stargazers, absorbed in ceremony, whose great cities sat nearly empty. That picture turns out to be shaky, and its roots are odd.

For centuries, most scholarly assumptions about the Maya came from Friar Diego de Landa, a 16th-century Spanish missionary in the Yucatan. By his own account, he burned most of the Maya's hieroglyphic books. He wrote that they held "nothing but superstitions and falsehoods of the devil."

One scholar who translated him into English said the friar burned 99 times as much knowledge as he gave us. So the main witness was also the one who destroyed the archive.

De Landa also described the Maya as poor rural peasants ruled by a ruthless aristocracy. Archaeologist Arlen Chase has a theory for why. The Spaniards, he says, slapped the two-class model of 1500s Spain onto the Maya.

Later came the British archaeologist J. Eric S. Thompson, whose influential 1954 book wondered how the Maya could "chart the heavens yet fail to grasp the principle of the wheel." Anthropologists of the time believed Maya cities were near-empty husks.

Then Arlen and his wife Diane Chase started digging. From 1979 they spent six years at Santa Rita Corozal in Belize, and found that many ordinary households owned obsidian tools. Obsidian is a dark, glassy stone found only in distant volcanic regions.

Here's the catch for the old view. Scholars had assumed elites controlled the economy and handed out goods directly. But so many ordinary households had obsidian that gifts from the top seemed unlikely. A better explanation: Santa Rita was a trading junction, and its people could buy imports like jade, ceramics and shells.

Santa Rita had limits, though. Much of it had been bulldozed or lay under modern buildings. Caracol, in contrast, was once seen as a minor site barely worth the time, and it had barely been excavated when the Chases arrived in the 1980s.

Its remoteness turned out to be an asset. Tikal and Chichen Itza were first encountered by European colonists in the 19th century. Caracol stayed hidden in dense forest.

And here is the last detail. Caracol's 141-foot palace, Caana, was abandoned around the year 900 and then buried by jungle for a thousand years. It is still the tallest human-made structure in Belize.

  • Friar Diego de Landa said he burned most Maya hieroglyphic books, and his writings shaped scholarly assumptions for centuries.
  • At Santa Rita Corozal, many non-elite households owned imported obsidian, pointing to market trade.
  • Caracol had barely been excavated when the Chases arrived in the 1980s.

Learn · Physics from first principles

Open problems: how physics keeps a list of what it can't explain

The last lesson is about the gaps. Physicists write them down, and the lists show how they get filled.

Here's a myth worth dropping on the final day: that physics is a finished book with a few pages left to proofread. Drop a spoon into hot tea and it warms up, and you would think we had that sorted. In 1900, how much heat a solid soaks up was one of two problems Lord Kelvin named in a speech about classical physics.

He called them "two clouds" hanging over the physics of the 20th century. One was the lack of evidence for the luminiferous aether. The other was the failure of the equipartition theorem to predict the heat capacity of solids, which is the spoon-in-the-tea question.

Here's the twist. Both clouds were solved within a decade. The aether cloud fell to special relativity, and the heat cloud fell to quantum mechanics. Two awkward leftovers turned into two whole new branches of physics.

That's why physicists keep lists. Isaac Newton did it first in this story: his book Opticks (first edition 1704) ended up with 31 queries about light and matter. Later people did the same with sharper aims. In 1984 Barry Simon listed 15 open problems on quantum operators.

Now do the arithmetic, because it shows how slowly this goes. By 2000, about 5 of Simon's 15 were solved. That's one in three after 16 years, with 10 still open. He then released a second list of 15 in 2000, and at least 3 of those have since been solved. Penrose's 1982 list on general relativity still had about 14 unsolved in 2017, and the Strings 2000 conference's list of 10 had none conclusively solved as of 2025.

So what's on the list today? Some problems are theoretical, where our theories can't explain what we observe. Others are experimental, where nobody has yet built a way to test an idea. Examples include the absolute mass of neutrinos, why there's more matter than antimatter, and what dark matter and dark energy are.

The biggest one sits inside the Standard Model, our current best theory of particles. Its mathematics is inconsistent with general relativity, and both theories break down at the Big Bang and at the centers of black holes. Vitaly Ginzburg added three "great problems" to his list: the arrow of time, how to interpret quantum mechanics, and whether life can arise from physics alone. Which of today's clouds will turn out to be the next relativity?

Key facts

  • In 1900 Lord Kelvin named two "clouds" over physics, and both were solved within a decade by special relativity and quantum mechanics.
  • Of Barry Simon's 15 problems from 1984, about 5 were solved by 2000.
  • The Standard Model is inconsistent with general relativity, and both break down at black hole centers and the Big Bang.
  • The 10 problems proposed at Strings 2000 had none conclusively solved as of 2025.

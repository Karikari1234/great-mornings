Novaya Gazeta found a 999-copy loophole to keep publishing. Now it is closing

Russia's Novaya Gazeta, a paper that kept working inside the country for years through legal workarounds, says it will close permanently next month.

Novaya Gazeta printed its magazines in runs of 999 copies. One more copy and it would have needed an official licence.

That mattered because the paper's licence had been taken away in September 2022, and soon after its website was blocked in Russia. But a Russian law says magazines under 1,000 copies need no licence. So Dmitry Muratov, who won the Nobel peace prize in 2021, and a core team stayed behind and kept publishing that way.

Here's who they are. Novaya launched in the 1990s and was partly funded for many years by the late Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev. It was known for long, dogged reporting on corruption and rights abuses. Six of its journalists have been assassinated, including Anna Politkovskaya, shot in the stairwell of her Moscow apartment block in 2006.

Then came the full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022. Novaya put out a bilingual Russian-Ukrainian edition calling the war "madness". When new censorship laws forced media to print only the official line, it printed blank white spaces instead.

By the end of March 2022 it stopped its print edition altogether. In April, Muratov was doused with red paint on a train, an attack that damaged his eyesight. US officials say Russian intelligence agencies ordered it.

Here's the catch. A loophole only works while nobody closes it. Now the paper says it is losing its newsroom, its donation platforms and its legal right to publish, and it will shut for good next month.

Also in April, journalist Oleg Roldugin was arrested and charged with "unlawful access to computerised information". He is under house arrest awaiting trial, and editor-in-chief Sergei Sokolov's home was searched as part of the case.

Sokolov wrote that the team managed to keep working "while being completely encircled by the security services" and now sits in a "kill zone". He wrote "war" as three stars, because Russian law says journalists must call it a "special military operation".

Elena Kostyuchenko spent 17 years at the paper. She called its survival "a miracle", but also "the daily choice of people who continued to go to work even when it was very scary".

So 999 copies was the whole trick. It held for years, and in the end staying under 1,000 was not enough.

Key facts

  • Novaya Gazeta says it will close permanently next month, citing intolerable pressure.
  • It lost its newsroom, its donation platforms and its legal right to publish.
  • Its team kept publishing magazines of 999 copies, under the 1,000-copy licence threshold.
  • Six of its journalists have been assassinated, including Anna Politkovskaya in 2006.

State Minister Humaiun Kobir says the PM's Tokyo visit was an economic partnership, with no third country discussed.

She also said she intends to send a business mission to Bangladesh in the near future.

The two leaders agreed to work for the early entry into force of the Japan-Bangladesh Economic Partnership Agreement. That is a trade deal. They also named cooperation on trade, investment, farm and fishery products, climate and aviation.

There was a security piece too. Takaichi announced Japan will provide a patrol vessel to boost Bangladesh's maritime security capabilities. The leaders also reaffirmed security and defence cooperation.

The countries also agreed to upgrade BIG-B to "BIG-B for Shared Prosperity", described as an initiative of "strategic cooperation". They also agreed to work to make the Bay of Bengal region, with Bangladesh as its hub, a "region of shared prosperity".

Responding to a question, Kobir said there was no scope for the government to go against the country's interests. On the Rohingya issue, he said Bangladesh keeps raising it, because the solution lies in Myanmar.

The leaders issued a joint statement after Tuesday's summit in Tokyo. Kobir's message, in short: the talks were about investment and shared prosperity.

Intel lost 29% in a day, its worst since the 1970s

Intel gave a weak forecast, and a stock that had run up about 150% this year fell hard while other chip stocks also dropped.

Intel shares fell 29% on Friday, their worst day since the 1970s. If you had held $100 of the stock on Thursday night, you would have woken up with about $71.

The trigger was a forecast. Companies give investors guidance, their own estimate of the coming quarter, and markets often care about it more than past results. Intel said it expects revenue of $15 billion to $16 billion. Analysts had expected $17.1 billion.

Profit was the same story: Intel guided to adjusted earnings of 25 cents a share, against the 43 cents analysts wanted. So where did the gap come from?

Part of it is the PC market. Memory and other components have become much more expensive, and global PC shipments fell 4% in the second quarter from a year earlier, according to Counterpoint Research. Finance chief David Zinsner said Intel sees the slowdown continuing at least through the first half of next year.

The other part is a tragedy. An explosion at an Intel fab in Israel earlier this month killed two workers and seriously injured a third. CEO Lip-Bu Tan called it an "unfortunate and tragic accident" that caused an "extended shutdown," and Zinsner put the cost at about $600 million in lost revenue over two quarters.

Here's the catch. Intel was having a great year. Heading into the report, its shares were up about 150%, helped by demand for the central processors that AI workloads need. A stock that has risen that far has a long way to fall.

It also matters beyond Intel. The U.S. government took a 10% stake in the company over a year ago, and the value of that holding dropped about $12 billion on Friday, though the investment is still profitable. Nvidia's stake lost about $6 billion.

The pain spread. The Nasdaq Composite slid 2.9%, Qualcomm fell 11%, Broadcom more than 5% and Nvidia 4%. Alphabet had its worst day on record on Thursday, and Amazon slipped 1.4% while keeping a $220 billion spending forecast for 2026. Investors are getting nervous about whether the huge AI spending will pay off.

Tan's comeback plan leans on Intel's foundry business, which makes chips for other companies. Intel says it is now in high-volume production of its 18A chip at a new Arizona fab. Tan said on the call that a couple of customers should be ready to announce in the next two quarters.

Those announcements are the thing to watch. If they land, the foundry story gets real customers. If they don't, the foundry story stays unproven.

Key facts

  • Intel shares fell 29% on Friday, their worst day since the 1970s.
  • Intel guided to revenue of $15 billion to $16 billion versus the $17.1 billion analysts expected.
  • The U.S. government's 10% stake lost about $12 billion in value.
  • An explosion at an Israel fab will cost about $600 million in revenue over two quarters.

A physicist says catching a single graviton is possible after all

Igor Pikovski says the belief that one particle of gravity could never be detected rested on faulty assumptions, and he is building a prototype detector.

Physicists long believed you could never detect a single particle of gravity. Igor Pikovski, a quantum information scientist at Stevens Institute of Technology, says that belief rested on faulty assumptions.

First, the gap. Quantum physics was formulated more than a century ago, and it can describe almost everything in the universe except gravity. In the standard model, each interaction has a particle tied to it. Gravity's particle is the graviton, which is hypothetical and has never been observed.

So why was it thought undetectable? The intuition was that gravity matters for huge things like Earth, the sun and planets, while quantum effects show up at the scale of atoms and electrons. At that tiny scale, gravity would play no role. Pikovski says the thinking was that you might see a collection of gravitons, say 10 to 30, but never one.

Here's the catch. His team found that the way people calculated it was not optimized. There is a way, he says, to extract a single graviton from a very, very large number of them. They published the theory two years ago and called the old view an oversight.

Where do all those gravitons come from? Gravitational waves, which were detected 10 years ago. Light is the model here: a light wave can behave like a wave or like a particle, and Einstein explained in 1905 that it is made of tiny particles called photons. Pikovski says gravitational waves can be thought of in nearly the same way.

He describes a gravitational wave as a tsunami hitting the shore. It is so weakly interacting that it essentially travels through Earth, shaking a detector only a tiny bit. The goal is to fish one graviton out of that tsunami.

That is what the prototype is built on, and Pikovski says it can be scaled up to detect these particles one by one. He thinks a machine that could detect a graviton might be ready within a decade or two.

Then comes the bet. The hunt for the graviton has been overshadowed by the hunt for dark matter, but Pikovski says, "I would even wager that we'll find a graviton before we find dark matter."

Nobody can say yet whether that wager pays off. But the argument has moved from whether a single graviton can be caught at all to whether anyone can build the machine that does it.

The child emperor at the end of Rome's western empire

Romulus Augustus took the throne as a child, but the man who put him there held the power.

How did a child whom the Eastern Roman emperor never recognized become the person whose removal marks the end of the Western Roman Empire? Romulus Augustus took the western throne on 31 October 475. His father, Orestes, put him there.

Orestes was a senior military commander, and Romulus was still a minor. The boy carried the imperial title, but he served as little more than his father's figurehead. Even his nickname, "Augustulus," meant "little Augustus" and was a jab at his age.

Here's the complication. Orestes had pushed aside Julius Nepos, the emperor before Romulus. Nepos fled to Dalmatia and kept claiming the title. Zeno, the Eastern Roman emperor, never recognized Orestes's son. So who was emperor?

That sounds like a dispute over names. It was also a dispute over power. In Italy, allied troops called foederati demanded land where they could settle. Orestes refused. Under Odoacer, the troops defeated and killed him, then deposed Romulus on 4 September 476.

Romulus had ruled for about ten months. No significant law, policy or inscription from his reign is known. If you expected the last emperor to leave a final command that changed everything, the surviving record gives you almost nothing of the kind.

So why does his removal get such a large place in history? Odoacer did not name another western emperor. He became king of Italy and accepted Zeno as his nominal superior. With no successor in the West, historians typically treat Romulus as its last emperor.

That closes the puzzle, though it leaves an odd picture. The western throne had no successor, while an emperor still ruled in the East. Some historians use Romulus's deposition to mark the transition from antiquity to the medieval period. The date is clear; the story around it is messier.

Romulus himself did not die in that overthrow. Odoacer spared him and let him retire to a fortress in Campania. What happened to him afterward is uncertain; the records give no firm account of his later life.

The event used to mark the end of the Western Roman Empire, then, was a child losing a title he had held for ten months, after the father who put him on the throne was killed.

Key facts

  • Orestes placed his minor son Romulus Augustus on the western throne on 31 October 475.
  • Julius Nepos continued to claim the title, and Eastern Roman emperor Zeno never recognized Romulus.
  • Odoacer's forces killed Orestes and deposed Romulus on 4 September 476.

That sounds simple until you try it. Think of a phone battery running down just trying to close an app that keeps restarting in the background. When your own thoughts turn on you, wrestling with them every hour burns through all your energy.

Back in the 1950s, scientists were only beginning to understand brain chemistry and genetics. Plath wondered in her letter whether her family was simply prone by blood to self-blame, or if her mind was something she could steer through sheer willpower.

Plath died by suicide in February 1963, at age thirty-one. For decades, many readers have treated her story as a tragedy defined entirely by its final chapter, as though her death proved that her illness won in the end.

Here is the flaw in that view. It assumes that dying after years of mental illness is a personal failure. In truth, blaming someone who struggles with mental illness makes no sense when the instrument itself is damaged.

No one can truly know what happens inside another person's consciousness. If your own brain runs smoothly, it is hard to grasp what it feels like to face misfiring neurotransmitters every morning.

To survive even one day with that kind of internal noise is an achievement. Plath did it for thirty-one years, which is more than 11,000 days in a row, while filling those years with poems that outlived her.

Remember that bleak January letter where she told her mother that your present life is the important thing. She held that nagging voice at bay across three decades, and every single day she stayed to write was a quiet victory.

Learn · Physics from first principles

Why the major rules of physics still disagree

Our two best theories of the universe break down when forced together, leaving fundamental questions wide open.

It is easy to assume that modern physics is a finished map where every rule fits together. Every day, you see gravity pull things downward while atoms stay solid. The catch is that the mathematical framework for particles, called the Standard Model, is inconsistent with general relativity.

Unsolved problems in physics fall into two camps: theoretical and experimental. Theoretical puzzles mean our existing theories cannot explain what we observe, while experimental ones mean we lack the tools to test a theory or inspect a phenomenon closely. Think of it like a kitchen where a recipe produces the wrong dish or calls for an oven nobody can build yet.

When theories disagree, they can survive side by side until they meet at extreme edges. In our universe, the Standard Model and general relativity break down completely at gravitational singularities, such as the Big Bang and the centers of black holes beyond their event horizons. That breakdown leaves open questions like the nature of dark matter, dark energy, and the absolute mass of neutrinos.

So how fast do these lists actually get cleared? In 1984, mathematical physicist Barry Simon wrote down 15 open problems on quantum operators. By the year 2000, physicists had solved about 5 of them, meaning 10 remained unsolved after 16 years of effort.

History shows that clearing these lists can be slow. In 1900, Lord Kelvin pointed to two major clouds over classical physics, and both were solved within a single decade. By contrast, when physicists at the Strings 2000 conference proposed 10 millennium problems in theoretical physics, not a single one had been conclusively solved 25 years later in 2025.

That leaves physics with deep cracks instead of a tidy ending. Nobel laureate Ginzburg even included three great problems: the arrow of time, the interpretations of quantum mechanics, and life arising from physics alone. If our two master theories fail when forced together, which one must change when someone finally unites them?

Key facts

  • The Standard Model is mathematically inconsistent with general relativity, causing both to break down at gravitational singularities like black holes and the Big Bang.
  • Lord Kelvin identified two major physics problems in 1900 that were both solved within a decade.
  • None of the 10 millennium problems in theoretical physics proposed at the Strings 2000 conference have been conclusively solved as of 2025.
  • Mathematical physicist Barry Simon proposed 15 open problems in 1984, of which about 5 were solved by 2000.

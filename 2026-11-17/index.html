Drones strike Russia's heavily defended Crimea bridge

Russia reported intercepting 2,049 Ukrainian drones in a day as its Crimea bridge was damaged and Kyiv faced repeated air raid alerts.

Days after envoys met to discuss ending the war, Russia said it had intercepted and destroyed 2,049 Ukrainian drones in 24 hours. Drones also struck a bridge Russia protects with layers of air and sea defences. How did the crossing end up damaged?

The Kerch Bridge links Russia to Crimea, the Ukrainian peninsula Russia invaded and annexed in 2014. Russia built the bridge afterward, and President Vladimir Putin inaugurated it in 2018. It is one of his signature infrastructure projects.

Sergei Aksyonov, the Moscow-installed head of Crimea, confirmed that drones struck the bridge and traffic was restricted. Russia described the damage as minor. Russian pro-war commentators gave a more specific account: they said drone warhead debris damaged the bridge, rather than a direct strike.

Ukraine's domestic security service and main military intelligence directorate had not responded to the BBC's request for comment. So the precise way the bridge was damaged is still unclear. Aksyonov's account confirms that the crossing was affected despite its defences.

The scale of the attacks is hard to hold in your head. Russia's military said it intercepted and destroyed 2,049 drones and four long-distance Flamingo missiles over 24 hours. Moscow's mayor said around 600 drones flew toward the capital alone, one part of the reported wave.

Kyiv, meanwhile, faced a record 17 air raid alerts in a single day. At least seven people were killed by Russian strikes, and attacks on energy infrastructure left 50,000 people without power. The fighting reached well beyond the bridge.

Here's the catch with the talks: meeting does not stop the attacks. US, Ukrainian and Russian envoys had held talks days earlier. Moscow still demands that Ukraine cede territory and give up its effort to join Nato.

European governments want a place in future talks about their security, too. Britain, France, Germany, Italy, Poland and a representative of EU institutions will represent Europe. The Kremlin has questioned how much their position would help the peace process.

The bridge drew attention because it was struck despite its defences. On the same day, attacks on energy infrastructure cut power to 50,000 people. The talks and the fighting are happening at once, with consequences that reach into people's homes.

  • Russia said it intercepted and destroyed 2,049 Ukrainian drones over 24 hours.
  • Crimea's Moscow-installed head confirmed drones struck the Kerch Bridge and traffic was restricted.
  • Kyiv faced 17 air raid alerts in one day.
  • Russian strikes killed at least seven people; attacks on energy infrastructure caused blackouts for 50,000.

December starts in two weeks. Whether she actually comes, and what happens to her if she does, are questions nobody has answered yet.

Xi, Trump and Putin to discuss 'world peace,' China says

The three leaders are due to meet in China, but Ukraine may go unmentioned and they disagree on where nuclear arms cuts should begin.

Three presidents are due to discuss "world peace" in China this week. Yet US officials say Donald Trump is not expected to raise Russia's war in Ukraine. How do you hold a peace discussion while leaving a war off the table?

That question hangs over a meeting China has announced for Thursday. Trump, China's Xi Jinping and Russia's Vladimir Putin are due to meet in Shenzhen during an Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit. China calls it their first trilateral summit, but has offered few details about what they will actually discuss.

Beijing says the leaders will have "in-depth discussions" about preserving peace and keeping relations stable. That's a wide brief. Trump has named denuclearization and the war in Iran as possible subjects, while avoiding Ukraine in his brief remarks about the meeting.

Ukraine is where the choice of who sits at the table becomes hard to ignore. German President Frank-Walter Steinmeier said Xi called it a European conflict and said Europeans must be present if it is discussed. German Chancellor Friedrich Merz has asked Trump for no "surprises" on Ukraine with Putin.

Putin may want a very different conversation. According to a Wall Street Journal report cited by DW, he wanted the talks to produce a Ukraine peace agreement that would serve Moscow's interests. That report and the US officials' account point in opposite directions. For now, the agenda is unclear.

Then there are nuclear weapons. Washington wants China in future arms control deals, which set limits on countries' weapons. Beijing says the US and Russia must reduce their arsenals first. China also rejected Trump's proposal for broader disarmament talks after a US-Russia nuclear treaty expired in February.

So even if all three leaders agree to talk about denuclearization, they do not agree on who should move first. UN Secretary-General Antonio Guterres said a meaningful commitment from the three would be a great achievement. A discussion is still a long way from that commitment.

Iran brings another war into the room. Trump said on November 8 that he was trying to end it. The war began with US and Israeli strikes on February 28; Pakistan-mediated US-Iran peace talks in April did not produce a lasting settlement. China, an economic partner of Iran, has sought to mediate.

Thursday's meeting could cover all of these subjects, or fewer of them. China has promised a discussion about peace. The question now is which conflicts the three presidents will actually discuss, and who else gets a seat when they do.

Key facts

  • Xi, Trump and Putin are due to meet Thursday in Shenzhen.
  • US officials say Trump is not expected to raise Ukraine.
  • The US wants China in future nuclear arms control deals; China says the US and Russia should cut their arsenals first.

Oil got cheaper even as Middle East shipping attacks continue

Oil prices fell more than 3% as Middle East exports recover faster than demand, even with tensions unresolved.

Why would oil get cheaper while ships in the Middle East are still being attacked? That's what happened on Monday, and the answer tells you a lot about how oil prices work.

First, the drop. Brent crude, the international benchmark price for oil, fell 3.55% to $92.44 a barrel. That's about $3.40 off every barrel in a single day. The main U.S. price, West Texas Intermediate, settled down 3.58% at $85.39.

So what changed? Shipments through the Strait of Hormuz recovered. That's the narrow channel between the Persian Gulf and the Gulf of Oman, and a large share of the world's seaborne oil passes through it. When it clogs, prices jump. When it flows, they ease.

Here's the catch. The trouble hasn't ended. Tensions between Iran and the U.S. remain at a stalemate, and the International Energy Agency says shipping attacks continue.

That's the clever bit. Prices respond to one simple question: will there be more oil than people want to buy, or less?

Think of a fruit stall near closing time. If there's more fruit than shoppers, the price drops, even if a delivery van got a flat tyre that morning.

And the oil is showing up. The IEA, an intergovernmental body that publishes monthly oil forecasts, said on Friday that Middle East exports are recovering faster than global demand. Its new outlook reverses its earlier forecast for a deficit, meaning a shortage.

How are producers managing with attacks still happening? Goldman Sachs points to two workarounds: ship-to-ship transfers, where oil moves between tankers at sea, and added pipeline capacity. The bank expects Middle East supply to climb above its 2025 average.

Russia is pumping more too. Its crude production rose by 210,000 barrels a day in October, to 8.73 million barrels a day, according to the IEA.

Where does that leave prices? Goldman expects Brent to average $88 a barrel in 2027 and fall to $78 by December 2027 as the market returns to a surplus. From Monday's price, that's roughly another $14 off each barrel.

What to watch: the IEA's next monthly report. If exports keep outrunning demand, the case for too much oil gets stronger. If attacks choke the strait again, Monday's question gets a different answer.

How Elizabeth I went from excluded child to queen

Declared illegitimate as a child and later imprisoned, Elizabeth became queen in 1558 and ruled for 44 years.

How did a child removed from the royal line end up giving her name to an era? Elizabeth was two when her mother, Anne Boleyn, was executed and she was declared illegitimate. Her claim to the throne was taken away.

On 17 November 1558, that same child became Queen of England and Ireland. She would rule until her death in 1603. The path between those two facts is the story worth following.

Her father, Henry VIII, restored Elizabeth to the line of succession when she was 10. But being in line did not make the crown certain. After Henry died, Elizabeth's younger half-brother Edward VI became king.

Edward tried to leave the crown to a cousin, Lady Jane Grey, passing over Elizabeth and their half-sister Mary. His will was set aside. Mary became queen, and during her reign Elizabeth was imprisoned for nearly a year on suspicion of supporting Protestant rebels.

So what changed when Elizabeth took the throne? In religion, she was relatively tolerant and avoided systematic persecution. That did not remove the danger around her: after the pope declared her illegitimate in 1570, conspiracies threatened her life.

Those plots were defeated with help from a secret service run by her minister Francis Walsingham. Abroad, Elizabeth manoeuvred between France and Spain. By the mid-1580s, England could no longer avoid war with Spain.

The name Elizabethan era can make a long reign sound like one tidy chapter. It held both the defeat of the Spanish Armada and a flowering of English drama, with playwrights including William Shakespeare and Christopher Marlowe.

It also held economic and military problems that weakened Elizabeth's popularity near the end. Her 44 years on the throne followed the short reigns of her half-siblings and gave the kingdom a stretch of stability.

That is the answer to the opening puzzle. Elizabeth did more than survive the loss of her place in line and a year of imprisonment. She ruled long enough for her reign, and its effect on history and culture, to lend an era her name.

Key facts

  • Elizabeth became Queen of England and Ireland on 17 November 1558.
  • She was declared illegitimate as a child and later restored to the line of succession.
  • During Mary's reign, Elizabeth was imprisoned for nearly a year.
  • Elizabeth ruled for 44 years, until her death in 1603.

What a warming solid can tell us about physics

An open problem shows where an explanation runs out, or where a proposed answer still needs a test.

Heat a solid object and it warms up. Simple enough. But physicists once had a rule that could not predict how much heat the object would take in. What do you do when a familiar thing stops fitting the rules?

In 1900, Lord Kelvin pointed to that problem and another: physicists had no evidence for a proposed substance called aether. Within a decade, quantum mechanics had addressed the problem of a solid's heat capacity, and special relativity had addressed the aether problem. The two questions led to different changes in physics.

Some open problems begin with an observation that a theory cannot explain. Others begin with a proposed explanation that physicists cannot yet test with a suitable experiment. Either way, the question shows them where to look next.

And there are plenty of questions left. Physicists still seek the absolute mass of neutrinos, an explanation for why matter and antimatter are unequal, and answers to what dark matter and dark energy are.

Here is another kind of gap. The Standard Model describes particles; general relativity describes gravity. Their mathematical frameworks are incompatible, and both break down under extreme conditions associated with the Big Bang and the centers of black holes.

That is why an open problem is worth keeping. It points to the edge of what a theory can explain. Then comes the question that moves the work forward: what experiment could tell us whether a new explanation holds up?

Key facts

  • Open problems can be theoretical or experimental.
  • Kelvin identified two problems in 1900; special relativity and quantum mechanics addressed them within a decade.
  • The Standard Model and general relativity are mathematically incompatible.
  • Questions about neutrino mass, the inequality of matter and antimatter, dark matter and dark energy remain open.

A missing figure, an empty seat and a draft agreement show how much depends on the systems behind an event.

A missing figure delayed United departures while its planes already in the air kept flying. A whole trip can turn on something you cannot see from your seat. That is the puzzle running through this paper: why do events that look sudden so often have a long story underneath?

One part of the answer is that ordinary systems depend on trust. You need the right information before a plane takes off. You also need people to accept the decisions made by institutions they may never meet. Five surveys found more Americans negative about AI than positive. The questions differed, but that repeated answer suggests trust cannot be assumed just because a technology is moving ahead.

Then there is the question of who gets to decide. Europe had no formal voice at Ukraine peace talks, even as the talks produced a prisoner exchange. A result can be real while the argument over who belongs at the table stays open. The COP31 draft calls for an immediate shift away from fossil fuels and more help with adapting to climate change. Even if a deal is reached, the work it calls for will unfold long after the meeting ends.

That may be the useful way to read a morning paper. A delay, a deal or a draft is the visible moment. Beneath it are the slower tasks of keeping systems working, earning consent and deciding who makes the rules. Tomorrow will bring different headlines.

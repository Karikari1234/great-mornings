Cover story

Spain's deadly train crash followed a computer failure and an "inexplicable" decision

A train stopped by a computer failure was hit from behind, and Spain's transport minister blames a control room order to move again.

The train that was hit had already stopped. It was sitting on the tracks in central Spain when another train ran into it from behind, and the transport minister says the cause was an "inexplicable" decision.

At least 68 people died and dozens more were injured. It happened on Wednesday just after 09:00, around 140km south of Madrid, between the towns of Mora and Orgaz.

Here is the sequence, as Transport Minister Óscar Puente described it. A train run by the private operator Iryo, with 147 passengers, had stopped because of a computer failure.

When a system fails, a 24-hour control centre in Madrid takes over. Puente said that centre made an "inexplicable" decision to tell the trains to move again, with no speed restrictions.

A Renfe train, run by Spain's state-owned railway company and travelling from Madrid to Seville, then hit the Iryo train. It carried 397 passengers, so 544 people were on the two trains. Several carriages fell down an embankment, and one caught fire.

Passengers broke windows to get out. Salvador Arriza, 62, called the force "brutal" and said some people were trapped among the seats. Tangui Binier, 22, said "suitcases started flying and people were screaming."

Spain's Civil Guard said it will need DNA samples from relatives to identify some of the victims.

Here's the catch. This is not the first one this year. In January, a high-speed crash near Córdoba, on the same rail line but much further south, killed 46 people.

Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez called two serious crashes in a year "unacceptable" and vowed to "get to the bottom of this" and determine if there was any negligence. The BBC reports this is Spain's deadliest train disaster since 2013, when 80 people died near Santiago de Compostela.

The blame is already contested. The union CSIF demanded the minister resign, accusing him of "scapegoating" a traffic manager by pointing to human error in the control room.

So the question is who gave that order, and why. The minister calls it inexplicable.

Key facts

  • At least 68 people died in a collision between two trains near Mora and Orgaz, south of Madrid.
  • The minister says a computer failure, then a control centre order to move again, led to the crash.
  • A January crash near Córdoba on the same line killed 46 people.
  • The union CSIF has demanded the transport minister resign.

Banks wanted $800 million. Bangladesh Bank sold them $122 million

Bangladesh Bank sold $122 million from its reserves on 28 October to cool the dollar rate, but banks had asked for $800 million.

Commercial banks asked Bangladesh Bank for $800 million on 28 October. They got $122 million. That is about 15 cents for every dollar they wanted.

The central bank sold the dollars from its reserves at Tk124 to Tk124.30 each. Reserves are the foreign money a central bank keeps in store, and selling some of it into the market is a way to hold the dollar's price down.

Think of a shop owner with a stockroom. When a crowd pushes in and prices start climbing, the owner puts more stock on the shelf.

So why was the crowd so big? Payments totalling more than $300 million were due that day. Bankers also said remittances, the money Bangladeshis abroad send home, tend to be low near the end of the month.

Another senior banker pointed to large payment needs from Petrobangla and the Bangladesh Petroleum Corporation. Import orders have been rising too: banks opened letters of credit, which are promises to pay for imports, worth more than $8 billion in September, up from $6.65 billion in August.

Here's the catch. The central bank's previous sale, on 20 October at Tk123.65 per dollar, did not bring the rate down much, according to a senior bank official.

This time there was some effect. Remittance houses had offered dollars to banks at Tk124.30 to Tk124.50, and those offers came down after the central bank supplied dollars.

The central bank has sold $153.50 million from its reserves so far this fiscal year, the government's 12-month budget year. Today's $122 million is most of that.

What next? One senior banker said more payments are due this month, and that "the market will remain stable if the central bank sells dollars from its reserves a few more times." Watch whether more sales come, and whether the rate settles when they do.

Key facts

  • Bangladesh Bank sold $122 million at Tk124 to Tk124.30 per dollar on 28 October against $800 million of demand.
  • More than $300 million in payments were due that day, and remittances tend to be low at month-end.
  • The previous sale, on 20 October at Tk123.65, did not bring the rate down much.
  • The central bank has sold $153.50 million this fiscal year.

India invites a Pakistani minister to Delhi, a first since Operation Sindoor

India has invited Pakistan's culture minister to a regional cultural forum in Delhi, because hosting it means inviting every member, even a neighbour it barely talks to.

Can two countries with frozen relations still sit in the same room? India is about to find out.

Sources told The Indian Express that India has invited Pakistan's National Heritage and Culture Minister, Aurangzeb Khan Khichi, to New Delhi on November 20-21. It is the first such invitation to a senior Pakistani minister since the military hostilities of May 2025, which followed the Pahalgam terror attack and Operation Sindoor.

So why would Delhi do it? Because of a rule that comes with hosting.

The event is a Shanghai Cooperation Organisation forum, a grouping that includes Russia, India, China, Pakistan, Iran and several Central Asian states. India proposed the Civilisational Dialogue Forum itself, so it has to invite representatives from every member country.

Think of it like hosting a club event. Once you volunteer to run it, you can't leave one member off the list, however awkward things are with them.

Here's the catch. The report says India has kept bilateral ties with Pakistan frozen for a decade, since the Pathankot and Uri attacks in 2016. But it still has to deal with Pakistan inside multilateral groups like this one.

The forum was first proposed by Prime Minister Narendra Modi at the SCO summit in Tianjin in September 2025. Defence Minister Rajnath Singh then announced that India would host it, at a meeting in Dushanbe, Tajikistan, on October 20, 2026. Its stated aim is to build people-to-people ties and show off the shared heritage, art and literature of ancient civilisations.

The engagement, such as it is, has been building through SCO meetings. Last month, Indian official Alok Dimri led a delegation to an SCO coordinators' meeting in Islamabad, the first Indian presence at an SCO meeting in Pakistan in almost two years. Pakistan is due to host the SCO leaders' summit next year.

The last senior Pakistani minister India hosted was Bilawal Bhutto, then foreign minister, who went to Goa in May 2023. The reporting does not say whether Khichi has accepted.

Key facts

  • India invited Pakistan's culture minister Aurangzeb Khan Khichi to an SCO forum in Delhi on November 20-21.
  • It is the first such invitation to a senior Pakistani minister since the May 2025 hostilities.

Saudi Arabia says it stopped Houthi missiles, but debris still hit Riyadh

Saudi Arabia says it shot down five Houthi ballistic missiles on Tuesday, falling fragments damaged property in Riyadh, and the rebels claimed hits no one has confirmed.

Saudi Arabia says its air defenses destroyed both ballistic missiles fired at Riyadh on Tuesday, October 27. Even so, a building, a construction site and a vehicle in the capital were damaged.

How can both be true? Start with who fired them.

The missiles came from the Houthis, an Iran-backed rebel group in Yemen, Saudi Arabia's neighbour to the south. Saudi authorities said they also destroyed three more Houthi ballistic missiles headed for the country's eastern region. That makes five in one day.

Saudi Arabia issued attack warnings for the capital and other areas. Witnesses heard at least four explosions over Riyadh. Later the warnings were lifted.

Here's the answer to the puzzle. Shooting down a missile doesn't make it disappear. Think of a plate smashed in midair: it never reaches the floor in one piece, but the pieces still land. Saudi Civil Defense said fragments from the two intercepted missiles caused the damage in Riyadh.

Then the other side gave its version. Houthi military spokesperson Brig. Gen. Yahya Saree claimed on X that a rebel missile directly hit the Saudi defense ministry headquarters in Riyadh. The Houthis also claimed a hit on the Ras Tanoura refinery in eastern Saudi Arabia.

AP could not verify either claim, and Saudi Arabia did not comment on the refinery. The AP report also gave no casualty figure, so we don't know yet whether anyone was hurt.

So two stories are running side by side. In one, every missile was stopped. In the other, they hit their targets. The fragments land somewhere in between.

The fighting runs both ways. The Houthi-run Health Ministry said a strike on a gas station near Taiz late Monday killed two people and wounded several, and blamed Saudi-backed Yemeni government forces. Taiz is Yemen's third-largest city, and the Houthis have largely encircled it.

Who carried out that strike has not been independently established. The Yemeni military said separately that its forces had launched airstrikes against Houthi positions.

And Tuesday wasn't over. Late that day, Saudi Civil Defense announced new alerts in the Jeddah and Yanbu areas, on the other side of the country from the eastern region it had already defended that day.

Key facts

  • Saudi Arabia said it destroyed two Houthi ballistic missiles aimed at Riyadh and three aimed at its eastern region on October 27, 2026.
  • Saudi Civil Defense said fragments from the intercepted missiles damaged a building, a construction site and a vehicle in Riyadh.
  • The Houthis claimed hits on the Saudi defense ministry and the Ras Tanoura refinery; AP could not verify either.
  • Late Tuesday, Saudi Civil Defense announced alerts in the Jeddah and Yanbu areas.

Google built an AI model and is handing it only to cyber defenders first

Google announced Gemini 4 Carbon, a model strong at finding and fixing security holes, and is releasing it first to a small group of defenders.

Google just announced an AI model, and you can't use it yet. Gemini 4 Carbon starts this week with only a subset of cyber defenders, the people whose job is protecting systems from attack.

Why the slow start? Google calls its approach deliberate, and security is what Carbon is built to be good at. Google says it has state-of-the-art performance across the cybersecurity defense lifecycle: finding vulnerabilities, validating them, and fixing them.

Here are the scores Google reports. On SEC-bench Pro, which tests finding vulnerabilities, it gets 88.2%. On ExploitGym, which tests exploitation, 54.8%. On CWE-bench, which tests patching, 71.0%.

Notice the middle one. A model that can exploit a flaw can, in principle, be used by the wrong people too.

So Google is going gradually. It says it is in the U.S. government's voluntary process for testing models before release. Access widens to more trusted partners through its Fairwind Program, while Google keeps gathering feedback and adjusting guardrails.

Here's the catch. Google does not give a date for everyone else. It says it wants to reach developers, businesses and consumers "as soon as possible."

What does it do well beyond security? Google says it is built for long jobs. Its own teams saw Carbon run for days, pushing through dead ends to track down and patch subtle bugs in large codebases.

Google also shares a few comparisons. Carbon more than doubled Argon's success rate at writing secure code on internal tests. Argon is the model Google launched two weeks earlier. The Chrome team reported Carbon did more than 10X better than another lab's model at producing high quality patches.

On price, it starts at $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens. Tokens are the small chunks of text a model reads and writes. After the introductory period, that rises to $4 and $20, double.

Key facts

  • Gemini 4 Carbon is available this week only to a subset of cyber defenders, widening through the Fairwind Program.
  • Google reports 88.2% on SEC-bench Pro, 54.8% on ExploitGym and 71.0% on CWE-bench.
  • Introductory price is $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens, rising to $4 and $20.
  • Argon launched two weeks before Carbon.

Why so many cells? Here is the puzzle. Every cell in your body carries the same DNA, yet a muscle cell and an immune cell behave very differently.

TenK10K combines single-cell RNA sequencing with other kinds of molecular profiling. Three research teams have now published results from its first phase in Nature. Their papers cover rare and common variants, repeated stretches of DNA called tandem repeats, and how open the genome is for reading.

Here is the catch. Finding a link between a variant and a gene's activity is only a step. The commentary's authors say this work brings scientists closer to understanding the chain from genetic variation to gene expression to health, not that the chain is solved.

So the big idea is simple. Where a DNA difference acts, meaning which cell type it works in, is closely tied to disease. Those first results come from nearly 2,000 people, out of the 10,000 the project is aiming for.

The Suez Crisis ended with Britain and France needing America's permission

Israel invaded Egypt on 29 October 1956, and the war that followed showed Britain and France could no longer act alone.

Here is a belief worth breaking: that a war is judged by who wins the ground. In 1956, Israel took the Sinai and Gaza, and the winners on paper still came out of it looking smaller.

The war started on 29 October 1956, when Israel invaded Egypt. Britain and France then joined in.

So why did they care? Because of a ditch. The Suez Canal opened in 1869 and gave ships the shortest ocean route between the Mediterranean and the Indian Ocean.

Think of it as a toll road for the world's oil. Western Europe imported two million barrels a day from the Middle East, and 1,200,000 of them came by tanker through the canal. The rest came by pipeline, which British leaders saw as less stable.

Britain had held the canal's strings for decades. It bought a 44 percent share in the Suez Company in 1875 and took de facto control of Egypt after invading in 1882.

Here's the catch. In 1952, Egyptian officers overthrew King Farouk, and Gamal Abdul Nasser rose among them. In 1954, Britain agreed to pull its troops out of the Suez base within 20 months.

Then came the war, and the result was odd. Israel occupied Gaza and the Sinai for four months and could sail through the Straits of Tiran. But the canal itself was closed from October 1956 to March 1957.

The bigger loss was political. The crisis strengthened Nasser's standing and humiliated Britain and France during the Cold War. It showed they could no longer run independent foreign policies without U.S. consent.

The UN set up an emergency force to police the Egypt-Israel border. Canada's external-affairs minister, Lester B. Pearson, won the Nobel Peace Prize for his diplomatic efforts at the UN.

The crisis may also have emboldened the USSR, helping make its invasion of Hungary easier. Nobody can prove that chain, but it shows how one canal fight can ripple a long way.

Gravity, magnets and the atom: are nature's forces secretly one?

Nature's forces look like separate things, and physicists keep asking whether the quantum ones are really one thing in disguise.

Here's a belief that feels obvious: the forces of nature are separate machines, each doing its own job.

Start with the one you know best. Gravity pulls together anything with mass or energy. Einstein's general relativity explains it as the curvature of spacetime, so a heavy object bends the stage that everything else moves across.

Then there's electromagnetism. It acts on any particle with an electric charge, and on electric and magnetic fields. That covers lightning, fridge magnets and the phone in your hand.

Here's the catch. Those two are only part of the set. Physicists count four fundamental forces in total. The other two work inside atoms: the strong force, which holds the core of an atom together, and the weak force, which is behind some kinds of radioactive decay.

So which forces are the quantum ones? Electromagnetism, the strong force and the weak force are all described by quantum theory. Gravity is the odd one out, because Einstein's picture of it is not a quantum one.

That sets up the real question: could those three quantum forces be one force wearing three costumes? The idea of unifying them is an old ambition in physics.

Learn · Physics from first principles

Open problems: how physics keeps a list of what it can't explain

The last lesson in the course is about the questions that are still unanswered, and how physicists keep track of them.

Thirty-one questions. That is how many Isaac Newton listed about light and matter at the back of his book Opticks, first published in 1704. He did not claim to have the answers. He wrote the questions down.

That habit is the idea for today. Think of any messy kitchen drawer or a to-do list that never empties. Some items get crossed off, new ones appear, and the list itself tells you what you still have to do. Physics keeps lists like that, and it has done so for centuries.

So what is on the list now? Some problems are theoretical: our best theories cannot explain something we observe. Others are experimental: we may have an idea, but nobody has worked out how to build a test for it. Examples include the absolute mass of neutrinos, why there is more matter than antimatter, and what dark matter and dark energy are.

Here is the biggest item, and the neatest to picture. The Standard Model, our main theory of particles, does not fit with general relativity, our theory of gravity. Each one breaks down in extreme places, like the Big Bang or the centers of black holes, beyond their event horizons.

Now for a worked example with real numbers. In 1900, Lord Kelvin said physics had "two clouds": no evidence for the luminiferous aether, and a failure to predict the heat capacity of solids. Both were solved within a decade, by special relativity and quantum mechanics. Two problems, two revolutions, ten years at most.

Do lists always shrink that fast? No. Barry Simon posted 15 problems in 1984, and by 2000 about 5 were solved, so roughly 10 were still open. His second list of 15 had at least 3 solved. Roger Penrose's 1982 list still had about 14 unsolved in 2017. And the 10 problems proposed at the Strings 2000 conference? None had been conclusively solved as of 2025.

That is the takeaway. Physics is not a finished book. It is a list that gets crossed off at uneven speeds, and the clouds that look small can turn out to be whole new theories. Vitaly Ginzburg even named three great problems: the arrow of time, how to interpret quantum mechanics, and whether life can arise from physics alone. Which cloud on today's list will turn out to be the next revolution?

Key facts

  • Newton's Opticks (first edition 1704) included a list of 31 queries about light and matter.
  • In 1900 Lord Kelvin named "two clouds" in classical physics; both were solved within a decade by special relativity and quantum mechanics.
  • The Standard Model is inconsistent with general relativity, and both break down at singularities such as the Big Bang and inside black holes.
  • None of the 10 millennium problems proposed at Strings 2000 had been conclusively solved as of 2025.

The news shows you the crack, not the years it took to form

A closing page on what a day of headlines says about how the world runs.

Read a day of headlines and you see events: a crash, a storm, a verdict. Underneath, slower things are doing the real work, and the news mostly catches them at the moment they snap.

That leads to the quietest pattern: someone chose the order, the limit, the default. Those choices are rarely written about when they are made. They become visible only when they fail, which is why failure fills the front page and the years of drift do not.

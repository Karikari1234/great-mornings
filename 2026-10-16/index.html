One classroom roof fell in Peshawar and took two floors with it

At least 33 pupils and a teacher died when a school roof in Peshawar collapsed through the classrooms below it, and police say the building was overloaded.

At least 33 pupils and a teacher were killed on Thursday when a school roof collapsed in Peshawar, a city in north-western Pakistan. More than a dozen others were injured, some critically, according to hospital officials.

The numbers make sense once you see how the building failed. Rescuers were called to the private Al-Khair school at 08:48, after lessons had begun. The roof of an upper-floor classroom had come down, rescue official Bilal Faizi said, and the ceilings below caved in too.

Here's the catch. Early reports spoke of a two-storey part of the building, but police chief Mian Saeed later confirmed that classrooms on three floors were brought down.

He said about 30 pupils were in the top class, 40 in the middle and 45 on the ground floor. That adds up to about 115 children in the rooms the falling roof passed through.

So why did it fall? Saeed said the structure had "substandard" roof slabs and was "overloaded", beyond the weight it could bear. Think of a stack of trays: once the top one drops, it lands on the next, and that one has nowhere to go.

Rescue was hard for a plain reason. The school sits on a very narrow street, which made it hard for crews to reach the site and get the injured to hospital.

Neighbours did what they could. An English teacher from a nearby school said he and others pulled more than 20 children from the rubble, six of them with his own hands. "Most of them were alive and talking," he said. "But later it was found that they had passed away."

Now come the questions. The school's owner has been arrested, and Saeed promised an inquiry into "whether there were any violations of building codes". Emergency official Arif Khattak said the building had been constructed on a temporary basis.

Family members accused the school of ignoring safety concerns. The governor of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Faisal Karim Kundi, has ordered a safety audit of all schools in the province.

This is not the only roof to fail this year. In June, fourteen children died when the roof of a private tuition centre near Lahore collapsed. Whether the Peshawar inquiry finds the rules were broken, or finds the rules were not enough, is the answer families are waiting for.

  • Police chief Mian Saeed said the structure had substandard roof slabs and was overloaded.
  • The school's owner has been arrested and the governor ordered a safety audit of all schools in the province.

Garment workers shut the Dhaka-Mymensingh highway, saying their 2023 raise no longer covers prices

Thousands of Uttara garment workers blocked the highway on Thursday to demand higher pay, and police cleared them with batons and sound grenades.

A pay rise is supposed to settle a wage fight. Garment workers got one in 2023. On Thursday, thousands of them blocked a highway to ask for another.

Workers from several factories gathered around Azampur and Rajlokkhi in Uttara, then took over the Dhaka-Mymensingh highway. Khalid Mansur, who heads Uttara West police station, put the crowd at 5,000 to 7,000. Traffic stopped in both directions. Hundreds of long-distance bus passengers were stuck, and some got off and walked.

So what do they want? Higher wages, pay on time, and an end to arbitrary layoffs. Some protesters are asking for a minimum basic salary of Tk 30,000 a month.

Here's the puzzle. The minimum wage went from Tk 8,000 to Tk 12,500 in December 2023. That's Tk 4,500 more every month. Why isn't it enough?

The workers' answer is groceries. Akhtar Hossain said rising prices of essentials make it impossible to get by on current pay, and that repeated appeals to the authorities have gone nowhere. On Wednesday, Sharifa Begum put it as simply as anyone could: "Our only demand is either to lower the prices of essential commodities or raise our wages so that we can afford to make ends meet."

This wasn't a one-off. Workers in Uttara also protested on Tuesday and Wednesday. On Wednesday, police talked them off the road. Thursday went differently.

Mansur said officers fired sound grenades after repeated requests to leave failed, and police also used batons. Another police official, Rezaul Karim, said one group agreed to withdraw, while another tried to throw bricks and stones at officers and was chased away. Traffic moved again by late morning or just after noon, depending on which report you read.

The same morning in Sreepur, Gazipur, more than 1,000 workers from C-Stage (BD) Ltd blocked another highway over unpaid September wages. They had arrived to find their factory closed for a general holiday. Industrial police dispersed them with sound grenades.

Scale matters here. The industry has about 4,000 registered factories and more than four million workers, according to the manufacturers' association.

Watch for the wage board. At a seminar on Wednesday, factory owners, union leaders and economists all called for a review of garment wages. The association's president said employers support forming a board, but the government decides when. No date has been reported. Until there is one, the highway is where this argument keeps happening.

Gaza's shops have gone without new goods for two days

Israel says commercial goods have stopped entering Gaza over a fee dispute; Hamas and Gaza's truckers say Israel's account is wrong.

Two days. That is how long Israeli authorities say no commercial goods have entered Gaza.

The body that announced it is COGAT, the Israeli military unit that oversees civilian affairs in Gaza. It said local merchants made the decision themselves, in response to a 10,000 shekel fee (about $3,220) that Hamas put on each truck, plus a demand that 30 percent of sales be in cash.

Here's the catch. Nobody on the Gaza side agrees with that story.

Gaza's government media office called it a "blatant disinformation campaign." It said the fee was a voluntary payment to a fund for people in need, such as widows and orphans. Cash, it said, was encouraged because of a cash shortage, but never required.

The head of Gaza's private truckers union, Nahed Sheheiber, went further. He said it is the Israelis who imposed the restrictions, and that the fees are being used as a pretext.

So why does a pause in shop goods matter, if aid is still arriving?

Because the two flows carry different things. Aid groups generally bring staples such as flour, while merchants import perishables like fruit and meat. Winter clothing is also among the halted goods.

Think of it as two supply lines feeding one market. Humanitarian workers say aid plus a working commercial market is what keeps prices under control. Shaina Low of the Norwegian Refugee Council put it plainly: "The markets need to be functioning."

The numbers show how much rides on commercial trucks. Israel is obliged under a US-mediated agreement to let in at least 600 aid trucks a day. Diplomats say far fewer normally arrive. Sheheiber puts it near 300, with two-thirds carrying commercial goods, which is roughly 200 trucks a day now stopped.

That lands on 2.1 million people who depend on Israel's border crossings, in a territory still suffering famine. A global hunger monitor warned in July that more than two-thirds of Gazans could face acute hunger by the end of the year.

Meta released Muse Spark Open, an 816-billion-parameter model, and says two Apple Mac Studios can run it on a desk.

Meta and Apple are usually rivals. Today they spent a launch cheering each other on.

The occasion is Muse Spark Open, a new AI model from Meta. Meta says it is the "most capable US open-weight model to date."

"Open-weight" is the bit worth a sentence. A model's weights are the numbers it learned during training. Release them, and anyone can download the model and run it themselves.

Here's where Apple comes in. Meta says this is the "first model in its class" that can "run on your desktop using two Apple M5 Ultra Mac Studios with the latest MLX framework." MLX is Apple's framework for machine learning on its own chips.

Why does that matter? The model has 816 billion parameters, the adjustable numbers inside it. That is a lot of numbers, and the claim is that two desktop computers can hold it.

Meta describes it as natively multimodal, meaning it handles more than just text. It says the model "excels at long-horizon tasks, complex software engineering, and multi-step reasoning."

MSCI proposes pushing Indonesia out of its emerging-market indexes

The index giant says Indonesia's stock market no longer belongs among emerging markets, and Indonesia has until mid-2027 to change its mind.

The usual punishment for a troubled stock market is a demotion, one step down the ladder. MSCI says the investors it heard from think that would be too mild for Indonesia.

First, who is MSCI? It's a company that builds stock indexes, which are lists of shares that huge investment funds copy. It sorts the world's markets into four groups: developed, emerging, frontier and standalone. Those labels help decide which countries get into its bigger indexes.

Think of it as a guest list. Funds that track an index buy what's on the list. Standalone markets generally sit outside both the emerging and frontier indexes, so if a country gets moved there, funds following those indexes may need to change what they hold.

That's what MSCI put on the table on October 15. It opened a consultation on moving Indonesia from emerging to standalone, saying its emerging-market status was "no longer appropriate." Feedback suggested that dropping it to frontier wouldn't reflect how deep the problems go.

So what's wrong? MSCI said investors remain worried about opaque shareholding structures and suspected coordinated trading. In plain terms: it's hard to tell who really owns some companies, and whether prices move for honest reasons. MSCI also cited market dysfunction and little evidence of meaningful enforcement. China's first atomic bomb was named after a broken promise

China's first nuclear test, on 16 October 1964, carried a name that marks the month the Soviet Union backed out of helping.

Project 596 sounds like a serial number. It is a date. The name points to June 1959, the month the Soviet Union said it would not provide the technical help it had promised China.

Here's the backstory. China's nuclear weapons program began on 15 January 1955, prompted by confrontations with the United States, including the Korean War and the 1955 Taiwan Straits Crisis. In 1956, Mao Zedong told the Politburo: "If we don't want to be bullied, we have to have this thing."

At first, Moscow was a partner. In 1957 the two countries signed an agreement on sharing defense technology, which included a prototype weapon supplied by the Soviets and an exchange of hundreds of scientists.

Then relations cooled. The Soviets were negotiating a test ban with the United States, which held up the prototype, and ideological disagreements grew. In the end Moscow withdrew the prototype and over 1,400 advisers and technicians.

So China went alone. Mao shifted toward self-reliance, and the bomb project carried on as an independent program. By 14 January 1964, the Lanzhou plant had enriched enough U-235.

Meanwhile the test site was rising at Lop Nur in Xinjiang. Construction began on 1 April 1960, with tens of thousands of laborers and prisoners working under tough conditions, and it took four years.

Here's the catch. In 1964 Chinese leadership received intelligence that raised its fear of a surgical US strike on the program. It weighed delaying the test, because a test would alert the United States and the Soviet Union to China's progress.

It went ahead on 16 October 1964. The device held about 15 kg of weapons-grade uranium and yielded 22 kilotons, comparable to the Fat Man bomb dropped on Nagasaki. China became the world's fifth nuclear-armed state, and Premier Zhou Enlai announced it the same day.

It was only the first of 45 successful tests China conducted between 1964 and 1996, all at Lop Nur. The test also pushed Taiwan under Chiang Kai-shek to pursue its own clandestine nuclear weapons until 1988.

So the 596 stamped on that bomb is the date of a promise withdrawn. Five years later, 16 October 1964 was the answer.

Key facts

  • Project 596 was detonated on 16 October 1964 at Lop Nur in Xinjiang.
  • It was named after June 1959, when the Soviet Union said it would not provide promised technical help.
  • The device yielded 22 kilotons, comparable to the Nagasaki bomb.
  • China conducted 45 successful nuclear tests between 1964 and 1996, all at Lop Nur.

Learn · Physics from first principles

The expanding universe

How physics proved that the cosmos cannot sit still, from Einstein's equations to the Big Bang.

If gravity pulls masses toward each other, why has the entire universe not collapsed into one clump? Drop an object, and it falls toward Earth because mass attracts mass, while the Copernican principle establishes that celestial bodies obey those identical physical laws. When Albert Einstein applied his general theory of relativity to the cosmos in 1916, he found that a static universe was impossible because all that distributed matter must pull together over time.

In 1917, Einstein added a constant term called the cosmological constant to his equations to counteract gravity and force a static universe. Here is the catch: that static model was unstable to small perturbations. Any small nudge would cause it to start expanding or contracting, meaning space cannot simply stand still.

Alexander Friedmann showed in the early 1920s that general relativity naturally allows universes that expand or contract, with geometries that can be open, flat, or closed. Astronomers were already seeing hints of this motion: in the 1910s, Vesto Slipher measured the redshift of spiral nebulae. Slipher interpreted that shift as a Doppler effect showing that those distant nebulae were rushing away from Earth.

Measuring speed is straightforward, but calculating cosmic distance requires the inverse-square law. Think of a light in a kitchen: if it sits 2 times farther away, its brightness drops by 2 squared, appearing one-fourth as bright. By measuring the brightness of Cepheid variable stars and assuming their intrinsic luminosity, Edwin Hubble calculated real distances in 1929, proving these nebulae were external galaxies beyond the Milky Way.

Hubble discovered that the redshift of a galaxy is proportional to its distance, meaning galaxies recede faster the farther away they are. If galaxy B is 2 times farther away than galaxy A, it recedes 2 times as fast, though Hubble's original calculation was off by a factor of 10 because he did not know Cepheid variables come in different types. That relationship, known as Hubble's law, supported Georges Lemaitre's 1927 proposal that the expanding universe began with the explosion of a primeval atom, later called the Big Bang.

Today, the standard model of cosmology explains these observations well, even though it requires large amounts of dark matter and dark energy that are not yet understood. That brings us to the question for our final lesson in this course. Because the equations allow space to expand or contract, will gravity eventually pull everything back together, or is the ultimate fate of the universe to expand forever?

Key facts

  • Einstein's general relativity showed a static universe is unstable because masses attract each other over time.
  • Hubble discovered that distant galaxies are receding at speeds proportional to their distance from Earth.
  • Hubble's original calculation of the expansion rate was off by a factor of ten due to unknown types of Cepheid variables.

Why an overwhelmingly Muslim nation keeps headscarves out of the classroom

Decades of Soviet state atheism reshaped Azerbaijan into a fiercely secular state that still bans headscarves in classrooms.

You might expect a country with 1,300 years of Islamic history to welcome religious headscarves in its classrooms. Across Azerbaijan, Muslim students, parents, and religious activists are protesting the opposite: strict school uniform regulations that ban the hijab.

The controversy exposes an apparent contradiction. Azerbaijan is overwhelmingly Muslim and predominantly Shia, yet its authoritarian government maintains tight control over religious life and promotes secularism.

Wait, how did a nation with 1,300 years of Islamic history become one of the most secular societies in the Muslim world? Islam first reached modern Azerbaijan during the seventh century as Muslim armies expanded into the Caucasus, turning cities like Baku and Ganja into major centers of Islamic learning. Shah Ismail I established the Safavid state in 1501 and made Shia Islam the official religion, transforming the population from mostly Sunni to predominantly Shia.

The political shift toward secular rule began in the nineteenth century, after Russian treaties divided Azerbaijani territories and Baku's oil boom created a new generation of modern intellectuals. By 1918, Azerbaijan established a short-lived independent republic that adopted a secular system and granted women the right to vote, becoming one of the earliest Muslim-majority states to do so.

Then Soviet forces conquered Azerbaijan in April 1920. The new Communist authorities committed to state atheism, closing mosques, dismantling Islamic schools, and executing clerics during Stalin's purges.

That Soviet era lasted approximately seventy years, roughly an entire human lifetime. Think of a family recipe passed down in a kitchen for generations: if an authority locks the pantry and bans the meal for seventy years, grandchildren will know the name of the dish without knowing how to cook it. By the time the Soviet Union collapsed in 1991, the transmission of Islamic knowledge between generations was severely disrupted.

Independence revived personal faith, as mosques reopened and religious influences arrived from Turkey and Iran. Here is the catch. Post-Soviet rulers, former president Heydar Aliyev and his son Ilham Aliyev, chose to preserve the secular system and consolidated extensive government control over religious institutions.

On paper, Azerbaijan's constitution formally guarantees freedom of conscience and separates religion from the state. In practice, the authorities defend school uniform rules, keeping the classroom strictly secular while families demand their religious freedoms back.

Key facts

  • Azerbaijan is overwhelmingly Muslim and predominantly Shia, yet its authoritarian government strictly enforces secular rules.
  • In 1918, Azerbaijan founded an independent republic that adopted a secular political system and granted women the right to vote.
  • Soviet authorities enforced state atheism for approximately seventy years, closing mosques and executing clerics during Stalin's purges.

What built up before the headlines

A closing thought on trust, pressure and the rules people live by.

A false picture can travel on the back of a real assault. The fake AI recreations of a protester being punched at a Trump rally offer a clue to this moment: an event and the apparent evidence of it can split apart. The news arrives all at once. The conditions that shape what we believe have been changing for much longer.

One pattern is that trust depends on records people can question. The disputed California voter list sits in that uncomfortable space. A list can look settled until someone asks who belongs on it, who checked it and who has the power to make the final call. Those questions are there before any lawsuit gives them a headline.

Another pattern is pressure building inside things people count on. Police say the Peshawar school building was overloaded before its roof fell. The Uttara garment workers' 2023 raise no longer covers prices. These are very different harms, unfolding at very different speeds. Each became news when a strain that had been easier to overlook could no longer be overlooked.

That may be why the world can feel as if it keeps changing in a day. Headlines capture the moment a roof falls, a claim is challenged or people take to a road. Beneath those moments are decisions about safety, pay, membership and proof. They shape daily life even when nothing dramatic happens. When the next headline arrives, what had to happen quietly for that moment to become possible?

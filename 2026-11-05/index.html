Vance says the war lost the midterms. Trump would do it again

Democrats took Congress, Trump's own vice president blames the Iran war, and the voter data shows why that argument is hard to settle while the war goes on.

"The reason we lost is the war." The vice president, JD Vance, wrote that about his own party. His boss, Donald Trump, called the post "fine" and said of the war, "I would have done it again."

So what happened? Midterms are the elections halfway through a president's four-year term, when all 435 House seats and about a third of the Senate are on the ballot. On November 3, Democrats took both the House and the Senate from Republicans, and by November 4 they held at least 54 of 100 Senate seats, with some races still uncalled.

The war is the one the US and Israel launched against Iran in February, in its ninth month by election day. Trump's reading is milder than Vance's: "I think a part of it was Iran."

So who's right? A survey of more than 100,000 voters found about six in 10 called military action against Iran the wrong decision. Sounds like a win for Vance.

Here's the catch. Only about a third said the war was key to their congressional vote, while roughly half said that about the cost of living or government corruption. So plenty of voters thought the war was a mistake and still didn't call it key to their vote.

Point to Trump, then? Wait. The war has driven up US gasoline prices and the cost of other goods, so someone naming the cost of living might be complaining about the war without saying so. The results alone can't tell you how much of this defeat was Iran and how much was everything else.

Vance threw down a challenge: anyone who thinks the war was worth losing the Senate for the next six years should make that argument. Trump says it was worth it to stop Tehran from "having a nuclear weapon."

And the winners? When the new Congress begins on January 3, 2027, a Democratic House can investigate and block legislation, and a Democratic Senate can reject nominees. Trump stays president, with a veto.

Iran's parliament speaker and chief negotiator, Mohammad Bagher Qalibaf, wrote that Americans had said "NO TO WAR."

Iran's supreme leader, Ayatollah Mojtaba Khamenei, dismissed the idea that the election would bring a major change in US policy toward Iran. On that, he sounds oddly like Trump, who told reporters: "The Iran thing we had to do, and we continue to do it."

Key facts

  • Democrats won control of both the House and the Senate in the November 3, 2026 midterms.
  • About six in 10 voters called US military action against Iran the wrong decision, but only about one-third said the war was key to their vote.
  • Vance wrote, "The reason we lost is the war"; Trump said only part of it was Iran and that he would have done it again.
  • Iran's supreme leader dismissed the prospect that the election would bring a major change in US policy toward Iran.

Garment workers get a date for new pay, but no amount

The new wage board says a revised garment pay scale starts in December, but it has not decided the raise, and it is unclear whether protesting workers will go back.

Bangladesh's garment workers now have a start date for new pay. What they don't have is the amount.

The new wage board agreed on November 4, at its first meeting, that a revised pay scale will begin in December, with the first salaries under it arriving in January. The size of the raise was never discussed, said workers' representative Babul Akter. More meetings will settle that.

Think of a job offer with a start date and a blank space where the salary goes. Would that be enough to clear the streets on day five of the protests? Hold that question.

Start with the size of the blank. Workers want a minimum of 30,000 taka a month. The current minimum is 12,500, set in 2023 when it rose from 8,000. So the last raise added 4,500 taka, and this demand would add 17,500.

Their case is about what money buys. Workers say food, housing and transport costs have eaten away at their pay.

The day of the meeting was also a day of violence. Workers and police clashed in Savar and Gazipur, where protesters blocked roads and highways and police used tear gas and sound grenades. In Fatullah, dozens were injured, according to media reports cited by AP.

Why does the whole economy feel this? Garments employ about four million workers and earn more than 80% of Bangladesh's export money. Hundreds of factories shut during the week, raising concern about delays to spring and summer export orders.

Here's the odd part. H&M, one of the buyers, said some factories had temporarily closed but that it had seen no major supply-chain disruptions.

So did the blank space clear the streets? Partly. Protests eased that afternoon after the announcement, but there was no immediate sign that workers would return to factories.

Many were unsure what the announcement meant, said Kamrul Hasan of the Ekota Garments Sramik Federation. Workers were asking on social media whether their pay had gone up. It hasn't yet.

The owners' side sees it differently. "The unrest should subside, as progress is evident now," said Mahmudul Hasan, president of the BGMEA. And the date does do something: the six-member board, which includes workers' and owners' representatives, must now settle the increase before January's pay.

Watch for its next meetings, which have no announced dates. That is where the blank gets filled in, and where the demand for 30,000 taka gets its answer.

Key facts

  • The wage board agreed on November 4 to apply a revised pay scale from December, with first salaries under it in January.
  • The board has not decided the size of the increase and will hold further meetings.
  • Workers are demanding a minimum of 30,000 taka a month, up from the current 12,500 taka.
  • Protests eased after the announcement, but there was no immediate indication that workers would return to factories.

Cyclone Murasu's Wednesday peak: 278.5 mm in Bhadradri, 13.5 mm in Khammam

Two Telangana districts shared a red alert for Cyclone Murasu, but by Wednesday evening one was still being drenched and the other barely wet.

Two Telangana districts were hit by the same cyclone. On Wednesday, the wettest spot in one had 278.5 mm of rain. The wettest spot in the other had 13.5 mm.

That is a gap of more than 20 times. To feel 278.5 mm, think of a standard school ruler standing in an open field: the water that fell would have come close to its top, if it had nowhere to drain.

Let's rewind to Tuesday, November 3. Khammam and Bhadradri Kothagudem were both under red alert for Cyclone Murasu. Overnight, one mandal in each got extremely heavy rain, between 204 mm and 235 mm. (A mandal is a small local administrative unit inside a district.)

The top readings were 235 mm at Mulakapalle in Bhadradri Kothagudem and 204.7 mm at Vemsoor in Khammam. Some more mandals in the two districts got very heavy rain, between 150 mm and 204 mm. So far, the districts look like twins.

Here's the catch. On Wednesday, Bhadradri Kothagudem got no break. By 8 p.m., Aswaraopet had 278.5 mm, with 188.3 mm at Malkaram, 175 mm at Ankampalem and 149.8 mm at Mulakalapally.

Khammam's story was different. Its highest Wednesday reading was 13.5 mm at JVR OCP I&II, followed by 6.3 mm at Pedapalli.

The 11 a.m. bulletin from forecasters put Bhadradri Kothagudem under orange alert, meaning heavy to very heavy rain. For Khammam and Mulugu it predicted moderate to heavy rain.

That is the clever bit about alerts: they get redrawn district by district, day by day. A place that was drenched overnight can be quiet by the next evening while its neighbour is still taking the hit.

Same cyclone, same alert on Tuesday. By Wednesday evening, 278.5 mm and 13.5 mm.

Key facts

  • Bhadradri Kothagudem's Aswaraopet got 278.5 mm of rain on Wednesday, per rainfall data till 8 p.m.
  • Overnight Tuesday into Wednesday, Mulakapalle (Bhadradri Kothagudem) got 235 mm and Vemsoor (Khammam) got 204.7 mm.
  • Khammam's highest Wednesday reading was 13.5 mm at JVR OCP I&II.
  • IMD put Bhadradri Kothagudem under orange alert on Wednesday.

Iran's top negotiator says Americans voted "no to war"

After Republicans lost badly in the midterms, Iran's chief negotiator said it backs the message, and Trump admitted the war and petrol prices played a part.

Here is a strange twist: after US midterm voters dealt President Trump's party a heavy defeat, Iran's chief negotiator had something to say about it.

Mohammed Bagher Ghalibaf, Iran's parliament speaker and chief negotiator in the talks with the US, wrote on X that Americans had voted "NO TO WAR". "We endorse this message," he said.

He added a warning. If the US government betrays the public will, "it will learn our NO comes with a Nightmare."

Did Trump disagree? Only partly. In a Fox News interview he said "I think a part of it was Iran."

His reasoning was about the pump. People agree with what he is doing on Iran, he said, but "probably weren't willing" to pay more for gasoline in the short term. He also insisted he does not regret the joint attack with Israel on Iran in February.

Then the part that matters for what comes next. Trump told CBS News the conflict would end "when I say it ends." In recent weeks he has warned the US and Israel could return to all-out war after the midterms if no deal is reached.

Iran's Supreme Leader, Mojtaba Khamenei, said anyone expecting a change in American "hostility towards the Iranian nation" was "gravely mistaken". He said a resumed war would have "much wider dimensions".

So where do the talks stand? Indirect negotiations, mediated by Qatar, have been stalled for months. A preliminary deal in June to end the war and reopen the Strait of Hormuz collapsed within weeks.

That strait is why petrol prices matter here. A fifth of global oil and gas shipments usually pass through it, and it was effectively closed after the war began.

The fighting there has not stopped. Iran's Revolutionary Guard claimed four tankers caught fire after hitting mines, but BBC Verify has found no evidence that four were destroyed.

A voting booth can send a message. Whether it changes a war that Trump says ends only when he says so is the open question.

Key facts

  • Mohammed Bagher Ghalibaf wrote on X that Americans voted "NO TO WAR" and Iran endorses the message.
  • Trump told Fox News that "a part of it was Iran" and said he does not regret the February attack.

In the Crimean War, battle killed fewer soldiers than everything else

Of 165,363 deaths in the Crimean War, only 45,770 came in combat, and the failures the war came to symbolise pushed reforms in Britain and Russia.

Which killed more soldiers in the Crimean War: the fighting, or everything else? Everything else, and it was not close.

The war's death toll was 165,363. Of those, 45,770 were combat deaths and 119,593 were non-combat deaths. That is more than two and a half non-combat deaths for every one in battle.

Here's the quick setup. Russia fought an alliance of the Ottoman Empire, France, the United Kingdom and Sardinia from October 1853 to February 1856. The spark was Tsar Nicholas I demanding that the Ottoman Empire's Orthodox subjects be placed under his protection. The Ottomans refused.

Britain and France feared Russian influence was growing, and joined the Ottoman side in March 1854. That September, their forces landed in Crimea to capture Sevastopol, Russia's main naval base on the Black Sea.

Which brings us to Inkerman in 1854. The Russians counterattacked, and the allies still beat them.

Here's the catch. Winning did not end anything. The front settled into the 11-month Siege of Sevastopol, with brutal conditions for troops on both sides.

That is the backdrop to the big number. The war quickly became a symbol of logistical, medical and tactical failures and mismanagement. In Britain, the reaction led to a demand for professional medicine, most famously through Florence Nightingale, who gained worldwide attention for pioneering modern nursing.

Russia took its own lesson. The war weakened its army and drained its treasury. The defeat forced its educated elites to name the country's fundamental problems, and reforms followed, including the 1861 emancipation reform that abolished serfdom.

One last twist is why it started at all. The historian A. J. P. Taylor argued that the war came from the interacting fears of the major players, with mutual fear rather than mutual aggression at its root.

  • The Crimean War killed 165,363 people: 45,770 in combat and 119,593 from non-combat causes.
  • At Inkerman in 1854, a Russian counterattack ended in defeat by the allies.
  • The war exposed mismanagement and drove demands for professional medicine in Britain.
  • Russia's defeat helped lead to the 1861 emancipation reform that abolished serfdom.

Learn · Physics from first principles

Open problems: how physics keeps a list of what it cannot explain

The last lesson is about the gaps. Physics has a habit of writing them down, and sometimes the gaps close fast.

What happens when you keep asking why? Ask it about anything, again and again, and sooner or later you hit a question where the honest answer is "we don't know yet." Physics has fourteen lessons of answers behind it. This last lesson is about where the answers stop.

Those stopping points come in two kinds. Some are theoretical: our best theories cannot explain something we have observed. Others are experimental: we have an idea, but nobody has worked out how to build an experiment that tests it.

Here's the biggest clash. The Standard Model and general relativity, two of our strongest theories, are mathematically inconsistent with each other. Push them to extreme conditions and both break down. Those conditions exist in the real world, at the Big Bang and at the centers of black holes, beyond their event horizons.

Beyond that sit more puzzles: the absolute mass of neutrinos, why there is more matter than antimatter, and what dark matter and dark energy actually are. The list is long. So how do you even keep track of it?

Physicists write the problems down. Isaac Newton did it in 1704, with 31 queries about light and matter at the end of his book Opticks. In 1900, Lord Kelvin gave a speech naming just two "clouds" over physics: no evidence for the luminiferous aether, and an equation called the equipartition theorem that failed to predict how much heat solids hold.

Now the worked example, with numbers. Two problems, and Kelvin's clouds were both solved within a decade, by special relativity and quantum mechanics. That's the catch: the small, annoying gaps turned out to be doorways to whole new physics. Other lists move slower. In 1984 Barry Simon listed 15 open problems on quantum operators, and by 2000 about 5 were solved, a third of them in 16 years. The 10 problems proposed at the Strings 2000 conference? None had been conclusively solved as of 2025.

Nobel laureate Vitaly Ginzburg kept such a list from 1971 to 2005, ending with 30 problems. He added three "great problems": the arrow of time, how to interpret quantum mechanics, and whether life can arise from physics alone. There is no lesson 16 to answer them. Which of these gaps will turn out to be a small cloud, and which a whole new door?

Key facts

  • The Standard Model is mathematically inconsistent with general relativity, and both break down at the Big Bang and inside black holes.
  • In 1900 Lord Kelvin named two 'clouds' over physics; both were solved within a decade by special relativity and quantum mechanics.
  • Barry Simon's 1984 list had 15 problems, with about 5 solved by 2000.
  • Vitaly Ginzburg's final list had 30 problems plus three 'great problems'.

Trump, Putin and Xi plan a three-way meeting in China, with no deal expected

The leaders of the world's biggest nuclear powers plan to meet next week in Shenzhen, but a senior US official reportedly expects no agreement.

The leaders of the world's biggest nuclear powers are planning to sit down together in China next week, and a senior US official reportedly doesn't expect them to agree on anything.

The White House says Donald Trump, Vladimir Putin and Xi Jinping will meet on the sidelines of the Apec summit in Shenzhen. An unnamed senior US official told AP and Reuters that no agreement was expected.

So why meet? Spokeswoman Olivia Wales said Trump will discuss "the reduction of nuclear weapons in the world", whose spread he believes is very dangerous. Iran may come up too. Ukraine, it is understood, will not.

Here's the backstory. A nuclear weapons control treaty between the US and Russia expired in February. That left the two biggest nuclear arsenals without major guardrails, and the UN urged both sides to agree a new framework without delay.

Trump's answer was that he could replace it with a better deal that includes China, which has been building up its arsenal.

Here's the catch. Former senior US officials and analysts told the BBC at the time that winning over Beijing and Moscow could create major hurdles and take years. A two-player deal would become a three-player one.

And not everyone has said yes out loud. Russia has confirmed the meeting. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said earlier this month there were "signals confirming readiness". China has yet to react.

Chinese state media did report that Xi will hold bilateral talks on the sidelines of the summit. It has not reported the three-way meeting.

Trump arrives late on Wednesday for the two-day summit. His talks with Xi on Thursday will be their fourth meeting since he returned to office in 2025, and the three-way meeting is how Wales says his China visit will end.

The thing to watch is whether Beijing confirms the meeting.

Key facts

  • The White House says Trump, Putin and Xi plan to meet on the sidelines of the Apec summit in Shenzhen.
  • A US-Russia nuclear weapons control treaty expired in February.
  • An unnamed senior US official said no agreement was expected.
  • Russia has confirmed the meeting; China has yet to react.

Hasina's return may hinge on an appeal Dhaka says she can't have

India reportedly wants Dhaka to promise Sheikh Hasina an appeal and an army-led handover, but Bangladesh says her appeal window has already closed.

The stakes are simple. If Sheikh Hasina comes home, does she get a new trial, or does she face a death sentence that is already final?

Some quick background. Hasina fled to India on a Bangladeshi military aircraft on August 5, 2024, as the uprising ended her government, and she has stayed there since. In November 2025, the International Crimes Tribunal convicted her in absentia of crimes against humanity over the 2024 crackdown. It gave her death on one charge and life imprisonment on another. She rejects the charges.

So what's new? On November 12, BBC Bangla reported that unnamed Indian officials had set out two conditions for helping her return. India has not confirmed them.

The first is about logistics. The Bangladesh Army would take responsibility for bringing her back, either by sending a military plane to Delhi or by taking her at a set point on the land border.

The second is the hard one. Bangladesh would have to guarantee her a chance to appeal her death sentence and a fresh trial. One unnamed foreign ministry official in Delhi told BBC Bangla that India would want a fresh, fair trial rather than knowingly hand her over to be executed.

Here's the catch. On November 10, chief prosecutor Md Aminul Islam said the 30-day deadline to appeal had passed. On November 14, Law Minister Md Asaduzzaman said she would not be entitled to appeal under the relevant law. So the reported condition asks for the one thing Dhaka's officials say they can't give.

Officially, nobody is saying much. Asked about the conditions, Indian foreign ministry spokesperson Randhir Jaiswal said, "I have nothing to say on that," and called extradition a legal matter. A Bangladesh foreign ministry official said Dhaka had received nothing from India about any conditions.

Dhaka has asked for her twice under the extradition treaty, in December 2024 and again after the verdict. Asaduzzaman says the legal work is done and the decision now sits with India.

Meanwhile, Hasina is making her own plans. In July she told Reuters she and senior Awami League colleagues planned to return around December and surrender. Through a London-based legal chamber, she has asked the High Commission in New Delhi for a new passport and NID card, and Bangladesh says it is checking that request.

What to watch: December is weeks away and she has named no date. If those travel documents are issued, or India answers the extradition request, the picture changes fast.

Key facts

  • BBC Bangla reported on November 12 that India wants an army-led handover and a guaranteed appeal and fresh trial; India has not confirmed this.
  • Bangladesh's chief prosecutor says the 30-day appeal deadline has expired, and the law minister says Hasina is not entitled to appeal.
  • Hasina was sentenced to death in absentia in November 2025 and says she plans to return in December 2026.

Delhi's air was the world's dirtiest on Friday, ahead of Lahore

On Friday, Delhi's air quality reading was higher than Lahore's, making it the most polluted city in the world that day.

Being the most polluted city on Earth is not a permanent title. It is more like a scoreboard that gets updated, and on Friday, Delhi came out on top, ahead of Lahore.

The scoreboard runs on a number called the Air Quality Index, or AQI. Think of it as a single grade for the air: the higher the number, the dirtier the air you are breathing.

SwissIQ, which tracks these readings, put Delhi at 334 on Friday. It labelled that level "hazardous". Delhi's reading was higher than Lahore's in Pakistan, and that was enough to put it first among cities worldwide.

Here's the catch. A ranking like this is a snapshot, not a verdict. It tells you who was worst on one day, not who is worst all year.

So why Delhi, and why now? The source offers one clue: pollution levels in Delhi increase significantly during the winter months.

What we do not know from this report is how long Delhi will stay there, or how far apart the two cities really are. The source gives one reading and one day.

So the question for the next few days is simple. Does Delhi hold the title, or does Lahore take it back?

Key facts

  • SwissIQ reported Delhi's AQI at 334 on Friday, categorizing it as "hazardous".
  • Delhi's AQI was higher than Lahore's in Pakistan, making Delhi the most polluted city globally that day.

Ukraine's president says the US floated land for NATO membership

Zelenskyy says US negotiators asked if Ukraine would leave parts of Donbas to join NATO, and he said he would only put a full package to a national vote.

What would Ukraine give up to join NATO? According to its president, American negotiators have asked him exactly that.

Volodymyr Zelenskyy says the question came up after the latest rounds of peace talks in Abu Dhabi and Ireland. In his words, the Americans asked: if Ukraine joined NATO, would it withdraw from Donbas? He told reporters this was the first time NATO membership had been linked to territory.

Here's the odd part. He said the areas the US asked about are ones Russia does not control. So the idea is about Ukraine leaving ground it still holds.

His answer was no. He said he refused to cede territory, though he added that the question means "NATO is back on the agenda."

But he left one door open. If the US and other NATO partners confirmed a full package, with security guarantees, military and reconstruction funds, and a recovery plan, he would ask Ukrainians directly: "do we accept it or not?" Any deal, he stressed, would not recognise occupied territories as Russian.

He was just as firm on neutrality. Committing Ukraine to neutrality would require changing its constitution, he said, so "that's not going to happen." His dry add-on: "We're not in NATO right now anyway."

Why does NATO matter so much? Putin has long called Ukrainian membership a red line, and cited it as one reason for the full invasion in 2022. Zelenskyy said Putin's wish to change borders in Europe is a fact, and a dangerous one.

Meanwhile the fighting did not pause for the talks. Russian officials said about 1,200 Ukrainian drones were fired at Russia on Friday, with oil infrastructure among the targets. Ukraine reported attacks on a hospital and on the port of Odesa.

Europe is lining up too. EU leaders at the Ireland summit agreed its team for any peace talks would be Britain, France, Germany, Italy and Poland plus EU officials. They would meet US and Ukrainian officials, but not Russia.

So the open question is whether anyone will actually put that full package on paper. Until then, Zelenskyy has no vote to call.

Key facts

  • Zelenskyy says US negotiators asked if Ukraine would withdraw from Donbas in exchange for joining NATO.
  • He said the areas in question are ones Russia does not control, and that he refuses to cede territory.
  • He would put a full package of guarantees and funding to a national referendum.
  • He rejected enshrining neutrality, saying it would require changing the constitution.

Voyager 1 is about to be one light-day from Earth

After nearly five decades of flying, a 1977 probe reaches the distance light covers in a single day.

Light covers one light-day in, well, one day. Voyager 1 has needed about 49 years to cover the same distance.

NASA says that on Wednesday the probe will reach at least 16 billion miles travelled since its 1977 launch. That equals one light-day, the distance light travels in 24 hours.

Here's the catch. Voyager 1 is moving at roughly 36,000 miles per hour, which is fast by any human standard. Space is just that big.

How big? NASA expects it to take 18,000 years to travel a full light-year, the distance light covers in a single year. It would need double that before it nears another star.

So where is it now? In interstellar space, the vast gap between stars. Its primary planetary mission ended with a Saturn flyby in 1980, and it has kept going ever since.

Here's the clever bit: it is still working. The plutonium-powered probe sends data home daily, including the strength and direction of the magnetic field, plus readings on pressure, dust and galactic cosmic rays.

Its twin, Voyager 2, launched shortly after and is expected to reach one light-day from Earth in 2035.

Who looks after them? A skeleton team of NASA engineers, who send commands roughly once a week, and more if a probe gets into trouble.

Trouble did show up. In October the team briefly lost communication with Voyager 1, and they have not yet figured out why.

Project manager Suzanne Dodd called Voyager 1 "a national treasure" and told The Associated Press the two probes' activities are akin to those of weather satellites. Voyager 1 also took the famous "Pale Blue Dot" photo of Earth in 1990.

"All of us, everyone on Earth, have been touched by these missions," the Planetary Society's Bill Nye said in a statement.

For now, the question the team has yet to answer is what went quiet in October.

Key facts

  • Voyager 1 launched in 1977 and will reach at least 16 billion miles, one light-day, on Wednesday.
  • It travels about 36,000 miles per hour; NASA expects 18,000 years to cover a light-year.
  • Voyager 2 should reach one light-day in 2035.
  • The team briefly lost contact with Voyager 1 in October and does not know why.

Anthropic's growth "slowdown" may just be everyone else's speed

Anthropic's quarterly growth fell from 147% to 45%, but 45% is roughly the pace of the whole AI economy.

When a company's growth rate drops from 147% a quarter to 45%, it sounds like it hit a wall. Many people read Anthropic's numbers exactly that way, as a stall.

Analyst Azeem Azhar, who writes the Exponential View newsletter, thinks that reading is wrong. The question to ask is: slow compared to what?

Here's the catch. 45% a quarter is the pace of the entire AI economy right now.

Start with the money. AI revenue reached $188bn in the twelve months to October. It now runs at $345bn a year, which is about $29bn a month.

That is 4.2 times its level a year ago. Spread over four quarters, 4.2 times works out to about 43% growth each quarter.

So Anthropic at 45% has not stalled. It has fallen back in line with the market. Year on year, it is still up about 1,000%.

Azhar's point is that the odd number was the 147%, not the 45%. The earlier rate was the outlier, and the slowdown is a return to normal.

He calls the recent chaos a flight to quality, not a collapse. It followed a correction in AI companies and a day when the Nasdaq 100 had its worst day since March 2020. Spending, he says, keeps rising.

What backs that up? Azhar says that in conversations with non-tech companies and the consultancies that serve them, large firms are slowly figuring out how to get results from AI. They intend to spend more next year than this year.

So the story people told themselves was a company running out of road. The numbers say a whole market growing at 43% a quarter, with one company keeping pace.

  • Anthropic's quarterly growth fell from 147% to 45%.
  • AI revenue hit $188bn in the twelve months to October and now runs at $345bn a year, 4.2 times a year ago.
  • That is about 43% growth a quarter for the whole AI market.

The teenage runaway who became China's paramount leader

In 2012, Xi Jinping took over the Chinese Communist Party, decades after he ran away from a poor village as a 15-year-old.

In 1968, Xi Jinping was 15 years old, and his father had been imprisoned. Xi left Beijing and arrived in Liangjiahe, a village in Shaanxi, as part of the Down to the Countryside Movement.

At the time, the Yan'an area around it was one of the poorest places in the country. After a few months, unable to stand rural life, Xi ran away to Beijing.

Here's the catch. He was arrested in a crackdown on deserters from the countryside and sent to a work camp to dig ditches. He later went back to the village after his aunt and uncle persuaded him.

Now jump ahead. In 2012, Xi replaced Hu Jintao as General Secretary of the Chinese Communist Party, which made him China's paramount leader. So how does a ditch-digging runaway get there?

Slowly, and through the same village. He spent seven years in Liangjiahe, living in a yaodong, a type of cave house found in northern China.

His father's disgrace kept getting in the way. By one account, his first application to join the party was rejected because of his father's status. The second was approved after a new policy of not judging children by their parents.

He went on to become the village's party branch chairman. In 1975 he applied to Tsinghua University in all three of his allowed choices, and studied chemical engineering there from 1975 to 1979.

Then came the climb. He was governor of Fujian from 1999 to 2002, then governor and party secretary of Zhejiang until 2007. That year he joined the Politburo Standing Committee, the party's top circle. He became vice president in 2008 and vice chairman of the Central Military Commission in 2010.

Notice the pattern. In the 1970s, a change in the rules let him into the party. In 2018, Xi amended the constitution to remove presidential term limits, and after his re-election in 2023 he became the first Chinese president to serve more than two terms.

Key facts

  • Xi was 15 when his father was imprisoned and he was sent to Liangjiahe village in 1968.
  • He spent seven years in Liangjiahe, living in a cave house called a yaodong.
  • He succeeded Hu Jintao as China's leader in 2012.
  • In 2018 he amended the constitution to remove presidential term limits.

Learn · Physics from first principles

Open problems: the gaps physics has not filled yet

Lesson 15 of 15 in Physics from first principles. Every science has a list of what it cannot explain, and physics keeps its list in public.

Finish most of a jigsaw puzzle and your eye goes straight to the holes. The finished parts are nice, but the holes are where the next bit of the picture is hiding. Physics works the same way, and today's lesson is a tour of its holes.

They come in two kinds. Some are theoretical: our best theories cannot explain something we have actually observed. Others are experimental: we have an idea, but nobody has worked out how to build a test for it. The list includes the absolute mass of neutrinos, why there is more matter than antimatter, and what dark matter and dark energy really are.

Here's the biggest crack. The Standard Model, the mathematical framework for the particles, does not fit with general relativity, our theory of gravity. Where conditions get extreme, both theories break down: at the Big Bang, and at the centers of black holes beyond their event horizons.

Do holes ever close? Yes, and sometimes fast. In 1900, Lord Kelvin named "two clouds" over classical physics: no evidence for the luminiferous aether, and the equipartition theorem failing to predict how solids hold heat. Within a decade, special relativity and quantum mechanics cleared them. That's the twist: the two clouds were the doorways to the two great theories of the 20th century.

Now a worked example, with numbers. In 1984, the mathematical physicist Barry Simon listed 15 open problems on quantum operators. By 2000, about 5 were solved. That is a third of the list in 16 years, so roughly one problem every three years, with about 10 still standing. He then released a second list of 15, and at least 3 of those have since been solved. Compare the 10 millennium problems proposed at the Strings 2000 conference: as of 2025, none has been conclusively solved.

Some questions are bigger than any list. Vitaly Ginzburg, a Nobel laureate, compiled his own list from 1971 to 2005, ending with 30 problems. He set apart three "great problems": the arrow of time, the interpretations of quantum mechanics, and whether life can arise from physics alone.

There is no lesson 16, so the last question is yours to carry out the door. Which hole will close next, and will it turn out, like Kelvin's clouds, to be the start of a whole new theory?

Key facts

  • In 1900 Lord Kelvin named two clouds over classical physics; both were solved within a decade by special relativity and quantum mechanics.
  • Barry Simon's 1984 list had 15 open problems; about 5 were solved by 2000.
  • The Standard Model is mathematically inconsistent with general relativity, so both break down at the Big Bang and inside black holes.
  • Of the 10 millennium problems proposed at Strings 2000, none has been conclusively solved as of 2025.

The Day, Connected

What builds beneath the headlines

A sudden event can expose a pressure that was there all along.

A router upgrade stopped US flights. It sounds like a problem that appeared in an instant, but one remote connection already carried enough weight to disrupt air traffic control when it failed. You see the break in a headline. The dependence underneath is much harder to see.

The same gap appears in trust. Italy's fine over Facebook photo permission turns on a use that users were not clearly told about. A permission box is a tiny moment; understanding what you have permitted takes more. Who explains the rules, and who gets to write them, can matter long before a dispute becomes news.

Then there is pressure that no single conversation can settle. Zelenskyy says US negotiators asked whether Ukraine would leave parts of Donbas to join NATO. His answer put a full package to a national vote. Beneath the proposal sits a harder question: who can decide what a country gives up, and what would make that decision hold?

Some processes barely offer a moment you can point to. Scientists warn the 1.5°C warming line could arrive by 2029, after five years of stalled climate action. News is good at showing us the warning, the failure, the proposal. The world underneath moves through accumulated choices and dependencies, often until one event makes them visible. When a headline catches your eye, what had to be building for that event to happen?

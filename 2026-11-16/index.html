Cover story

Six drones aimed at a US radar site in Bahrain

Iran's latest attack on Bahrain is part of a fight over who controls the Strait of Hormuz.

Iran says it sent six drones toward a US air-defence radar facility in Bahrain. Bahrain says its defences destroyed the attack before it could achieve its goal. Why would a radar site in Bahrain matter so much?

The answer lies nearby, in the Strait of Hormuz. About a fifth of the world's oil and gas supply usually flows through it. Put another way, for every five parts of that supply, roughly one normally passes through this waterway.

Shipping there has been restricted since war broke out in February. Iran insists it should control the strait and has attacked traffic it calls "unauthorised". The US military, meanwhile, is protecting tankers carrying oil out of Gulf Arab states.

That makes the radar target easier to understand. Radar helps air defences detect incoming threats. Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, or IRGC, says the facility it targeted was at al-Riffa air base. Bahrain's military says the attack failed.

This was Iran's second attack on Bahrain in three days. On Friday, the IRGC claimed it had struck a US early warning radar system there. The US denied that claim and said its forces shot down both drones, with no injuries or damage.

There was also an exchange around Qeshm Island in the strait earlier this week. US officials told CBS that US strikes there were limited and defensive, following IRGC fire at a US aircraft. The IRGC said it shot down US drones near the island.

So why are the two sides still fighting over the waterway? They reached a preliminary agreement in June to end the war and reopen it. That agreement collapsed within weeks after Iranian attacks on shipping resumed and the US reinstated a naval blockade of Iranian ports.

Iranian negotiators had since discussed a proposal with mediators to reopen the strait if the US met several conditions. But this week Donald Trump said the US was no longer interested in a deal to end the war with Iran.

Bahrain's account says Sunday's drones did not achieve their objective. That leaves the larger question open: with attacks continuing and the earlier agreement gone, how will ships regain passage through a strait that normally carries one part in five of the world's oil and gas supply?

  • Bahrain's military said it intercepted and destroyed the attack.
  • About a fifth of the world's oil and gas supply usually flows through the Strait of Hormuz.
  • A preliminary US-Iran agreement to end the war and reopen the strait collapsed within weeks.

Modi's Manila trip may include a Quad summit

India's talks with Southeast Asia centre on the sea, while a meeting with three other leaders is still being planned.

Narendra Modi is heading to Manila for two summits. A third, with Donald Trump and the leaders of Japan and Australia, is still being worked out.

The scheduled meetings are the ASEAN-India Summit and the East Asia Summit, during Modi's November 16-17 visit. ASEAN is a group of Southeast Asian nations. So why is the sea such a large part of India's talks with them?

India and Southeast Asia occupy adjoining maritime zones, Indian official Rudrendra Tandon says. He argues that stronger ties can help both sides handle rapid geopolitical change. This year, India and ASEAN have marked a Year of Maritime Cooperation.

That cooperation covers maritime security, safety, connections across the water and economic activity tied to the sea. It puts a practical question beside the security one: how do countries keep those connections working? Economic cooperation and development partnership are also on the summit agenda.

The Philippines is putting rules on the table, too. Opening the ASEAN summit in Manila on Sunday, President Ferdinand Marcos Jr. called for stronger maritime cooperation and an effective code of conduct in the South China Sea, in line with international law. A code of conduct means agreed rules for how countries behave.

Trade gives the talks another reason to focus on connections. Officials say India and ASEAN are working to strengthen trade and investment, improve connectivity and make supply chains more varied. Supply chains are the steps that get goods from producers to buyers.

There is also a development effort with a narrower aim. Indian officials say support for an ASEAN integration initiative helps build capacity in Cambodia, Laos, Myanmar and Timor Leste, with the aim of narrowing development gaps.

Then comes the meeting that has yet to be confirmed. A Quad leaders' summit is being planned alongside the ASEAN gatherings. The Quad brings together India, the United States, Japan and Australia; Modi, Trump, Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi and Australian Prime Minister Anthony Albanese are expected to attend.

US official Dylan Johnson said work was underway to formalise that meeting. He said Quad foreign ministers have met repeatedly over the past two years. A meeting of the four leaders would bring another conversation about the region into the same visit.

Modi is scheduled to attend the ASEAN-India and East Asia summits on Tuesday. The next thing to watch is whether the Quad meeting joins them.

Bangladesh plans its first sovereign bond abroad

The government wants foreign currency for development projects, but the size and market of its planned bond are still undecided.

Why borrow abroad if Bangladesh can already sell bonds at home? Finance and Planning Minister Amir Khosru Mahmud Chowdhury gave a plain answer on 15 November: development projects need foreign currency. Selling a bond in the local market does not settle that need.

That is the puzzle behind the government's plan for its first sovereign bond in international markets, starting from early January. A bond lets a government borrow from investors and repay them later with interest. Where it sells that bond matters when it needs foreign currency.

Khosru said the government wants more ways to finance development, beyond its usual multilateral and bilateral lenders. It is also looking at debt management as external repayments and interest costs rise. More funding choices could help, but each new loan still has to be repaid.

So what is actually ready? Prime Minister's Special Assistant Tanvir Shariar Gani said the government wants to enter the international market within two to three months. New York and London are possible markets. The bond's size and the amount to raise have yet to be decided.

Khosru also said the government is considering dollar-denominated bonds in New York and an equity fund focused on Bangladesh in Hong Kong. He mentioned possible bonds in Japan and China, and Sukuk bonds at home, for the future. These are options he described, rather than issued bonds.

There is another question: if the government is looking for investors, what happens to its International Monetary Fund loan programme? Khosru said Bangladesh is staying in it. The government wants to adjust the programme and its conditions to fit its development plan and current economic circumstances.

He said two working groups are discussing disagreements with the IMF. He hopes that process will finish by January or February so the programme can return to its normal course. That is a separate negotiation from choosing where and how much to borrow through a bond.

Khosru said the IMF conditions were agreed under the previous government and could put pressure on ordinary people and businesses if implemented overnight. He also wants development partners' programmes to fit together more closely, because differences between them make projects harder to carry out.

Watch early January. An issued bond would answer the question the announcement cannot yet answer: how much foreign currency the government will seek from investors, and in which market.

  • He said local bonds do not meet development projects' need for foreign currency.

NASA says 2026 El Niño could outgrow the 1997 event

The Pacific looks strikingly like it did before a powerful El Niño, but the weather may take a different path.

Josh Willis says this El Niño is on track to be even bigger than the powerful 1997 event. Willis, a scientist at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, is looking at the height of the Pacific Ocean to make that comparison. How can a change in sea level tell him anything about the weather?

Start with what usually happens. Steady winds push warm surface water toward the western Pacific. Water piles up there, leaving the ocean surface slightly higher in the west than in the east.

When those winds weaken, warm water moves back east. That shift can weaken the winds further, allowing still more warm water to spread. This is part of El Niño, a recurring rise in ocean temperature that can change weather patterns far beyond the Pacific.

Here's the clever bit. Warm seawater expands, so satellites can spot where heat is gathering by measuring small changes in ocean height. NASA has 35 years of measurements from missions that followed nearly identical paths, giving scientists a way to compare events across decades.

The October measurements show an unusually high ocean surface in the eastern Pacific in both 1997 and 2026. Warm water also stretches along the equator toward the South American coast in both cases. The 2026 event reached this point more abruptly.

That resemblance matters because the 1997-98 El Niño brought substantial rain to California and other parts of the southern United States. It raises the possibility of similar conditions this winter. But the ocean has a complication for anyone hoping to read a weather forecast straight from the map.

The powerful 2015-16 El Niño also had warm water and raised sea levels along the equator. Its warm water did not reach toward South America as strongly. That winter, much of the El Niño-related rain fell over Central America and the southeastern United States, while Mexico had significant flooding.

So strength is only part of the story. Where the warmest water gathers, and how the atmosphere responds, also affect where an El Niño's weather lands. As Willis puts it, the search for a typical El Niño is elusive.

The satellites show scientists where the Pacific's heat is gathering. The question they cannot yet settle is where this winter's rain will fall.

Key facts

  • Willis says the 2026 El Niño is on track to be bigger than the 1997 event.
  • NASA compares ocean height using 35 years of satellite observations.
  • The 1997 and 2026 October measurements show similar patterns of warm water reaching toward South America.
  • The 2015-16 event shows that powerful El Niños can bring different rainfall patterns.

A lost hammer led to a Roman treasure hoard

A search for a hammer uncovered a Roman hoard, and a prompt report preserved the clues inside it.

What do you find when you go looking for a lost hammer? Eric Lawes found silver spoons, gold jewellery and Roman coins in a Suffolk farm field on 16 November 1992. The hammer turned up too.

Tenant farmer Peter Whatling had asked Lawes, a retired gardener with a metal detector, to help find it. What Lawes found was far more than the first objects he picked up: 14,865 gold, silver and bronze coins, plus about 200 pieces of silver tableware and gold jewellery.

That many coins would be remarkable on their own. Yet the question this find can help answer is smaller and stranger: how did someone put all those things away? Lawes and Whatling left most of them in the ground.

After taking out a few objects, the two men told the landowners and police. They stopped digging. The next day, archaeologists excavated the hoard, removing some unbroken blocks of material for closer work.

Here is why the pause mattered. The objects were still largely in place inside the remains of an oak chest. Some had been put in smaller boxes or bags; others were wrapped in fabric. Bowls and ladles had been stacked together.

Their positions gave archaeologists a way to work out the chest's original layout, even though the wood had decayed. Lawes's prompt report made that possible. A pile of precious objects had become evidence of how someone had packed them.

The coins date the hoard to after AD 407, around the end of Britain as a Roman province. The packing was careful. Who buried the chest, and why, is unknown.

Its contents are consistent with what one very wealthy family might have owned. They may even be only part of that wealth: the hoard lacks large silver serving vessels and some common kinds of jewellery. Those clues can narrow the possibilities without naming an owner.

The Hoxne Hoard is the largest hoard of late Roman silver and gold found in Britain. It is now in the British Museum, where selected objects are on permanent display. The missing hammer was recovered and donated there too.

The hammer's owner is known. The chest's owner is still a mystery.

  • Eric Lawes found the hoard while searching for Peter Whatling's lost hammer in 1992.
  • It contained 14,865 Roman coins and about 200 items of silver tableware and gold jewellery.

Learn · Physics from first principles

How physics finds its open problems

A failed prediction can show physicists where an explanation is missing.

Put heat into a solid and it warms up. But how much heat should it take? A failure to predict that amount was once one of physics' major open problems.

An open problem is a question the available theories cannot answer, or one that experiments cannot yet test well enough. The gap matters because a theory has to meet the world somewhere: in what we observe and measure.

Here is a worked example. In 1900, Lord Kelvin identified two problems. One was a lack of evidence for the proposed luminiferous aether. The other was a failed prediction about the heat capacity of solids, the amount of heat needed to warm them.

Within a decade, both had been solved: special relativity addressed the first, and quantum mechanics the second. Start with two open problems in 1900; by 1910, the count for Kelvin's pair was zero. An unsolved question can point to an explanation that does not exist yet.

Today's gaps include the nature of dark matter and dark energy, and the absolute mass of neutrinos. There is also a harder clash: the Standard Model and general relativity do not fit together under extreme conditions, including those at the Big Bang and beyond a black hole's event horizon.

That leaves the question for the next lesson: how could you test a proposed answer when the phenomenon itself is so hard to investigate?

Key facts

  • Open problems can be theoretical or experimental.
  • Kelvin identified two problems in 1900; both were solved within a decade.
  • The nature of dark matter and dark energy remains open.
  • The Standard Model and general relativity are incompatible under extreme conditions.

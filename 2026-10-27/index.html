Activists allege Paltan office attack; police deny it

After a procession celebrating an activist's release, union leaders alleged an attack at a party building in Purana Paltan; police denied it.

That leaves a basic question: what happened after a celebratory procession in Purana Paltan? To follow the dispute, you have to start with a different protest earlier on 26 October.

Cultural activists had gathered in Dhanmondi-27 to protest the obstruction of female Baul singers from performing in Sunamganj. Police stopped their planned human chain. Participants said police detained Dipak Shil, a former Bangladesh Chhatra Union president.

Dipak was released around 9pm. Chhatra Union and Jubo Union activists then held a celebratory procession in Purana Paltan. They alleged that Jatiyatabadi Swechchhasebak Dal activists attacked a gathering afterward and that the confrontation spread to the Communist Party of Bangladesh, or CPB, office.

Here is where the accounts split. Around 10:30pm, Chhatra Union president Mahir Shahriar said he and other leaders were inside their office when it came under attack. He alleged that local BNP activists were involved and that the attackers tried to portray his group as Awami League supporters.

Mahir's claim about the label matters because it is part of his explanation for the confrontation. It does not establish who was involved. The report also says local BNP, Jubo Dal and Chhatra Dal leaders and activists were accused of attacking the CPB office.

Chhatra Union leaders alleged that two Jubo Union members were injured and taken away by police. Paltan Police Station officer-in-charge Ariful Alam denied both the attack and the injuries. He said there was tension at the scene and police were present.

At 11:10pm, CPB general secretary Abdullah Al Kafi said he was heading to the office after hearing about the incident. He said a mob had attacked a party office, while adding that this was all he knew so far.

One detail helps place the competing claims. CPB occupies the sixth and seventh floors of Muktibhaban; Chhatra Union and Jubo Union have offices on the eighth. The offices named in the allegations are on different floors of the same building.

The building's layout is clear. What happened inside it that night is still disputed.

Publishers owned by Ambani and Adani groups take India's privacy law to court

A publishers' association says India's data protection law has no exemption for journalism and threatens sources, reporters and RTI users.

A privacy law is meant to protect people. So why are news publishers, including outlets owned by the Ambani and Adani groups, asking India's Supreme Court to knock out parts of one?

The challenge comes from the Digital News Publishers Association, or DNPA, whose members span the media. It targets the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 and the Rules of 2025. A three-judge Bench headed by Chief Justice Surya Kant is scheduled to hear a batch of activists' petitions on October 27, and the DNPA petition is listed before it too.

Here's the first complaint. The petition says the law gives no exemption for journalism as its own kind of data handling. As a result, it says, publishers, editors and reporters fall under notice and prior consent, compelled erasure and verifiable consent.

Think about what that means in practice. Reporting often involves people who would rather not be named, and a consent rule sits awkwardly on top of that.

The second complaint is about government power. Section 36 and Rule 23, read with the Seventh Schedule, let the Central government demand information from any data fiduciary or intermediary. A data fiduciary is simply an organisation that holds people's data.

The petition says that power comes with no judicial oversight, no test of necessity or proportionality, and no notice to the person affected. It calls it "capable of being turned to the identification and targeting of journalistic sources".

The third complaint reaches past newsrooms. Section 44(3) amends the Right to Information Act, the law that lets citizens ask the government for records. The petition says the change puts every category of "personal information" beyond disclosure and creates an absolute bar on seeking information about public offices.

Here's the neat part. The DNPA leans on the Supreme Court's own privacy judgment, which held the right inviolable and fundamental to human dignity and life. Its argument is that a privacy law has ended up contradicting the privacy ruling it should follow.

So the thing to watch is the hearing on October 27. If the Bench agrees that journalism needs its own protection, the law's reach over reporters and RTI users could look very different.

Key facts

  • The Digital News Publishers Association, whose members include outlets owned by the Ambani and Adani groups, has challenged the DPDP Act, 2023 and DPDP Rules, 2025.
  • A three-judge Bench headed by Chief Justice Surya Kant is scheduled to hear a batch of petitions on October 27.
  • The petition says the law has no journalism exemption and lets the Central government requisition information without judicial oversight.
  • It also says Section 44(3) puts personal information beyond RTI disclosure.

US military ready to strike Iran within four days of Trump order

American forces have target lists ready for an order that could come after the midterms, while quietly bracing for immediate retaliation.

If Donald Trump gives the word, American forces in the Middle East are ready to launch a major military operation against Iran within ninety-six hours. That is just four days from an executive order to strikes underway.

The Pentagon already has sufficient forces deployed in the region, and its planning goes far beyond recent skirmishes. Instead of hitting radar sites and rocket launchers that can be quickly restored, the target list takes aim at Iranian energy infrastructure, military installations, and nuclear facilities.

So what is holding the trigger? Trump has not made a final decision, and officials point out that he has previously appeared inclined toward direct action before backing away at the last moment.

Here is the catch. As planners map out potential targets, they are also bracing for the retaliation that would follow immediately.

Intelligence assessments indicate that Iran and Houthi rebels in Yemen would waste no time striking American personnel and facilities across the Middle East. An unclassified memo from late October warned that Iranian plotting against Americans abroad had already reached advanced stages.

Think of it like boarding up windows before a hurricane hits. The US Embassy in Riyadh has already ordered non-essential staff to leave, other diplomatic missions are capping staff numbers, and extra troops scheduled to deploy have been told to stay home.

Out on the water, the pressure is already building. American forces are maintaining a blockade of Iranian ports and clearing mines from the Strait of Hormuz, where one US official describes the standoff as untenable over the long term while oil prices stay high.

Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu has been lobbying hard for a joint attack, but Washington asked him to wait until the administration is ready. Among planners inside the Pentagon, some now believe the question is when Trump will give the order, rather than whether he will.

The next date to watch is the US midterm vote. Officials familiar with the planning say that if an order comes, it will most likely arrive between those elections and the end of the year.

OpenAI claims to break a longstanding computer science rule

OpenAI says an algorithm disproved a 1999 hypothesis, bringing in the original theorists to help verify the claim.

Since 1999, computer scientists have widely believed that a basic rule of math was unbreakable. OpenAI now claims an algorithm proved it wrong.

The rule is called the Exponential Time Hypothesis. In plain terms, it says that certain logic puzzles take an impossibly long time to solve because computers have to check too many combinations.

Think of a giant group chat where every member has picky rules about who they are willing to talk to. As more people join, testing every possible combination quickly outlasts the life of the universe.

OpenAI says its new algorithm found a shortcut that counts these solutions in subexponential time. If the math holds up, it knocks down a longstanding barrier in computer science.

The claim comes alongside others. Less than a month after releasing hundreds of manuscripts with solutions to old math questions, the company announced three new discoveries spanning math, computer science, and quantum physics.

Here is the twist. Instead of just releasing papers, OpenAI recruited outside academics as shepherds to help explain what the AI actually produced.

For the computer science proof, the company went straight to the source. It brought in Russell Impagliazzo and Ramamohan Paturi, who were part of the group that proposed the hypothesis in 1999, alongside MIT professor Ryan Williams.

Wait, why bring in the original creators? Because nobody knows for sure if the machine is right, and checking machine-generated math takes immense human labor.

The group decided to release the write-up and proof on GitHub so anyone can inspect the work. But that shepherd approach immediately ran into pushback.

An advisory panel of mathematicians put together by OpenAI warned that giving chosen researchers early access creates borrowed reputation and biased selection. The panel urged AI companies to release results on public repositories instead of handpicking who gets first look.

The group released an open letter asking how credit should work when an AI finds the answers and humans do the checking. Nobody has an answer yet.

Key facts

  • OpenAI announced three discoveries across math, computer science, and physics, claiming to refute the 1999 Exponential Time Hypothesis.
  • The company recruited outside academic shepherds, including the hypothesis's original co-creators Russell Impagliazzo and Ramamohan Paturi, to evaluate the proof.
  • OpenAI's math advisory panel warned against granting favored researchers early access to AI discoveries, citing biased selection and borrowed reputation.

Artificial intelligence might not be a black box after all

A researcher who helped prove AI was an unreadable tangle now says its inner workings make sense.

Nick Cammarata used to believe artificial intelligence was an untangleable mess. In 2020, while studying a Google image model, he found a single neuron that fired for two completely unrelated things: cat faces and the fronts of cars.

Think of a kitchen drawer where someone tossed batteries and spoons together. The leading theory was that a network ran low on neurons and jammed unrelated ideas into the same slot. If that was true, inspecting an individual neuron could never tell you what the model was thinking.

Here is the twist. When Cammarata revisited that cat-and-car neuron, he noticed both images shared a prominent V shape. When he tested further, the same neuron responded to the corner of a pool table and the pointed nose of a Japanese bullet train.

Instead of grouping random junk, the neuron was simply tracking a visual shape. As the network learned, it branched into neighboring concepts, a process Cammarata calls recursive generalization.

Now at the startup Goodfire, Cammarata has laid out his case in a 26,000-word post, roughly the length of a short novel. He argues that even the largest models may be far simpler to read than anyone assumed.

So how common is this order? In tests on the language model Qwen3-32B, roughly nine out of ten neurons tracked just one main concept. Cammarata says he found similar results inside Kimi K3, a model with one trillion parameters.

Not everyone is convinced. Chris Potts, a Stanford University researcher, told MIT Technology Review that looking at thousands of examples is no substitute for rigorous evaluation. Jacob Hilton at the Alignment Research Center wrote on X that understanding individual neurons misses how they connect into larger circuits.

Cammarata himself cautions that people have taken his findings too far. As he told Tech Review, he still has big questions about whether combinations of neurons matter more than individual ones.

The next milestone to watch is Cammarata's forthcoming technical paper on the trillion-parameter Kimi model. That study will show whether his optimism holds up under formal testing.

Why a Catholic priest poured blood over draft records

Philip Berrigan fought in World War II, became a Catholic priest, and used his own blood to stop the military draft.

Philip Berrigan was an American Catholic priest who had fought in the artillery during the Battle of the Bulge and served as an infantry lieutenant in World War II. On October 27, 1967, he decided that prayers alone would never halt the Vietnam War. He walked into the Selective Service Board at the Custom House in Baltimore, carrying a mixture of his own blood and poultry blood.

Berrigan and two fellow activists poured the blood directly over government filing cabinets. Their goal was simple: destroy the physical draft records used to order young men into military combat.

Why blood? In a written statement, Berrigan called it a sacrificial and constructive act meant to protest what he described as the pitiful waste of American and Vietnamese blood in Indochina.

To understand how an ordained priest reached that point, you have to look at what shaped him. Military boot camp in the American South had exposed Berrigan to racial segregation and racism, an experience that deeply affected him. After leaving the army, he joined the Josephites, a Catholic religious society dedicated to serving African Americans, and was ordained in 1955.

He threw himself into the civil rights movement, taking part in sit-ins, bus boycotts, and desegregation marches. When he began organizing public protests against the Vietnam War, church leadership disapproved and repeatedly transferred him to new postings, but the moves never slowed him down.

His protest group became known as the Baltimore Four. While three of them poured blood, the fourth member, an Air Force veteran and pastor named James Mengel, chose not to pour anything. Instead, Mengel handed paperback scriptures to draft board workers, police officers, and newsmen.

The legal consequences came quickly. A federal court sentenced Berrigan to six years in prison. Yet six months later, while out on bail, he joined eight others, including his Jesuit brother Daniel, to burn six hundred draft records with homemade napalm in Catonsville, Maryland.

That defiance defined the rest of his life. In 1973, Berrigan married a former nun named Elizabeth McAlister, an act that saw both excommunicated before being reinstated. Over their twenty-nine years of marriage, the two spent eleven years separated because one or both were locked in prison for anti-war protests.

  • While out on bail, Berrigan and eight others, including his Jesuit brother Daniel, burned six hundred draft records with homemade napalm.

The universe's ending is back in question

New galaxy measurements have reopened a question physicists thought they had answered: how will the universe end?

Astrophysicist Ethan Siegel is explaining why a universe headed for endless expansion might have an uncertain ending after all. New measurements of galaxies across vast stretches of cosmic time suggest the answer may depend on something physicists thought they understood.

By the end of the 20th century, physicists had a picture of the far future. Space would keep expanding. Galaxies would drift so far apart that, eventually, only our immediate neighborhood would remain in view. A universe full of galaxies would look much emptier.

So what could disturb that picture? Dark energy. That is the name scientists give to whatever is behind the universe's accelerating expansion. You cannot put it in a jar and watch it work; its behavior has to be inferred from what happens across space.

Think of a phone battery. If you assume it drains at a steady rate, you can guess when it will run out. Change the rate, and the forecast changes. The same problem sits inside the universe's ending: a prediction depends on how something behaves over time.

Here's the clever bit. Looking far away also means looking back. Light from a distant galaxy has taken time to reach us, so galaxies at different distances let scientists examine different chapters of cosmic history. Their arrangement gives clues to how expansion behaved in those chapters.

The new galaxy measurements are why Siegel is returning to the question. They suggest dark energy may behave differently from what the old forecast assumed. The universe's ending is still uncertain.

And this is where the puzzle gets bigger. A change to the story of expansion matters when the question is what happens forever. A measurement of past galaxies leaves the far future unresolved. Scientists still have to work out what dark energy does and whether the apparent change holds up.

For now, the old image is worth keeping in your head: galaxies drifting away until our neighborhood seems alone. What has shifted is confidence in the rule used to extend that image indefinitely.

That once lonely future now rests on a question the galaxies are helping scientists ask again: does dark energy stay the same?

Key facts

  • By the end of the 20th century, physicists believed the universe would expand forever.
  • That forecast left only our immediate galactic neighborhood in view.
  • New measurements of galaxies across cosmic time suggest dark energy may behave differently than expected.

Open problems in physics

A theory can work well and still leave questions it cannot answer or test.

A lamp turns on when you flip a switch. That everyday reliability can make physics feel complete. Yet its current theories break down under extreme conditions, including at the center of a black hole beyond its event horizon.

What makes a problem "open"? Sometimes an observation or experimental result exists, but current theories cannot explain it. Sometimes there is a proposed explanation, but making an experiment to test it remains a challenge.

Here is a deeper puzzle. The Standard Model describes particles and their interactions; general relativity describes gravity. Their mathematical frameworks do not fit together. Under extreme conditions, such as those associated with the Big Bang, both break down.

That leaves questions at different levels. Physicists have yet to determine the absolute mass of neutrinos or identify the nature of dark matter. They also need a way to understand what happens where their current frameworks fail.

Take a concrete count. Physicists proposed 10 theoretical problems at the Strings 2000 conference. None had been conclusively solved as of 2025. That is 0 out of 10 after 25 years: writing down a question can be much easier than settling it.

So the lamp was a starting point, not a promise that every part of nature is understood. An open problem tells you where an explanation runs out, a test is hard to make, or two theories clash. How could physicists test a new idea where their current theories break down?

Key facts

  • Open problems can be theoretical or experimental.
  • The Standard Model is inconsistent with general relativity.
  • The nature of dark matter remains an open question.
  • None of the 10 Strings 2000 problems had been conclusively solved as of 2025.

The slow work beneath the headlines

A closing thought on trust, dependence and the rules people live by.

A morning paper makes the world look as if it moves in jumps. A government concedes. A company asks investors for a vast sum. A law lands in court. You see the moment something becomes news, but the pressure behind it may have been building for years. That is the puzzle in this stack of stories: why do so many sudden events seem to be about old questions?

One question is who gets heard, and who gets to set the rules. Serbia's student-led protest became an election victory; Indian publishers are challenging a privacy law they say threatens journalism. These are different fights, but both ask whether people can contest decisions made over their heads. Trust matters here because a rule has to hold up when the people affected by it push back.

Another question is what a society depends on, and how it prepares when that dependence feels exposed. Anthropic hopes to raise $100 billion; Singapore plans to store Saudi oil in caverns. One story is about finding money to build, the other about keeping a supply close. In both, a future need is shaping choices made now, before anyone knows exactly how that future will arrive.

News is good at showing the visible step: the vote, the filing, the agreement. The slower story is how trust is earned or lost, how dependence grows, and who has room to change the rules. Tomorrow's headline may bring a different cast and a different shock. What had to build up for it to happen, and who gets to decide what follows?

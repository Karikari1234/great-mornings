Cover story

Nvidia's CEO quit, came back in under two weeks, and the president says he helped

Jensen Huang is Nvidia's CEO again, and President Trump says he helped make it happen, which has people asking who should pick a company's boss.

Jensen Huang left the top job at the world's most valuable chipmaker less than two weeks ago. On Monday he got it back, and the sitting US president says he was in the room.

Here's the short version. Huang resigned amid reports of extramarital affairs, including with Alice Huang, a woman alleged to be a Chinese spy. Beijing denies she is a spy.

Nvidia's board had started investigating, but Huang quit before it finished. Chief Financial Officer Colette Kress took over as interim chief executive while the board looked for a permanent replacement.

Then came the pushback. A petition to bring him back collected more than 180,000 signatures, Elon Musk's among them.

Investors had their own view. Analyst Dan Ives estimated that losing Huang could wipe as much as 30 percent off the company's value.

So why the U-turn? A few days earlier, the board had ruled out bringing him back. Now board chair Harvey C Jones says reinstating Huang is in the best interests of stockholders, employees, partners and customers.

Here's where Washington comes in. Trump says he took part in the board meeting and played a central role, after Silicon Valley figures urged him to step in. He told CNBC the stock market reaction "probably" factored in, though he weighed Huang's long-term value more.

A Boston College professor, Aleksandar Tomic, calls it unprecedented for a sitting president to publicly pressure a corporate board like this. Another expert, Martin Mulyadi, says a board can listen to government, but should not let an outsider choose the CEO without explaining why to shareholders.

The timing is awkward. The Justice Department and the SEC are still investigating Nvidia. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has accused it of "seemingly illicit sales" to China and wants an outside CEO.

So the question nobody has answered is how much Trump actually shaped the decision, and whether Nvidia's shareholders will ever get a full explanation.

Key facts

  • Huang resigned, then was reinstated as Nvidia CEO less than two weeks later.
  • A petition for his return drew more than 180,000 signatures.
  • Trump says he took part in the board meeting and was central to the decision.
  • The DOJ and SEC are still investigating Nvidia.

After a glacier collapse flooded Nepal in August, India's prime minister announced a 240 billion rupee package for rebuilding.

On August 26, part of a glacier on Nepal's border with China collapsed. The mass of mud and rock that came down crashed into the Trishuli river and kept going.

The flood washed away villages. It snapped highways, bridges and power lines, and buried homes in mud. About 1,500 people were killed, and another 5,200 are missing and presumed dead.

Now, nearly three months later, Nepal's prime minister Balendra Shah was in New Delhi. It was his first bilateral trip since taking power in March.

There, India's prime minister Narendra Modi announced a development cooperation package of 240 billion Nepalese rupees, which is $1.62 billion. Modi said it was meant to give fresh impetus to Nepal's reconstruction and long-term development.

So what is the money for? Modi named three jobs: helping affected families get their lives back on track, creating new opportunities, and strengthening Nepal's ability to deal with future disasters.

Why India? Think of the map. Nepal is a poor country wedged between two giants, India and China. Its border with India is open, and hundreds of thousands of Nepalis live and work on the Indian side.

India was also the first to respond after the flood. It sent several plane-loads of aid to restore communication links that had been cut in the mountains.

Shah thanked India for acting as a first responder for Nepal in difficult hours. He also welcomed Indian investors to Nepal and invited Modi to visit.

The two leaders signed agreements to boost their economic relationship. Then they opened a train link connecting the two countries together.

Here is the part I like. The flood started in the mountains, and the answer is partly a railway that runs across a border.

Ukraine hit a Russian warship in Libya, nearly 2,000 miles from Moscow

Ukraine sank a Russian corvette in a Libyan port, and the war has been spreading well beyond its borders for a while.

The captain of Russia's corvette Merkury might well have thought his ship was safe. It was anchored in a Mediterranean harbour nearly 2,000 miles from Moscow. It wasn't.

The night-time attack in Benghazi, Libya, was co-ordinated by Ukraine's SBU intelligence agency and its armed forces. A cargo ship berthed alongside was also hit. Ukraine's President Zelensky called it "an exemplary operation". Russia called it "a dangerous escalation".

So is the war spreading? BBC security correspondent Frank Gardner says yes, and that it already was. This attack just raises the stakes another notch.

Here's the catch. Libya says it is not a party to this war. But the ships were in the eastern part of the country, which is run by the pro-Moscow warlord Khalifa Haftar. The United Nations mission in Libya has already voiced "deep concern".

The reach is the new part. Zelensky likes to call Ukraine's long-range drone strikes "long-range sanctions". One hit a Russian dry-cargo ship in the Gulf of Finland at the weekend, which the Kremlin called terrorism. Another, Operation Spider's Web, used a specially adapted lorry to release a swarm of explosive drones near a Russian bomber base.

Russia's side of the ledger looks similar. On Friday, Russian projectiles landed inside Poland, just 60 miles from an Anglo-French exercise. Poland's PM Donald Tusk called it "a deliberate provocation".

Romania and Moldova are often struck by overflying Russian drones. Germany's Leipzig-Halle airport, a resupply hub for Ukraine's cargo aircraft, was targeted by three explosive drones this autumn. They were found before anyone was hurt. Moscow denies involvement in explosive packages sent across Europe.

Why not hit back harder? Gardner points out that Russia is already heavily sanctioned, so the West's options are limited short of military confrontation, which no one wants.

Which leaves the question from Gardner himself: if Ukraine can hit ships in a nominally neutral harbour so far from home, where does this stop? Global shipping, he notes, will be wondering whether this sets a dangerous precedent.

A planet nearly five times Jupiter's mass circles a small, dim star

Astronomers have found a giant planet around a lightweight star, and the usual recipe for building giant planets can't explain it.

The textbook recipe for a giant planet starts small. Bits of solid material clump into a core, and once that core is heavy enough, it pulls in a huge coat of gas.

That recipe, called core accretion, has a problem with HIP 88962 b. It's a gas giant with about 4.83 times the mass of Jupiter, and it circles a star with only about 0.62 times the mass of our Sun.

To feel the mismatch: Jupiter alone weighs about 318 Earths, so this planet is roughly 1,500 Earths heavy. The study's authors say published core-accretion models for low-mass stars don't produce a planet with this mass on an orbit this long.

Here's the odd part. Nobody has actually seen the planet. A team from Carnegie Science in Washington and Shanghai Jiao Tong and Tsinghua universities watched its star instead, using the 6.5-metre Magellan Clay Telescope in Chile.

That's the clever bit. As a planet orbits, its gravity tugs its star back and forth. This star sways at up to 94.9 metres per second, about 340 kilometres an hour. The wobble alone only gives a minimum mass, so the team added measurements from the Hipparcos and Gaia satellites to pin down the true one.

It took patience. The planet needs about 19.3 years to go round once. The team gathered observations on 41 nights between 2010 and 2025, and that still covers less than one full lap.

Its path is strange too. It sits about 6.15 times the Earth-Sun distance from its star, a bit farther out than Jupiter is from the Sun, on a strongly stretched oval.

So how did it get there? The team favours the main rival idea, gravitational instability: a heavy, cold patch of a young star's gas disk collapses under its own gravity and becomes a planet. They didn't watch it happen, though.

Kaitlin Kratter, a University of Arizona astronomer who wasn't involved, said both ideas struggle to explain this system, and that the stretched orbit is a puzzle for either one.

Which leaves the bigger question. If small stars can host giants like this, how many more are out there? Other possible cold giants around similar stars are still waiting to be confirmed with future Magellan observations.

Key facts

  • HIP 88962 b has about 4.83 times Jupiter's mass; its star has about 0.62 times the Sun's mass.
  • It was found by measuring the star's wobble with the Magellan Clay Telescope in Chile, over observations from 2010 to 2025.
  • The planet takes about 19.3 years per orbit on a strongly elongated path.
  • The authors favour formation by gravitational instability, but an outside astronomer says both main models struggle to explain it.

Put it among 100 people. In 2020, about 26 were Muslim; the projection puts that figure at 36 by 2070. By then, the report expects more than 3.6 billion Muslims worldwide, compared with just under three billion Christians.

So what drives the gap? Pew points mainly to births and the age of the population, rather than people converting. That distinction matters because a young population can keep growing even when birth rates later fall.

In 2020, the median age of Muslims worldwide was 24, against 33 for non-Muslims. The median divides a population into two equal halves by age. More Muslims are therefore approaching or entering the years when people tend to have children.

Birth rates add to that effect. For 2020 to 2025, the report estimates 3.1 children per Muslim woman, compared with 2.0 per non-Muslim woman. Pew expects fertility rates to decline, but the younger age structure can keep population growth going.

The changes will look different from place to place. Britain’s Muslim share is projected to rise from 6.4% in 2020 to 16% in 2070. Over the same period, the share of people there reporting no religion is projected to rise from 40.2% to 52.3%.

There is a geographic shift, too. Pakistan is projected to have the world's largest Muslim population by 2070, followed by India. Indonesia has the largest Muslim population now, while India is expected to remain Hindu-majority even as its Muslim population ranks second.

These are projections, and Pew says they depend on recent patterns broadly continuing. Its researchers point to uncertainty about future religious affiliation: in some countries, departures from organised religion rose faster between 2010 and 2020 than anticipated. How much could that pattern change the 2035 forecast?

Key facts

  • The report projects Muslims will outnumber Christians worldwide by 2035.
  • It projects 80% Muslim population growth from 2020 to 2070, against 29% growth in the world population.
  • The median age of Muslims worldwide was 24 in 2020, compared with 33 for non-Muslims.
  • Pew identifies natural population growth as the main driver.

Darwin's book sold out; its central idea took decades

Darwin's 1859 book sold out on its first day, while broad agreement on natural selection came much later.

How can a book sell out on its first day while its central explanation takes decades to win broad agreement? That happened to Charles Darwin's On the Origin of Species.

Darwin had been puzzling over wildlife and fossils collected during a five-year voyage on HMS Beagle. Different kinds of living things appeared in different places. He wanted to know how that variety came to be.

By 1838, he had devised an answer: natural selection. The short version is that living things vary, and some differences help their bearers survive and have offspring. Over generations, helpful differences can become more common. Small changes can add up as populations branch into different species.

Think of selective breeding, where people choose which plants or animals reproduce because they like a trait. Darwin proposed a similar sorting process without a human chooser. In nature, the struggle to survive and reproduce does the sorting.

Here's the long pause. Darwin discussed the idea with other naturalists but kept researching, with geological work taking priority. He was still writing up the theory in 1858 when Alfred Russel Wallace sent him an essay setting out the same idea.

That changed how the idea was introduced. Darwin and Wallace submitted it jointly to the Linnean Society of London. Then Darwin published On the Origin of Species with evidence in 1859. Its initial print run sold out on the first day.

Fast sales did not settle the harder question. By the 1870s, the scientific community and a majority of the educated public accepted evolution as a fact. But many still preferred explanations that gave natural selection only a small part.

This is the difference to hold onto: evolution says life changes and species share ancestry; natural selection explains one way that change happens. You can accept the first claim while doubting whether the proposed process does most of the work. That was the gap the book's sales could not close.

Broad agreement that natural selection was the basic mechanism came only with the modern evolutionary synthesis, from the 1930s to the 1950s. The book vanished from its first run in a day. Agreement about its explanation took far longer.

Key facts

  • Darwin devised his theory of natural selection in 1838.
  • Wallace sent Darwin an essay outlining the same idea in 1858.
  • The initial print run of On the Origin of Species sold out on its first day in 1859.
  • Broad agreement on natural selection as evolution's basic mechanism developed from the 1930s to the 1950s.

What an open problem in physics means

A gap in physics can mean we cannot explain an observation, or cannot yet test an explanation.

A cup of tea cools while you wait. That feels like the sort of thing physics has figured out. Yet the direction of time itself appears on a physicist's list of great open problems. How can both be true?

Physics begins with observations and tries to explain them. An open problem is a place where that effort stops short: a theory cannot explain something we observe, or an experiment cannot yet test an idea. Knowing what happens is not always the same as knowing why.

Here is a worked example. In 1900, Lord Kelvin pointed to two problems: missing evidence for a proposed medium called the aether, and a failed prediction about how solids store heat. Within a decade, special relativity addressed the first and quantum mechanics the second. Two questions led to two new ways of explaining nature.

Today's questions come in different shapes. Physicists still seek the absolute mass of neutrinos and the nature of dark matter and dark energy. These are names for questions, not answers you need to memorize.

There is also a deeper mismatch. The Standard Model describes particles, while general relativity describes gravity. According to the source, their mathematical frameworks do not fit together under extreme conditions, including those associated with the Big Bang and the centers of black holes.

So the lesson is this: a successful theory can have a boundary. Finding that boundary tells physicists where an explanation or a test is missing. How could an experiment turn one of today's open questions into an answer?

Key facts

  • Open problems can be theoretical or experimental.
  • Kelvin identified two problems in 1900; both were solved within a decade.
  • The Standard Model and general relativity are inconsistent under extreme conditions.
  • Open questions include neutrino mass, dark matter and dark energy.

The slow forces beneath the headlines

Who gets a say, which rules hold, and what lasts after an event ends?

Some of today's biggest questions sit one step behind the event. Trump says he helped Jensen Huang return to Nvidia. The visible part is easy to name: a boss has his job again. The harder part is working out who gets a say when a decision inside a company draws in power from outside it.

That question reaches beyond companies. Yunus says Grameen's borrowers own most of the bank. Ownership tells you who has a stake, but it leaves another question open: what can those people count on when the bank comes under pressure? Trust grows or weakens through what happens to people over time, not through a rule written on paper alone.

Some pressures outlast the event that makes the news. COP31 ended without a deal over fossil fuels; a Lebanese family returned to a burned home after an Israeli withdrawal. A meeting ends. An army leaves. The dispute that blocked an agreement and the damage that survives a handover are still there afterward. A headline can tell you when something happened. It has a harder time showing how long the consequences will last.

That may be why the world can look as though it changes all at once, then prove so slow to change. News catches the moment a return, a failed deal or a homecoming becomes visible. Underneath are people testing who can exercise power, whether a rule will hold, and what an earlier decision has left behind. Those processes keep going after the event has passed. When you read any headline, what changed that day, and what was already at work beneath it?

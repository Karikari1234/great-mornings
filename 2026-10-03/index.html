G7 agrees to release 100 million barrels of fuel reserves

G7 nations will deploy 100 million barrels of oil and diesel over four months after the United States agreed to drop threats of a fuel export ban.

The G7 agreed to release 100 million barrels of oil and diesel from reserves to ease global supply pressures. French President Emmanuel Macron chaired the meeting of leaders to settle the joint plan. Under the agreement, member countries will co-ordinate the release through the International Energy Agency over four months. The measure includes a frontloaded release of diesel within the first 20 days.

The accord followed warnings from President Donald Trump that he would ban American diesel exports if European countries did not release domestic stocks. In a joint statement, G7 governments agreed to refrain from energy export restrictions on one another. Trump wrote on social media on Friday that Europe agreed to release a "massive amount" of diesel oil immediately. He later said at the White House that an export ban on diesel was "never really on the table".

The price of global benchmark Brent crude oil briefly fell below $100 a barrel, then rose back to around $102 by Friday evening. Before the US and Israel invaded Iran, the benchmark traded at around $73. Matt Smith, director of commodities research at Kpler, said prices rose again because of renewed strikes between Saudi Arabia and the Houthis in Yemen.

Diesel supplies have tightened following conflict in the Middle East and an export ban by Russia after Ukrainian attacks on Russian refineries. The US is a primary supplier of the fuel to world markets. According to the US Energy Information Administration, American refineries produce four to five million barrels of diesel daily. Domestic consumption accounts for about 3.6 million barrels, and refiners export the remaining 1.2 to 1.5 million barrels.

The agreement provides relief to countries that depend on imports. In the UK, diesel prices at the pump topped £2 a litre on Friday. The country imports over half of its diesel, and 31% of those shipments come from the US. G7 leaders said they will co-ordinate refinery maintenance schedules to avoid simultaneous shutdowns, and will encourage partner countries to ramp up diesel refining.

Key facts

  • The G7 and partner countries will release 100 million barrels of oil and diesel over four months through the International Energy Agency.
  • Brent crude oil traded at roughly $102 a barrel on Friday evening, compared with around $73 before the US and Israel invaded Iran.
  • United States refineries produce four to five million barrels of diesel daily, consuming about 3.6 million and exporting 1.2 to 1.5 million.
  • Diesel pump prices in the United Kingdom topped £2 a litre on Friday, with 31% of the country's diesel imports supplied by the United States.

Uranium shipment for Rooppur nuclear plant arrives in Dhaka

A delayed cargo flight carrying uranium fuel for the Rooppur plant landed at Dhaka airport late Friday night.

A Russian-made cargo aircraft carrying uranium fuel for the Rooppur Nuclear Power Plant landed in Dhaka late on Friday night, 2 October. The four-engine Ilyushin Il-76 touched down at Hazrat Shahjalal International Airport at around 11:43pm. The consignment is fuel for the first unit of Bangladesh's first nuclear power plant.

The shipment arrived more than a week after its originally scheduled date of 24 September. Delivery was delayed after Pakistan did not grant permission for the aircraft to use its airspace. The aircraft was initially expected on Friday afternoon, and arrival was delayed by several hours.

Science and Technology Minister Faqir Mahbub Anam said on 28 September that the shipment was awaiting clearance from Pakistan. The flight route included Azerbaijan, Kazakhstan, Pakistan, and India. Anam told reporters that clearance had been granted previously and that the flight would be cleared.

Civil Aviation and Tourism Minister M Rashiduzzaman Millat made a surprise visit to the airport earlier on Friday night. He said police had taken necessary security measures for the movement of the uranium shipment.

Airport Executive Director Group Captain Ragib Samad told The Business Standard that information related to the movement of the consignment was classified.

Britain detonates its first atomic bomb off the Australian coast

A plutonium implosion device exploded inside a frigate hull, making the United Kingdom the world's third nuclear power.

Britain detonates its first atomic device on 3 October 1952 in Main Bay off Trimouille Island, in Western Australia's Montebello Islands. The test, code-named Operation Hurricane, used a plutonium implosion device. The successful blast made the United Kingdom the third nuclear power, after the United States and the Soviet Union.

The bomb was exploded inside the hull of the frigate HMS Plym, anchored 350 metres off Trimouille Island. Planners detonated the weapon there to test the effects of an atomic bomb smuggled into a port by ship. The detonation occurred 2.7 metres below the water line. It left a saucer-shaped crater on the seabed 6 metres deep and 300 metres across.

Rear Admiral A. D. Torlesse commanded the naval force for Operation Hurricane. Five Royal Navy vessels, led by the escort carrier HMS Campania as flagship, sailed to the islands with the frigate HMS Plym. Eleven Royal Australian Navy ships, among them the aircraft carrier HMAS Sydney, joined them at the test site. Leonard Tyte of the Atomic Weapons Research Establishment at Aldermaston was the technical director. Technicians assembled the bomb without its radioactive components at Foulness before its voyage to Australia.

The operation followed a January 1947 decision by a British cabinet sub-committee to build nuclear weapons under a project named High Explosive Research. Lord Portal directed the project, and William Penney managed bomb design. British officials initially preferred the Pacific Proving Grounds in the Marshall Islands, with Canada and Australia considered as fallbacks. Prime Minister Clement Attlee requested access to the Montebello Islands from Australian Prime Minister Robert Menzies. The Australian government agreed to the site in May 1951.

British atomic development began during the Second World War under the code name Tube Alloys, after calculations by Otto Frisch and Rudolf Peierls at the University of Birmingham. The 1943 Quebec Agreement merged that work into the American Manhattan Project. After the war ended, the American government stopped technical cooperation under the Atomic Energy Act of 1946. In February 1952, Prime Minister Winston Churchill announced in the House of Commons that the first test would occur in Australia before the end of the year.

Key facts

  • 3 October 1952: Date of the Operation Hurricane atomic test off Trimouille Island.
  • 300 metres: Diameter of the seabed crater created by the underwater blast.
  • William Penney: Head of bomb design for Britain's High Explosive Research project.

Learn · Physics from first principles

Units of measurement give physical quantities an agreed standard

A standard unit provides a definite magnitude that allows physical quantities to be measured as numbers.

Early societies needed rudimentary measures to construct dwellings of an appropriate size and shape. People also needed measures to fashion clothing. Before the decimal metric system began in France during the late 18th century, people determined many units of length by the size of human body parts.

A unit of measurement is a definite magnitude of a physical property, defined and adopted by convention or by law. It is a standard for measurement of that property. Metrology is the science of developing nationally and internationally accepted units. Formally, a unit is a real scalar quantity. Any other quantity of that kind can be compared with it to express the ratio of the two quantities as a number.

Length is a physical quantity. The metre, written with the symbol m, is a unit of length that has a definite predetermined length. When a person references 10 metres, or 10 m, what is meant is 10 times the definite predetermined length called the metre. Any other quantity of that kind is expressed as a multiple of the unit.

Reproducibility of experimental results is central to the scientific method, and a standard system of units facilitates this reproducibility. In 1790, the French National Assembly charged the French Academy of Sciences to create a universal unit system. In 1875, 17 nations signed the Metric Convention Treaty. This treaty established the General Conference of Weights and Measures, which adopted the International System of Units in 1954. This modern metric system replaced the centimetre-gram-second system.

The use of a single unit of measurement has drawbacks. It is impractical to use the same unit for the distance between two cities and the length of a needle. Unit prefixes make large numbers and small fractions easier to read. A system of measurement is a collection of units and rules that relate them to each other. An inch could once be defined in terms of a barleycorn.

As science progressed, researchers needed to relate the measurement systems of different quantities, such as length and weight. The effort to connect traditional systems exposed inconsistencies and led to new units. How do scientists relate systems of measurement for different physical quantities?

Key facts

  • In 1790, the French National Assembly charged the French Academy of Sciences to create a universal unit system.
  • Seventeen nations signed the Metric Convention Treaty in 1875.
  • The General Conference of Weights and Measures adopted the International System of Units in 1954.

Cover story

Gaza hospital strike kills six, and both sides tell opposite stories

An Israeli strike hit the surgical building of Nasser hospital in southern Gaza, killing six people, while Israel says it targeted Hamas fighters and Hamas denies it.

Dr Mohammed Qdeih, a surgeon, had just finished work around 00:30 and was getting ready to sleep when an explosion came. He told the BBC his whole body was trapped under the rubble.

He crawled out in the dark, with no lights. A colleague of his, Dr Rizq Abu Shaira, a surgeon, did not. Qdeih says he had been lying in a bed near him.

Here is what the health ministry says. Gaza's health ministry says six people were killed and about 30 injured in an Israeli air strike on Nasser hospital. That includes medical staff, patients and companions.

Nasser is the largest hospital in southern Gaza and serves almost one million people. The hospital's director, Dr Ahmed al-Fara, said two missiles hit the al-Yasin surgery building, which holds surgical wards and intensive care units. One of them did not explode.

Here's the catch. The two sides describe the same strike in opposite ways.

The Israeli military says it was a precise strike on the commander of Hamas's Khan Younis Brigade and other fighters who were hiding inside. It says they had turned the compound into a headquarters. It also says it gave advance warnings.

Fara says the hospital got no warning. Hamas calls Israel's account a false narrative to cover up a war crime. The health ministry calls the attack a flagrant violation of international law.

Why does that matter? Under international law, hospitals have protected status. Israel accuses Hamas of using hospitals for weapons and command rooms, and Hamas denies it.

So who is right? Nobody outside can easily check. Israel does not allow international news organisations, including the BBC, into Gaza, which makes it hard to verify what is happening there.

Key facts

  • Gaza's health ministry says six were killed and about 30 injured at Nasser hospital.
  • Hospital director Dr Ahmed al-Fara says two missiles hit the surgery building and one did not explode.
  • Israel says it targeted a Hamas commander and fighters; Hamas denies this.
  • Israel does not allow international news organisations, including the BBC, into Gaza.

Garment factory holidays jumped from 146 to 204 in two days

Unrest at garment factories has spread to Gazipur, nine factories are shut indefinitely, and owners say more closures could follow.

Count the factories that declared a holiday: 146 on 10 November, 182 on 11 November, 204 on 12 November. That climb is the quickest way to see how fast this unrest is spreading.

The trouble began on 31 October, eased after 5 November, then flared again on 10 November. Until Wednesday it was confined to Ashulia and Savar. Yesterday it reached Gazipur, where 67 factories sent workers home, on top of 137 in Ashulia and the wider Savar area.

Here's the catch. A holiday is temporary, but nine factories have now been shut indefinitely under Section 13(1) of the Bangladesh Labour Act. That clause lets owners suspend operations during labour unrest, and workers are not paid while it lasts. The phrase for it is "no work, no pay". About 20,000 workers are affected, according to BGMEA sources.

BGMEA president Mahmud Hasan Khan Babu told TBS that owners kept reopening, only to find workers unwilling to resume. He said production could restart once things return to normal.

So why are workers staying away? The accounts differ. A police superintendent in Ashulia blamed outside influence and social media messages, and said many workers stay home out of fear or pressure. Bricks and stones were thrown at factories, and a junior quality inspector was hurt in the head.

Another explanation is money. The new minimum wage is not yet final. At the latest Minimum Wage Board meeting, workers' representatives submitted a proposal and the owners' side did not.

Union leader Md Hashmat Ali said some workers believe they should wait to see the owners' proposal before going back. He also said most workers want to work, and that factories declared holidays as a precaution. He alleged people tied to the ousted Awami League were mixing in with workers, which is his claim alone.

Now the clock. An anonymous BGMEA leader said owners could shut far more factories if things do not improve by tomorrow. Owners are also considering closing every factory in Ashulia indefinitely. Watch for that decision, and for the owners' wage proposal.

Key facts

  • Nine factories were shut indefinitely under Section 13(1), affecting around 20,000 workers.
  • Factories declaring holidays rose from 146 (10 Nov) to 182 (11 Nov) to 204 (12 Nov).
  • Under Section 13(1), workers are not paid while a factory is closed.
  • The new minimum wage is not yet finalised; owners' reps did not submit a proposal at the latest meeting.

The Quad has not met at leader level since September 2024

India's prime minister is heading to Manila, where a long-delayed Quad leaders' meeting may finally happen on the sidelines of a bigger summit.

A group of four countries has not had its leaders in one room since September 2024. Next week in Manila, that could change.

The group is the Quad: India, the United States, Japan and Australia. It was revived in 2017, during Donald Trump's first term, to counter China's aggressive behaviour in the Indo-Pacific. Beijing calls it a "small clique".

So why the gap? India was supposed to host the leaders' summit in 2025 and 2026. It did not happen.

Scheduling has been the stated reason. Here's the catch: the source also points to strained ties between India and the US over tariffs, visa curbs, deportations and Washington's close ties with Pakistan's military. Those have kept Trump from visiting India for two years.

Meanwhile, the group has kept ticking along one level down. Quad foreign ministers met three times this year, in Delhi in May, Manila in July and Atlanta in October.

Now the setting. Prime Minister Narendra Modi will visit Manila from November 16 to 17, invited by Philippine President Ferdinand Marcos Jr. The Philippines chairs ASEAN, the Southeast Asian regional bloc, for 2026.

On November 17, Modi will take part in the 23rd ASEAN-India Summit and the 21st East Asia Summit. Trump, Japan's Sanae Takaichi and Australia's Anthony Albanese are expected at the East Asia Summit too.

That is the opening. If all four leaders are in town, a Quad meeting on the sidelines is expected. Think of it like colleagues who all attend the same conference and grab a table together.

Is it happening? Not officially. Asked about it, India's Secretary (East), Rudrendra Tandon, declined to confirm the Quad summit or any side meetings, saying they are being worked out.

One more wrinkle: Trump's engagement with China's President Xi Jinping has raised some concerns about what it means for the Quad.

Nearby sits a smaller galaxy, NGC 3077. The wispy blue patches scattered across the image are galactic cirrus. Here's the twist: they are not far away at all. They are clouds in the foreground, part of our own home galaxy.

Zoom in on Messier 81 and you can pick out individual stars inside its disc. Another galaxy, NGC 1291, is about 33 million light years away, and stars are still forming in it. They show up as small blue dots, with even more distant galaxies behind, the farthest ones glowing red.

Britain's blockade helped topple Iran's leader in 1953. Today looks different

Starving Iran of oil income helped bring down its prime minister in the 1950s, but one scholar argues Iran is better placed to hit back now.

Squeeze a country's economy hard enough and its leader will fall. It sounds like a rule. It did work once, on Iran, and the story of how is a useful warning about copying it.

The leader was Mohammad Mossadegh. In March 1951 he persuaded parliament to nationalize the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company, which Britain controlled. Many Iranians saw it as reclaiming their wealth. Britain saw theft of its largest overseas asset.

So Britain squeezed. It told Iranian tankers their receipts would not be accepted on the international market. In August it added a naval blockade, intercepted tankers, banned exports like sugar and steel to Iran, and froze Iranian accounts in the U.K.

Here's the catch. Britain also pulled out its technicians and closed the Abadan refineries. Mossadegh assumed foreign technicians could replace them, but the British convinced oil companies in the U.S. and Europe, except Italy, to stay away.

The result: between 1950 and 1952, Iran's petroleum production fell about 96% and its oil income dropped to almost zero. Meanwhile British Petroleum and Saudi Aramco doubled their output to cover the gap.

Poverty and a fracturing political coalition weakened Mossadegh, and a British and American propaganda campaign pushed on him. He was overthrown in Operation Ajax on Aug. 19, 1953.

So why bring it up now? On Nov. 8, 2026, President Trump posted that "Very little gets into, or out of, Iran!" and praised "history's greatest Naval Blockade." It followed Task Force Iron Gate, a new plan to isolate Iran's economy, with the U.S. war in Iran now in its ninth month.

The author, an expert on Iran's relations with the world, sees the echoes: oil, economic coercion, a Western power hoping to push Iran's leaders to their knees. But they argue the comparison stops there. Iran has shown it can fight back through asymmetric warfare and economic disruption, which Mossadegh's Iran could not.

One last detail from 1954. The Shah signed a new oil deal with the British, who lifted the blockade. Iran's share of profits rose only slightly, and between 75% and 79% still went to British and American companies.

Learn · Physics from first principles

Why physics still has open problems

A theory can leave a question unanswered. An experiment can be too hard to run. Both keep physics moving.

Flip a switch, and light fills a room. It feels like the sort of thing physics has settled. Yet Isaac Newton published 31 questions about light and matter in 1704. What does it mean for a question in physics to stay open?

Sometimes a theory cannot explain something we observe. Sometimes there is a proposed explanation, but no experiment can yet test it. Those are different problems: one asks for a better idea; the other asks how to check an idea.

Here is a concrete example of how the list can change. In 1984, Barry Simon set out 15 open problems about quantum operators, mathematical tools used in quantum theory. About 5 had been solved by 2000. Subtract 5 from 15, and about 10 were still open.

Some questions reach across whole theories. The Standard Model describes particles; general relativity describes gravity. Their mathematical frameworks do not fit together under extreme conditions, including the Big Bang and the centers of black holes beyond their event horizons.

That is why an open problem is more than a blank space in a textbook. It can show exactly where an explanation stops working, or where a test is still needed. Physicists also have unanswered questions about neutrino mass, dark matter and dark energy.

So the lesson to keep is simple: a successful theory can still have a boundary. When two theories reach that boundary together, what could an experiment tell us about which explanation to trust?

Key facts

  • Open problems can be theoretical or experimental.
  • Newton published 31 questions about light and matter in 1704.
  • About 5 of Simon's 15 problems from 1984 had been solved by 2000.
  • The Standard Model and general relativity break down under extreme conditions.

The slow forces beneath the headlines

Events arrive suddenly. The pressures behind them take longer to see.

Six people were killed when an Israeli strike hit the surgical building of a Gaza hospital. Israel says it targeted Hamas fighters; Hamas denies it. You can see the damage an event does while the account of what happened is still contested. That gap matters because people have to make sense of a shared world through claims they may not trust.

Some pressure shows up more quietly. Garment factory holidays in Bangladesh jumped from 146 to 204 in two days as unrest spread. A closure is a decision made on a particular day. The strain that leads to it builds over time, then becomes visible all at once to workers and owners alike.

Money has the same habit of carrying yesterday's problems into tomorrow. Bangladesh is asking for grants and debt relief at COP31 so it can protect itself from climate harm without taking on more borrowing. The immediate question is how to pay for protection. Underneath it sits a harder one: who carries the cost when a country needs to prepare?

Then there is the question of who gets to set the rules. Nonprofits in California have asked a court to pause new AI models until companies can prove people can control them. That case may move slowly even as the technology changes. News gives us strikes, closures, meetings and lawsuits. To understand a headline, you have to look for the pressure that made this moment possible: who is trusted, who bears the cost, and who gets to decide?

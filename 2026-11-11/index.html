Cover story

The US wants a "final end" to the Ukraine war. Europe wants a seat.

Russia, Ukraine and the US held talks in Abu Dhabi, with a plan for Ukraine's security and a new Russia-Europe arrangement, while Europe worries about being sidelined.

For years, Vladimir Putin has complained about Nato expanding eastward and tried to renegotiate the balance of power between Nato and Russia. The Guardian reports that a sitting US administration has never before embraced those demands. This one has, and it says it is desperate for a deal.

That is the backdrop to Tuesday's talks in Abu Dhabi. Russia, Ukraine and the US sat down together for the first substantive negotiations between Russia and Ukraine since February. Neither Putin nor Ukraine's president, Volodymyr Zelenskyy, was in Abu Dhabi.

US officials say they want a "final end" to the war. Part of their proposal is a new "long-term security architecture" between Russia and Europe, which could include reviving a Nato-Russia council suspended after the 2022 invasion and possibly lifting sanctions on Russia.

Here's the catch. European officials worry Russia will stick to its demand that Nato shrink its military footprint across eastern Europe. That would leave allies open to Moscow's influence.

Then there is Ukraine's own safety. US officials say they discussed with Russia guarantees similar to Nato's article 5, the rule that protects members if one is attacked. "What they would be getting would be effectively very similar to Nato," one official said.

Russia, though, has strongly opposed both Nato admitting Ukraine and western troops on Ukrainian soil. Zelenskyy called the first day "constructive and fairly substantive".

So where is Europe in all this? European officials met separately with Ukraine and the US and said any deal must respect Ukraine's sovereignty and territorial integrity. They are not convinced Russia will negotiate in good faith.

Italy's prime minister, Giorgia Meloni, has complained that the "disorderly" mix of meetings and calls weakens Europe's ability to speak with one voice. US secretary of state Marco Rubio said: "Europe's at the table. They're with us." He also said the goal is eventually to widen these talks to include European voices.

The talks continue on Wednesday. Watch for two things: whether Russia softens on Nato's footprint, and whether Europe moves from the next room to the table.

Key facts

  • Abu Dhabi talks are the first substantive Russia-Ukraine negotiations since February.
  • The US proposal includes a new long-term security architecture between Russia and Europe.
  • US officials say they discussed Ukraine guarantees similar to Nato's article 5.
  • Rubio said the goal is to eventually widen the talks to include European voices.

A 30-minute meeting in Antalya covered defence, trade and the Rohingya

Bangladesh's prime minister met Turkey's president for about 30 minutes before a climate summit and raised defence, trade, energy and the Rohingya.

The meeting lasted around 30 minutes. In that time, Prime Minister Tarique Rahman and Turkish President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan covered defence, trade, energy, climate and the Rohingya crisis.

That works out to a lot of topics for a short slot. So what did Tarique actually ask for?

Start with defence. He said the two countries should deepen and diversify their cooperation, including through joint investment. He framed it as part of efforts to strengthen the long-standing partnership.

On trade, he called for closer discussions to explore ways to expand commerce and attract more investment. On energy, he pointed to openings for Turkish investment in Bangladesh's renewable sector. The two leaders also discussed minerals.

Here's the setting. They met at the Maxx Royal Hotel in Antalya, ahead of the world leaders' summit of COP31, the 31st UN climate conference. The summit was scheduled for 11 and 12 November, and Tarique arrived for a three-day visit.

Climate came up too. He said international cooperation matters for vulnerable countries like Bangladesh, which face the adverse effects of climate change. He also congratulated Türkiye on hosting COP31.

Now the Rohingya. Tarique thanked Türkiye for its solidarity at the International Court of Justice and for humanitarian help on the ground.

Then he asked for more. He said Türkiye's role at the United Nations and the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation was key to a safe, dignified and sustainable return of the Rohingya to their homeland. He called on Türkiye to keep backing them at international forums.

The meeting began at 7:15pm local time. Foreign Minister Khalilur Rahman and State Minister-1 for Foreign Affairs Humaiun Kobir were also there. The readout came from the PM's Deputy Press Secretary, Hasan Shiplu, so we have Bangladesh's account of the talks, not a Turkish one.

Sonia Gandhi says she has had "practically no real contact" with Modi in 12 years

In her new memoir, Sonia Gandhi uses her own grief and a few small gestures to show how India's politics turned colder.

Sonia Gandhi says she has had "practically no real contact" with Narendra Modi in the 12 years he has led India. Even when they are in the same room, she writes, he "tends to look away, avoiding eye contact".

The line is in Belonging, her political memoir, published on 10 November. Gandhi is a veteran leader of the Congress party. She came to India in 1968 from a small town in Italy, all for love, she says.

That love was Rajiv Gandhi, a former prime minister. The book covers his assassination in 1991 and that of her mother-in-law, Indira Gandhi, in 1984. It also covers the loss of her first child, who died shortly after birth.

Then there are the grenades. After Operation Blue Star, the 1984 military operation against Sikh separatists at the Golden Temple in Amritsar, she writes of a chilling discovery: grenades in a room of their house.

So why does a missed glance matter? Because she says it was not always like this. Leaders on opposing sides used to "treat one another with cordiality".

Her example is small and human. After the 2001 terror attack on parliament, she immediately called the then prime minister, AB Vajpayee of the BJP, to ask if he was safe. She says he appreciated it.

Now she sees a style of politics where those in power seek to "belittle and torment" opponents. For years, the BJP has mocked her son Rahul as "Pappu", a derisive term meant to paint him as inexperienced and naive.

The memoir also returns to the Emergency, which Indira imposed in June 1975. For 21 months, civil liberties were suspended, opponents were jailed and the press was gagged. In Gandhi's account, it caused a rift within the family.

Zoom out and the book spans six decades. She arrived in a Congress-dominated secular republic. Today, Hindu nationalism is the dominant political force under Modi.

Hold two things side by side: one phone call in 2001 to ask a rival if he was safe, and 12 years of almost no contact at all.

Key facts

  • Belonging, Sonia Gandhi's political memoir, was published on 10 November.
  • She writes she has had "practically no real contact" with Modi in his 12 years leading India.
  • After the 2001 parliament attack she called then prime minister Vajpayee to check on his safety.
  • The book covers the 1984 and 1991 assassinations and the 1975 Emergency.

Anthropic asks Wall Street to value it at $1.4 trillion

Anthropic, the maker of Claude, plans to raise up to $64.4 billion in a share sale that would value it at about $1.4 trillion, before it has turned a profit.

How much would you pay for a company that hasn't turned a profit yet? Anthropic is about to put that question to Wall Street, and it's suggesting roughly $1.4 trillion.

Anthropic was founded in 2021, is led by Dario Amodei and makes the Claude chatbot. On November 10 it filed updated paperwork with US regulators for its IPO, which is a company's first sale of shares to the public.

Here's the plan. Anthropic and some existing shareholders would sell 825 million shares at $70 to $78 each, raising up to $64.4 billion. A separate $26 billion private sale to investors including Google, Nvidia and Advanced Micro Devices could bring the total to $90.4 billion.

Wait, so is it worth $1.4 trillion or not? That depends on how you count. The shares that exist today would put it at about $1 trillion. Add the shares that could still be created from stock options and restricted stock, and you get $1.4 trillion, which is called the "fully diluted" figure.

So why would anyone pay that much? Growth. Anthropic's annualized run-rate revenue, meaning what it would earn in a year if its latest pace held, reached roughly $81 billion by October 31. That was $10 billion more than a month earlier.

Here's the catch. At $1.4 trillion, investors would be paying about $17 for every $1 of that yearly revenue, for a company that still hadn't made a profit as of mid-2026. It also has nearly $900 billion in computing commitments with partners including Amazon and Google. That's about 11 years of revenue at today's pace.

Varun Datta, founder of Truth Ventures, summed up the tension: "Anthropic could become an exceptionally successful company, but at this valuation, much of that success is already reflected in the price."

The prospectus lists its own risks too. Rivals could build AI models faster, and advances in AI could outpace Anthropic's safety and security measures.

There was also a small tell. Terms that briefly went out to banks late on November 9, and were then pulled back, pointed to a raise of $69.3 billion. The official filing the next day aimed lower.

Watch November 19. That's when shares are expected to start trading on the Nasdaq under the symbol ANT. The final price depends on how much investors want the shares, and that will show whether $1.4 trillion is Wall Street's number or only Anthropic's.

Key facts

  • Anthropic and some shareholders plan to sell 825 million shares at $70 to $78 each, raising up to $64.4 billion.
  • The price would value Anthropic at about $1.4 trillion fully diluted, or about $1 trillion counting only existing shares.
  • Annualized run-rate revenue was roughly $81 billion as of October 31, against nearly $900 billion in computing commitments.
  • Shares are expected to start trading on the Nasdaq on November 19 under the symbol ANT.

A Fed official named their inflation number. October hit it exactly

October US inflation came in hotter than expected, pushing bond yields up and stocks down.

Christopher Waller, a governor at the US Federal Reserve, set a tripwire before the data came out. If October's core inflation reading was 0.3%, they said, they would consider raising interest rates by half a percentage point at the Fed's December 8-9 meeting.

Tuesday's report arrived. Core inflation was 0.3%.

First, some quick background. The Fed is America's central bank. When prices rise too fast, it raises its key interest rate. Borrowing gets more expensive, people and businesses spend less, and prices cool down.

The Fed usually moves in steps of a quarter of a percentage point, so a half point would be double that. Waller said a bigger step might be needed so the Fed doesn't put its credibility at risk.

So what is "core"? It's inflation with food and energy taken out, because those prices can jump around wildly from month to month. Economists had expected core prices to rise 0.2% from September to October. They rose 0.3%. Prices across the board rose 0.4%, when forecasters had expected 0.3%.

A miss of a tenth of a point sounds tiny. Here's why it mattered anyway. This was the last consumer price report Fed officials were due to see before their December meeting.

Investors reacted. The two-year Treasury yield usually moves with what traders expect the Fed to do, and it jumped 18.44 basis points to 4.731%. A basis point is a hundredth of a percentage point, so that one-day move was about three-quarters the size of a normal Fed hike.

The 10-year yield, which helps set rates on mortgages and other loans, climbed to 4.988%. That's just under 5%. Stocks slipped: the S&P 500 fell 0.4% and the tech-heavy Nasdaq fell 0.7%. Nvidia dropped 5.5% and pulled the S&P down more than any other stock.

Waller isn't the only one leaning this way. Lorie Logan, president of the Dallas Fed, backed at least two more quarter-point increases and said they might need to come faster than recent ones.

The Fed raised rates by a quarter point in September and held them steady in October. The next meeting is December 8-9. Waller's number has arrived. A half-point hike would show they meant what they said. Another quarter point would mean the tripwire only counted as a warning.

Key facts

  • Core CPI rose 0.3% in October, above the 0.2% forecast; headline CPI rose 0.4% vs 0.3% expected.
  • Fed Governor Waller had said a 0.3% core reading would lead him to consider a half-point hike in December.
  • The two-year Treasury yield rose 18.44 basis points to 4.731%; the S&P 500 fell 0.4%.
  • This was the last CPI report before the Fed's December 8-9 meeting.

Two giant black holes appeared far too early for theory to explain

Astronomers found a close pair of feeding black holes, 900 million years after the Big Bang, that no theory can yet explain.

Astronomers have found two giant black holes that current theory cannot explain. Both are feeding fiercely, and both date to just 900 million years after the Big Bang.

The pair is called J1602+4228AB, with a Hawaiian nickname, Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua, from a Hawaiian naming program. A feeding black hole like this is called a quasar. Think of it as a black hole with its lights on: the energy from what it swallows outshines every star in its home galaxy.

So why is a pair such a big deal? Galaxies collide often, so astronomers expect quasar pairs to be common. Finding them is hard. The bright phase is short, the objects are far away and faint, and up close the two blur into one.

This pair is only the third known from cosmic dawn, the early era of the universe. The two are about 18,100 light-years apart, roughly one-fifth the diameter of the Milky Way. Their masses are 1.5 billion and 400 million times the sun.

Here's the catch. At that age, the first stars, galaxies and black holes were still young. Silvia Onorato of NSF NOIRLab, who led the study, says: "There is no definitive theoretical explanation for how a single one of these giant quasars exists so early in the age of our universe, much less two."

How did anyone spot them? A James Webb Space Telescope program called Aether collected 200 quasars, 43% of all known at this stage of cosmic history. During visual inspection of the data, the team saw that quasar J1602+4228A had a companion.

Splitting the two from the ground should have been nearly impossible, because B is so faint and so close to A. But the weather over Maunakea was perfect, and Onorato says the team got its Gemini North data in only two nights.

The team proposes the pair may mark an extreme peak in the matter density of the early universe. The full origin story is still a mystery.

Onorato adds another detail. The universe was only a thirteenth of its present age, and these black holes are already as massive as those we see today.

Key facts

  • J1602+4228AB, nicknamed Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua, is the most distant compact quasar pair found, 900 million years after the Big Bang.
  • The two are about 18,100 light-years apart, with masses of 1.5 billion and 400 million suns.
  • Onorato says there is no definitive theoretical explanation for even one such quasar this early.
  • It was found in JWST's Aether survey and resolved with Gemini North.

Infineon forecasts more than 20% growth on data-centre power demand

German chipmaker Infineon expects revenue to rise more than 20% to about €20 billion in fiscal 2027, helped by chips that power data centres.

What does a data centre actually run on? The obvious answer is computer chips. The less obvious answer is electricity, and someone has to make the chips that handle it.

One of those someones is Infineon. On November 10 the German chipmaker said it expects revenue to grow more than 20% in fiscal 2027, to about €20 billion, up from €16.353 billion in the year just ended.

Here's the eye-catching part. Infineon now expects revenue from data-centre power supplies to reach close to €4 billion in the year through September 2027. Its own earlier guidance for that same year was roughly €2.5 billion.

So in one update, the company added about €1.5 billion to its forecast for a single business. On the new numbers, that's roughly a fifth of everything Infineon expects to sell.

Wait, what's a power chip? Power semiconductors switch and convert electricity so machines get the current they need. Think of the charger brick on your phone cable: it turns wall power into something your phone can safely take. A data centre needs that conversion over and over.

Why does this matter so much to Infineon? In recent years, weak car chip demand weighed on it, as carmakers worked through stockpiles built up during the pandemic. Chief executive Jochen Hanebeck said the company enters the new year with "continued strong momentum", citing a cyclical recovery and demand in structural growth markets.

The latest numbers back him up. Fourth-quarter revenue rose 19% from a year earlier, and net profit more than doubled, to €532 million from €231 million. Shares rose more than 4% in Frankfurt.

Data centres are not the only engine. Hanebeck also pointed to energy infrastructure, electric cars and software-defined vehicles, meaning cars whose features are run and updated through software.

Here's the catch. Chips need factories, and factories cost a lot. Infineon opened a €5 billion plant in Dresden in July 2026. Yet at an industry event in Munich, Hanebeck said the company needs customer commitments to justify expanding production capacity further.

Which leaves the open question. The forecast says the demand is coming. Whether customers will commit firmly enough for Infineon to build more, nobody has said yet.

World War I ended on a signature that was not a surrender

The armistice of 11 November 1918 stopped the fighting, but it was a pause with terms, not a formal surrender.

World War I did not end with a surrender. Not formally, anyway.

What ended it was an armistice, which is an agreement to stop fighting while the peace gets worked out later. It was agreed at 5:00 a.m. in a railroad car in the Compiègne Forest, and it came into force at 11:00 a.m. on 11 November 1918.

So why did Germany sign? Its allies had already gone. Bulgaria, the Ottoman Empire and Austria-Hungary had each agreed their own armistices, and at home, food shortages caused by the Allied blockade were feeding discontent and disorder.

On 29 September 1918, German army command told Emperor Wilhelm II the situation was hopeless. Quartermaster General Erich Ludendorff said he could not guarantee the front would hold for another two hours. He demanded a request for an immediate ceasefire.

Here's the catch. When Germany wrote to US president Woodrow Wilson, he set conditions: retreat from occupied territories, an end to submarine activity, and the Kaiser's abdication.

Then came the strangest turn. In late October, Ludendorff saw that the Allied terms were much harsher than he had expected, and he demanded the war resume. That was the same war he had declared lost a month earlier.

The terms were largely written by the Allied Supreme Commander, French Marshal Ferdinand Foch. German forces had to pull back west of the Rhine, and hand over aircraft and warships. Allied prisoners were released, but German prisoners were not, and the naval blockade stayed.

That is why it was only a pause. The armistice was extended three times while a peace treaty was negotiated. The Treaty of Versailles was signed on 28 June 1919 and took effect on 10 January 1920.

One last fact. The fighting did not stop when the paper was signed at 5:00 a.m. It went on until 11 a.m., and 2,738 men died on that last day. Spread over 24 hours, that is about one every half minute.

Key facts

  • The armistice took effect at 11:00 a.m. on 11 November 1918 and was not formally a surrender.
  • Terms were largely written by Marshal Foch; the naval blockade of Germany stayed in place.
  • The Treaty of Versailles was signed 28 June 1919 and took effect 10 January 1920.
  • 2,738 men died on the last day of the war.

Learn · Physics from first principles

Open problems: where physics's map runs out

Lesson 15 of 15. Every physics theory works until it hits an edge, and the edges are where the next theory comes from.

In 1900, Lord Kelvin gave a speech and named two problems in classical physics. He called them "two clouds" hanging over the physics of the 20th century. One was the lack of evidence for the luminiferous aether, a invisible substance light was once thought to travel through. The other was that a rule called the equipartition theorem failed to predict the heat capacity of solids, meaning how much heat a material soaks up as it warms.

Here's the everyday version of what he was doing. Think of a map of your city. It is brilliant for getting to work, and then you drive to the edge of it and the paper just stops, or worse, shows a road that is not there. A physics theory is a map of how the world behaves. An open problem is a spot where the map gives the wrong answer, or none.

Now the worked example, using Kelvin's own numbers. He named 2 clouds in 1900. Both were solved within a decade. The aether problem was solved by special relativity and the heat problem by quantum mechanics. So two small gaps in the old map ended up producing two new theories.

That's the clever bit: gaps are not embarrassing leftovers. They are where progress comes from. People have been writing them down for a long time. Isaac Newton published a list of 31 queries about light and matter in his book Opticks, first edition 1704. Barry Simon proposed 15 problems on quantum operators in 1984, and about 5 were solved by 2000, which leaves about 10. His second list of 15, released in 2000, had at least 3 solved. Some gaps close. Others stay stubborn: a list of 10 problems from the Strings 2000 conference had none conclusively solved as of 2025.

So what does the map look like at its edges today? The biggest one is that the mathematics of the Standard Model, our best description of particles, is inconsistent with general relativity, our best description of gravity. Both break down in extreme places: at the Big Bang, and at the centres of black holes beyond their event horizons. There is also a list of open questions beyond the Standard Model: the absolute mass of neutrinos, why there is more matter than antimatter, and what dark matter and dark energy are. Some of these are theoretical, where our theories cannot explain what we see. Others are experimental, where nobody yet knows how to build the test.

Vitaly Ginzburg, a Nobel laureate, compiled a list of 30 open problems from 1971 to 2005. He also picked out three "great problems": the arrow of time, the interpretations of quantum mechanics, and whether life can arise from physics alone. There is no lesson 16 to answer them. That question is left open, as the lesson says it should be: which of these gaps is the next of Kelvin's clouds?

Key facts

  • In 1900 Lord Kelvin named two "clouds" over physics; both were solved within a decade by special relativity and quantum mechanics.
  • The Standard Model is inconsistent with general relativity, and both break down at the Big Bang and inside black holes.
  • Barry Simon's 1984 list of 15 problems had about 5 solved by 2000.
  • A list of 10 problems from Strings 2000 had none conclusively solved as of 2025.

The pressure beneath the headlines

The day's events are sudden. The forces behind them have been building for longer.

Australia leads climate talks while exporting fossil fuels. There is a puzzle in one sentence. We ask institutions to solve problems while they remain tied to ways of working that make those problems harder to solve. A promise meets its real test after it is made, when someone has to change what they do. That is where trust is won or lost.

Then there is the question of who gets to decide. Europe wants a seat in the Ukraine talks. An agreement can be announced in a day, while the rules it creates last much longer. You can see why the question of who was in the room matters, even before you know what the agreement says. Being heard is part of whether people trust an outcome.

And what happens when something already strained takes another hit? Gaza's floods killed at least four as thousands lost shelters. The storm fits neatly into a headline. The crowded tent camps were there before it arrived. News gives us a moment we can point to; the conditions that make that moment so damaging are harder to fit in a line.

These patterns keep crossing: promises pressed against practice, decisions that depend on who has a voice, and fragile conditions exposed by a sudden event. A morning paper catches the visible part. The harder story is how long a pressure has been building, and who had the power to ease it along the way. When you read a headline, what had to be true before that event could happen?

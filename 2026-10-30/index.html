Cover story

A stopped train was hit from behind in Spain. Why were trains moving?

A Renfe train hit the back of a stationary Iryo train in Spain, killing 69, and the transport minister says the control centre restarted traffic after a safety system failed.

The train that was hit was not moving. It was standing still when a Renfe train ploughed into its back on Wednesday morning in Castilla-La Mancha, in central Spain. The death toll, first reported as 68, rose to 69 on Thursday.

So how does one train reach another that is stopped? Transport Minister Oscar Puente laid out his version. An automatic control system went down on 69 kilometres of track, about 43 miles, and that caused all trains there to be stopped.

Think of a traffic light failing at a busy junction. Everyone is meant to wait until someone has sorted it out.

Here's the catch. Puente said the control centre decided to resume traffic "in circumstances that, from our point of view, are inexplicable." The Renfe train then travelled 25 kilometres, about 15 miles, before it struck the Iryo train.

He added that the control centre "had more than enough time to verify the error, and they did not fix it." That is the minister's account. Prime Minister Pedro Sanchez, at the crash site, pledged to establish every last detail of what happened.

Renfe is the state operator. Iryo is a private Spanish company. The crash happened on the key line linking Madrid with the southern city of Seville.

Survivors described the moment. Josimar Medina, 31, who was asleep on the Renfe train, said "I woke up and I was in the air." Pablo Mejia, 42, escaped with head injuries and says a last-minute change of seat saved his life.

Spain began three days of mourning on Thursday. Crews used sniffer dogs and thermal drones to search the site, and a school in the village of Urda became a place where relatives could ask about their loved ones. A Red Cross psychologist, Carmen Garcia, said of the grieving: "The grieving will be complicated."

This is the second high-speed collision in Spain this year. In January, two trains collided in Andalusia and 46 people died. That investigation is still going, and the government faced heavy criticism over its management of the railways.

Watch two dates. Sanchez faces a general election on November 29, and both crash investigations are still to give their answers. Train drivers also struck on Thursday to defend rail safety, a strike called before this crash happened.

Key facts

  • A Renfe train struck the back of a stationary Iryo train in Castilla-La Mancha; 69 people died.
  • Puente says an automatic control system failed on 69 km of track and the control centre restarted traffic anyway.
  • The Renfe train travelled 25 km before the collision.
  • In January, a separate high-speed collision in Andalusia killed 46.

227,917. That is how many votes Mirza Faisal Amin of BNP collected in the Thakurgaon-1 by-election on 29 October. The runner-up got 96,513, so the winner pulled in more than double.

A by-election is a vote held in a single constituency to fill one seat in parliament. Nothing else is on the ballot, so all the attention lands on one place.

Faisal Amin is the younger brother of President Mirza Fakhrul Islam Alamgir. His nearest rival, Delawar Hosen, stood for the Jamaat-led 11-party alliance. Three others trailed far behind: Khademul Islam with 3,289 votes, Abdul Alim Nizami with 836, and Muhammad Omar Farukh with 450.

Turnout was 64.81%, roughly two voters in three. Polling began at 8am at 185 centres across Thakurgaon Sadar, Ruhia and Bhulli upazilas. Around 5,300 law enforcement and administration personnel were deployed.

Here's the catch. The winner is clear, but the day is described in two very different ways.

Election Commission Senior Secretary Akhtar Ahmed said, "We have completed a good election in a peaceful environment with everyone's cooperation." No unexpected incidents were reported through the day.

Jamaat-e-Islami rejected that. Its Secretary General, Mia Golam Porwar, told a press briefing in Moghbazar that there were widespread irregularities, intimidation and partisan conduct. He alleged that the stubs of some ballot books had been stamped and signed in advance for vote rigging.

There was also a development before polling. Nine polling officials were pulled from duty after allegations that they directly or indirectly supported the ruling party, and they were replaced, the returning officer told reporters on Wednesday night.

So there is one count and two stories. One side points to a peaceful day and a wide margin, the other to ballot books it says were marked early. The source does not say how, or whether, those claims will be checked.

Ambani and Adani news outlets take India's privacy law to court

India's big publishers, including outlets owned by Mukesh Ambani and Gautam Adani, say a data law with no journalism exemption could expose sources and force factual reports to be pulled.

Madhavi Divan told India's Supreme Court on October 27 that a new privacy law could create "pre-censorship" and shut newsrooms down. Her clients are not who you'd expect in a free-press fight.

She was arguing for the Digital News Publishers Association, a group of 21 publishers. Among them are Network18, owned by Mukesh Ambani, and NDTV, owned by Gautam Adani.

So why would some of India's biggest publishers sue over the government's law? The answer comes down to one exemption the law doesn't have.

The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, passed in 2023, sets rules for any organisation that handles people's personal data. It makes exceptions for research, archiving and statistical work. Journalism gets no exception.

Here's why that matters. The publishers argue the law would make a reporter tell the subject of an investigation and get their consent. Think of a detective who has to ask the suspect for permission before looking into them. The filing says the subject could use that warning to destroy evidence or intimidate witnesses.

That's only the first problem they raise. People can withdraw consent and ask for their data to be erased, which the publishers say could force them to pull or heavily redact factual reports. A clause that lets the government demand information from data handlers could, they argue, expose confidential sources.

Here's the odd bit. According to the publishers' filing, government officials told media companies in an August meeting that the law would protect press freedom.

The government says it still means that. A day after the hearing, Information Technology Minister Ashwini Vaishnaw said it had no intention of affecting journalistic freedom, invited editors to send suggestions, and said it was willing to act if those talks showed a need.

The judges are holding back for now. Chief Justice Surya Kant said the court must balance the rights of innocent citizens with those of the media. His three-judge bench sent notice to the government but did not put anything on hold before hearing the government's side.

Next comes November 17, when the case returns to court. Most of the contested rules start in May 2027. That leaves about six months before they take effect.

Key facts

  • 21 publishers, including Ambani's Network18 and Adani's NDTV, challenged the data law in India's Supreme Court on October 6, 2026.
  • The law exempts research, archiving and statistics but not journalism.
  • The court issued notice to the government without granting a stay; the next hearing is November 17, 2026.
  • The contested provisions mostly take effect in May 2027.

Intel's budget laptop chip is sold out, and the memory shortage is why

Intel says its budget Wildcat Lake chips are sold out as a memory shortage pushes laptop prices up, and it expects no relief in 2027.

Intel has a chip it cannot supply fast enough. In the same earnings call, the company admitted the PC market is slowing down.

The chip is called Wildcat Lake, and it is aimed at budget laptops. CEO Lip-Bu Tan said demand for it "is outstripping supply." CFO David Zinsner went further and said the chips are now sold out.

So how does a slowing market produce a sellout? Here's the catch: shortages of RAM and other components have pushed up PC prices over recent months. RAM is the short-term memory a laptop uses to keep programs running. When everything costs more, a cheaper machine looks better.

Zinsner put it this way: Wildcat Lake is "coming exactly at the right time because it's cost-optimized." You can already find it in new lower-cost Windows laptops like the Dell XPS 13 and the HP Omnibook 5 14.

There's a trade-off. Those laptops start at friendly prices, but often with just 8GB of RAM, which Windows laptops can struggle to get by on.

And the memory problem is not going away soon. Asked about supply, Zinsner said, "Make no mistake, we are not optimistic about next year in terms of memory."

His expectation is that things do not improve in 2027, and that 2028 is when Intel will find out. That is actually the sunnier view. SK Hynix has predicted the RAM shortage will drag on through 2030.

Tan blamed the squeeze on demand for AI, which he called "super intelligence." He described one of the most significant supply shortages in the industry's history.

Zinsner still struck a hopeful note: "We do think the PC is going to go through a renaissance despite this."

Whether that renaissance arrives before the memory does is an open question.

Key facts

  • Intel's CFO David Zinsner said the budget Wildcat Lake chips are sold out.
  • RAM and component shortages have pushed up PC prices, making cheaper laptops attractive.
  • Intel expects memory supply not to improve in 2027; SK Hynix predicts the shortage lasts through 2030.

Learn · Physics from first principles

Open problems: how physics keeps its list of what it doesn't know

The last lesson is about the questions still standing, and why physicists write them down.

Ever notice that answering one question tends to hand you three new ones? Physics works the same way. After fourteen lessons on what we know, this one is about what we don't.

Physicists sort their unsolved problems into two piles. Theoretical ones are cases where our best theories cannot explain something we have actually observed. Experimental ones are cases where an idea exists but nobody has worked out how to build a test for it, or how to look closely enough.

Here's the big one. Our two best descriptions of nature, the Standard Model (the rulebook for particles) and general relativity (the account of gravity), do not fit together mathematically. Both break down in extreme places, like the Big Bang and the centers of black holes. Around that sit more puzzles: what dark matter and dark energy are, why there is more matter than antimatter, and what neutrinos actually weigh.

Now the worked example, and it comes from 1900. Lord Kelvin gave a speech naming two "clouds" over classical physics. One was the lack of evidence for the luminiferous aether. The other was that the equipartition theorem failed to predict the heat capacity of solids.

Here's the twist. Both clouds were cleared within a decade, by special relativity and by quantum mechanics. Two small-looking worries became two revolutions. That's the clever bit: the unsolved problems were where the next physics was hiding.

So how fast do problems fall? Take Barry Simon's list of 15 problems from 1984. By 2000 about 5 were solved, which is one in three over 16 years. He then released a second list of 15, and at least 3 were solved. Then there is the list of 10 from the Strings 2000 conference: as of 2025, none has been conclusively solved.

Writing these lists is an old habit. Isaac Newton published 31 queries about light and matter in 1704, and Vitaly Ginzburg kept a list from 1971 to 2005 that ended at 30 problems. He added three great ones: the arrow of time, the interpretations of quantum mechanics, and life arising from physics alone. There is no lesson 16 to answer them, so here is the question to take away: which small cloud today is the next one that turns out to be a revolution?

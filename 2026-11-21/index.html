An Anthropic mathematician says he and Claude proved the Riemann hypothesis

Levent Alpöge says he and Claude found a proof of the Riemann hypothesis, but other mathematicians say it needs rigorous checking first.

The document that might settle what many mathematicians consider the most important open problem in the field is four pages long. Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, wrote: "I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis."

Claude is Anthropic's large language model, the kind of AI that writes text by predicting what comes next. So an AI company says its AI helped crack a problem from 1859.

First, what is the problem? Prime numbers are the ones that only divide by 1 and themselves. They look scattered with no pattern.

Bernhard Riemann, a German mathematician, found that their spread is encoded in the zeroes of the Riemann zeta function. Those are the points where the function equals zero.

Here's the catch he left behind. Riemann conjectured that, apart from some obvious but uninteresting cases, all those zeroes sit on one vertical line. If true, primes are scattered about as randomly as they could be.

Does the proof hold? Nobody knows yet. James Maynard, a Fields Medal winner at Oxford, says: "If this fully checks out, it is clearly a historic milestone in mathematics." Note the "if".

Andrew Granville, a number theorist at the University of Montreal, is blunter. He says Alpöge's document is "garbled" and should have been written up more clearly before the announcement.

Then there is the timing. The claim came just days after Anthropic's stock market debut, which initially valued the company at US$1.5 trillion. The valuation dropped on 19 November, and Granville points to that downturn as the reason for the timing.

Alpöge addressed that in his document. He wrote that there is "an obvious cynical view, to do with the timing, but it is false." Anthropic did not respond immediately to Nature's request for comment.

So the next step is for mathematicians to check the claim rigorously. Until someone does, this is a claim, not a proof.

Key facts

  • Levent Alpöge of Anthropic says he and Claude found a proof of the Riemann hypothesis.
  • His document is 4 pages and includes a sketch of the proof.
  • Andrew Granville calls the write-up garbled and links its timing to Anthropic's falling valuation; Alpöge says that cynical view is false.
  • James Maynard says it would be historic if it fully checks out.

Pew projects Muslims will become the largest religious group, driven by age

A new Pew forecast says Islam grows fastest mainly because Muslims are younger and have more children, not because of conversion.

In 2020, the median Muslim in the world was 24 years old. The median non-Muslim was 33. Nine years doesn't sound like much, but it sits behind one of the loudest numbers in a new Pew Research Center report.

That number: Pew projects Muslims will become the world's largest religious group by 2035. By 2070 it expects more than 3.6 billion Muslims, against just under three billion Christians.

So how does a group grow 80% in five decades? Pew says mostly through births, rather than conversion. Between 2020 and 2025, Muslim women had an estimated 3.1 children each, compared with 2.0 among non-Muslim women.

Here's the catch, and it's the clever bit. A younger group has more people just reaching the age of having children. Think of a school where the biggest class is about to graduate into parenthood: the total keeps climbing for a while even if each person has fewer kids, which Pew expects to happen.

Put the shares side by side. Muslims go from 26% of the world to 36%, while Christians hold steady at 29%. The whole world grows 29% in that time, so Islam grows at almost three times the global rate.

The map shifts too. Indonesia has the most Muslims today, but by 2070 Pakistan is expected to, followed by India, Nigeria and Indonesia. On the Christian side, the Democratic Republic of the Congo is projected to overtake the United States for the largest Christian population.

Britain gets its own forecast. Muslims rise from 6.4% of the population in 2020 to 16% by 2070, roughly one in six people. In the same period, people with no religion go from 40.2% to 52.3%, which would make Britain a majority non-religious country. Muslims are not projected to become a majority in Britain or Europe.

Pew is careful to say these are projections, built on the assumption that recent patterns broadly continue. It also admits uncertainty, because between 2010 and 2020 the number of people leaving organised religion grew faster than expected in some countries.

One last detail shifts the picture. Globally, the share of people with no religion is projected to fall from 24% to 17%, partly because China, home to one of the largest unaffiliated populations, is expected to shrink substantially. Much of this forecast is really a story about who is being born, and where.

Key facts

  • Pew projects Muslims become the world's largest religious group by 2035.
  • By 2070: over 3.6 billion Muslims vs just under 3 billion Christians.
  • Median age in 2020: Muslims 24, non-Muslims 33.
  • Britain: Muslims projected at 16% by 2070, up from 6.4% in 2020.

Port Arthur, 1894: a massacre the world heard about through one reporter

After Japan captured the Chinese port of Port Arthur on November 21, 1894, its troops killed soldiers and civilians, but the toll is still uncertain.

James Creelman was a newspaper correspondent travelling with the Japanese army in November 1894. The newspaper accounts of what that army did at Port Arthur leaned heavily on what he reported.

First, some background. Japan and China's Qing dynasty went to war in 1894 after both stepped in following a peasant uprising in Korea. Port Arthur, now called Lüshun, is a port on the coast of northeastern China.

Creelman reported that the advance on the port began on November 17, with about 23,000 troops and 78 guns. On the last day, he wrote, the assault restarted at 6:45 a.m. and one fort fell at 8:05. Japanese troops took the town on November 21.

Then his account turns. Creelman said soldiers killed people in their homes and shot women and children running toward the hills. He wrote that the troops made no attempt to take prisoners.

His numbers are the hardest part. He estimated that no more than 100 Chinese died in battle, and at least 2,000 unarmed men were killed. By his count, that's at least 20 unarmed people killed for every one who fell fighting.

So why did it happen? Creelman gave one detail: as they advanced, Japanese troops saw the mutilated bodies of fellow soldiers. That's his account, not an established explanation. The sources don't say who ordered the killings or whether anyone was punished.

Japan pushed back. On December 13, its legation in Washington expressed strong disbelief in the massacre reports. Yet it also said it had heard that some Japanese soldiers committed "some excesses" during the battle.

A report that same day, relaying a dispatch to the New York World, said the killing went on for three days and the town was plundered. Creelman's own story ran on that paper's front page, dated December 20.

The war ended in a Japanese victory in 1895. The Treaty of Shimonoseki forced China out of Korea and handed Taiwan to Japan.

So how many people died? The sources don't settle it. Creelman's figure was one witness's estimate, and a 1994 report cited a claim of more than 20,000 Chinese killed.

That same 1994 report described researchers examining the Lüshun Wanzhong Cemetery. They found human remains, bullets, and objects associated with women and children, a century after one reporter wrote that women and children were shot as they ran.

How one new router helped halt every US departure for hours

A nationwide US ground stop was linked to a new router that flooded the air traffic network, and nobody yet knows why it did that.

Josh Tyrangiel, who writes for The Atlantic, keeps hearing a warning from cybersecurity people. Then every departing flight in America was stopped. He wrote about it that night.

The warning goes like this. AI keeps getting stronger, it is spreading, and the world's software is rickety. The specialists he interviewed expect more everyday things to break.

Then came Friday, November 13. The FAA, which runs US air traffic control, stopped departures nationwide after communications and radar problems. The stop lasted more than four hours, the longest nationwide ground stop since the September 11 attacks.

Here's what's easy to miss. Stopping departures doesn't empty the sky. About 3,500 planes were already flying, and controllers still had to guide every one of them. The next day, more than 6,000 flights were canceled.

So was it AI? Tyrangiel wrote that night that he didn't know the cause. He pointed out that the FAA was rolling out an $875 million AI-enhanced system. Its maker, Air Space Intelligence, denied its technology was responsible.

The real culprit was much smaller. A contractor, L3Harris, was replacing old copper wire with fiber optic. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said a newly installed router in El Dorado, Arkansas, was linked to patchy communications and radar at 17 of the FAA's 22 regional centers. A radar site in Amarillo, Texas, also played a part.

These aren't the Wi-Fi box in your living room. They connect the network's old analog parts to its newer digital ones. This one flooded the network with 1,000 connection attempts every four to five seconds.

Think of a group chat where one member posts 200 to 250 messages every second. Nobody else's messages get through. The FAA paused installing those Juniper routers and went back to older Cisco ones.

Officials found no evidence of a cyberattack. That's the unsettling part. No evidence of a hacker, no established AI link, just one box in Arkansas, and the country's flights stopped.

Tyrangiel's specialists aren't predicting an apocalypse. They expect service disruptions to keep coming over the next 18 months. One reason is that defenders may simply switch off systems they can't patch fast enough.

One question is still open. As of November 18, Duffy said investigators still didn't know why the router sent so many connection attempts, whether it was faulty or misconfigured. Routers like it were used in 68 earlier FAA projects, and nobody can yet say why this one behaved differently.

Key facts

  • The FAA's nationwide ground stop on November 13 lasted more than four hours, the longest since 9/11.
  • A newly installed router in El Dorado, Arkansas, was linked to problems at 17 of 22 regional centers.
  • The router flooded the network with 1,000 connection attempts every four to five seconds.
  • Officials found no evidence of a cyberattack, and the router's cause was still unknown as of November 18.

Learn · Physics from first principles

Open problems: where physics still breaks

Lesson 15 of 15: the unsolved questions, and why the small cracks matter most.

Two. That was how many big problems Lord Kelvin said were left in classical physics, in a speech in 1900. He called them "two clouds" hanging over the 20th century.

Start with something you already know. Drop a cup and it falls. Heat a kettle and the water warms. Over fourteen lessons we built ideas that predict this kind of thing. This last lesson asks where those ideas fail, because that is where physics is still alive.

An open problem comes in two flavours. Sometimes a theory cannot explain something we have observed. Other times the puzzle is practical: nobody can yet build an experiment to test an idea, or to look closely enough at a phenomenon.

Back to Kelvin's clouds. One was the lack of evidence for the luminiferous aether. The other was that a rule called the equipartition theorem failed to predict the heat capacity of solids, meaning how much heat a solid soaks up as it warms. Here's the clever bit: both were solved within a decade, by special relativity and quantum mechanics. Two small clouds, two revolutions.

So how fast do open problems fall? Take the worked numbers. In 1984 Barry Simon listed 15 open problems on quantum operators. By 2000, about 5 were solved, which is one in three after 16 years. Roger Penrose's 1982 list on general relativity still had about 14 unsolved in 2017. And a list of 10 problems from the Strings 2000 conference had none conclusively solved as of 2025.

Here's the catch for today's physics. The mathematics of the Standard Model, our theory of particles, is inconsistent with general relativity, our theory of gravity. Both break down in extreme places, like the Big Bang and the centres of black holes. Beyond that sit more open questions: the absolute mass of neutrinos, why there is more matter than antimatter, and what dark matter and dark energy are.

Vitaly Ginzburg, who kept a list of open problems from 1971 to 2005, added three great ones: the arrow of time, the interpretations of quantum mechanics, and whether life can arise from physics alone. This is the last lesson, so no next one answers it. The question is yours: which of today's cracks is the next cloud that turns into a revolution?

Key facts

  • In 1900 Lord Kelvin named "two clouds" over classical physics; both were solved within a decade by special relativity and quantum mechanics.
  • The Standard Model's mathematics is inconsistent with general relativity, so both break down at singularities like the Big Bang and black hole centres.
  • Barry Simon's 1984 list of 15 problems had about 5 solved by 2000.
  • Of 10 problems proposed at Strings 2000, none was conclusively solved as of 2025.

The Day, Connected

The news shows the loud part. The slow part is underneath.

Three patterns running under a day of headlines, and one question that works on any of them.

Read a day of headlines and you see events. Read them twice and you start to see what they are standing on. Here is what I saw underneath today.

First, trust is the scarce thing. An Anthropic mathematician says he and Claude proved the Riemann hypothesis, and other mathematicians say it needs rigorous checking first. An AI checked 1,000 AI-written economics papers and none was error-free. Making claims has become cheap. Checking them is still slow, and that is where the weight sits now.

Second, the systems we rely on are more fragile than they look. A new router helped halt every US departure for hours, and nobody yet knows why it flooded the network. Old, quiet infrastructure works until someone upgrades it. Then everyone learns how much hung on it.

Third, rules belong to whoever can say no. More than 70 countries want talks on killer robots, but a treaty that runs on unanimity gives every state a veto. Old pressures keep resurfacing this way, because the rule for deciding is older than the question being decided.

And the news itself is a trick of timing. A ground stop, an IPO, a falling stock are events you can photograph. Veto rules, ageing networks and slow checking are processes, and they only show up when something breaks.

So here is the question that turns any headline into understanding: what slow thing had to be true for this sudden thing to happen?

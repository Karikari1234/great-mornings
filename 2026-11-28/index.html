Cover story

Two Italians died in Kuwait, and Iran calls the incident suspicious

Kuwait says Iranian attacks killed two Italians with falling missile debris, while Iran denies any role and offers to help investigate.

Iran's foreign minister said he was sorry two people had died. In the same message, he said his country had nothing to do with it.

Here is the claim. Kuwait's army said on X that two Italians were killed by falling missile debris after Iranian attacks on the country. Two others were injured.

Here's the catch. Iran's military told state media that no offensive or military operations had been carried out in the area. It called the accusations a cover for "continued malevolence" by U.S. and Israeli forces.

Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said he had spoken with Italy's Antonio Tajani and expressed regret, while stressing Tehran had no role. Then he wrote on X: "The incident is suspicious and looks like a precooked recipe for war." That's a pointed way of hinting someone wants things to escalate.

He also said Iran was ready to cooperate with Kuwait and Italy in investigating. So far, that is an offer.

Meanwhile, the pressure from another side was building. Israeli military spokesman Effie Defrin said Israel was ready to strike Iran "at any moment", even without U.S. forces joining. That came a day after U.S. envoy Jared Kushner told a podcast that Israel "needs to get out of the constant wartime mindset."

Israel has been pushing the Trump administration to back another offensive. Reuters reported on Nov. 18 that Israeli spy chief Roman Gofman pushed for joint strikes when he met U.S. officials earlier in the month.

Then came Trump. Late Thursday he told PBS the U.S. had "victory at hand", and that it would come "whenever I want it to be, to be honest."

On a Thanksgiving call with U.S. military personnel, he said Washington was still trying to give Iran "every chance to make a deal and save a lot of lives." He added that he was not sure Tehran would agree: "Are they crazy? Maybe they are."

So the question hanging over Thursday is a plain one. Who examines the debris, and will both sides accept what they find?

Key facts

  • Kuwait's army said two Italians were killed by falling missile debris after Iranian attacks, and two others were injured.
  • Iran denies any role; Araghchi called the incident a "precooked recipe for war" and offered to cooperate in an investigation.
  • Israel said it was ready to strike Iran "at any moment", even without U.S. forces.
  • Trump told PBS victory would come "whenever I want it to be".

Learn · Physics from first principles

Where physics runs out of answers

An open problem begins where an explanation fails or an experiment cannot yet settle a question.

You can feel a solid object warm up. In 1900, the amount of heat a solid could hold was one of two problems Lord Kelvin called "clouds" over physics. How could something so ordinary trouble a theory?

A theory earns its place by explaining what we observe. If its prediction fails, physicists have a question to solve. Kelvin's problem was that a classical theory gave the wrong prediction for the heat capacity of solids.

Here is the worked example. Kelvin named two clouds in 1900: the failed heat prediction and a lack of evidence for a proposed substance called the aether. Within a decade, quantum mechanics resolved the heat problem and special relativity resolved the aether problem. Two questions on that list, two resolved: two minus two leaves zero.

Open problems today can take two forms. Sometimes an existing theory cannot explain an observation. Sometimes someone has proposed an explanation, but making an experiment to test it is the hard part.

One question goes deeper. The Standard Model describes particles, while general relativity describes gravity. Their mathematical frameworks do not fit together, and both break down under extreme conditions such as those at the Big Bang and at the centers of black holes.

Kelvin's clouds show that a failed prediction can lead to a new way of understanding the world. If our current theories break down together, what would a theory that joins them need to explain?

Key facts

  • Kelvin identified two problems in classical physics in 1900.
  • Both were resolved within a decade through special relativity and quantum mechanics.
  • Some open problems concern explanations; others concern experiments.
  • The Standard Model and general relativity are mathematically incompatible.

That is a useful way into this list. Book Marks, Lit Hub’s home for book reviews, includes Long Wave among the month’s best reviewed fiction, alongside Louise Kennedy’s Stations and Tom Drury’s West of Loveland. The roundup gives you glimpses of what readers admired in each.

For Long Wave, the praise starts with Johnson’s sentences. The excerpt describes images that feel drawn from myth or fairytale, held close to ordinary truth. If you tend to read quickly to find out what happens, this review asks you to slow down and notice how the telling feels.

Kennedy’s Stations draws a different kind of praise. Its excerpt calls the work extraordinary and bittersweet, then points to a lived-in caution in Kennedy’s writing. The reviewer says they would hurry to read her next project, whatever she chose to write about.

West of Loveland gets a quieter description. Its excerpt praises the novel’s measured pace and the power it gathers as it goes. It calls the book a humane meditation on fate, belonging and connections between seemingly ordinary lives.

Notice what these three excerpts give you, and what they leave open. They say little here about who does what in the stories. Instead, they tell you how the books move: through careful language, a distinctive way of seeing, or an effect that builds over time.

Who is responsible when roles change?

Today's headlines point to slower shifts in power, risk and trust.

A state investor can now do the job of a company buyer. Some sovereign funds buy and manage companies themselves, taking on private equity's work and risks. That sounds like a change in technique, but it points to a question running through today's news: when an institution changes roles, how do you know what it can be trusted to do?

Trust also depends on whose account of an event holds up. Kuwait says Iranian attacks killed two Italians through falling missile debris; Iran denies any role and offers to help investigate. The deaths do not wait for an agreed explanation, and neither do the judgments people make about who is responsible.

Technology brings its own version of the question. Children are forming bonds with AI companions as downloads rise, with a gap in protection; Nvidia has strong sales and a huge order backlog even as the bubble question persists. In each case, people are using or investing in something before they have a settled answer to who carries the risk.

A news day makes these look like separate events, each with a clear beginning and a name attached. The slower story is about rules and trust catching up with new power, and about the pressure that builds when they cannot. For any headline, what has been changing underneath long enough to make this moment possible?

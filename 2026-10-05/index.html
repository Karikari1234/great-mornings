Trio wins Nobel Prize in medicine for discoveries in optogenetics

Karl Deisseroth, Peter Hegemann and Georg Nagel won the award for their discoveries concerning light-gated ion channels and optogenetics.

The Nobel Prize in physiology or medicine for 2026 has gone to Karl Deisseroth, Peter Hegemann and Georg Nagel, the Nobel Assembly announced on Monday, October 5. The three scientists received the award for their discoveries concerning light-gated ion channels and optogenetics. The technique combines optics and genetics to stimulate neurons without invasive procedures.

The work began as Hegemann studied how a single-celled green alga swims toward light. He and Nagel discovered a protein that opens a channel to create an electrical impulse when illuminated by blue light. When placed into other cells, the protein makes them light-sensitive. Deisseroth then worked with the gene that controls that protein to create a light-controlled switch for nerve cells in the brains of living mice.

The three laureates are based at universities in Germany and the United States. Hegemann is a professor of neuroscience at Humboldt University of Berlin and a specialist in photobiology. Nagel is a biophysicist and professor of neurophysiology at the University of Würzburg. Deisseroth is a professor of bioengineering and of psychiatry and behavioral sciences at Stanford University.

Optogenetics allows researchers to observe how neural pathways function and how brain circuits become disrupted. Abdel El Manira, a Nobel committee member and professor of neuroscience, stated that the findings carry implications for conditions such as blindness and depression. In clinical medicine, researchers are using the method in attempts to restore sight in people with visual impairment. Researchers also hope the field could lead to treatments for Parkinson's disease and epilepsy.

The medicine prize is the first Nobel award announced this week. Laureates in physics, chemistry, literature and peace will be named through Friday. The awards will be presented at ceremonies in Stockholm, Sweden on December 10. Each prize carries a cash award of 12 million Swedish kronor, equivalent to just over $1.2 million or €1 million.

Key facts

  • October 5, 2026: Announcement date of the 2026 Nobel Prize in physiology or medicine.
  • Karl Deisseroth, Peter Hegemann and Georg Nagel: The three laureates based in Germany and the United States.
  • 12 million Swedish kronor (about $1.2 million): The cash award accompanying the prize.
  • December 10: Date of the award presentation ceremony in Stockholm, Sweden.

Bangladesh remittance inflows rise 15.1 percent in early FY27

Bangladeshi workers sent 8.798 billion dollars home between July 1 and October 3, up 15.1 percent from a year earlier.

Read

Bangladesh received $8.798 billion in remittances between July 1 and October 3, an increase of 15.1 percent over the $7.643 billion collected in the same period of the previous fiscal year. Arief Hossain Khan, executive spokesperson of Bangladesh Bank, confirmed the figures on Sunday, October 4. He stated that the growth was driven by higher transfers from Bangladeshi expatriate workers during the opening months of the fiscal year.

The central bank recorded $1.877 billion in remittance inflows during the first 20 days of September. Of that sum, workers transferred $130 million across three days, from September 17 to September 19, compared to $1.869 billion received during the first 19 days of September last year. Gross foreign exchange reserves were approximately $36.14 billion as of September 29, after standing at $36.30 billion on September 20. Under the International Monetary Fund's BPM6 calculation method, foreign exchange reserves were $31.45 billion on September 20.

Mars orbiter photographs coral reef patterns in Arabia Terra

Images from the Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter show rock layers shaped by ancient water and cyclical dust storms.

The Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter has photographed rock formations resembling coral reefs in the Arabia Terra region of Mars. Regular patterns etched into the rock and dust indicate that cyclical forces created them before surface water disappeared. The solidified stacks of sediment point to regular shifts in the surface and atmosphere, with water likely involved in their formation.

Arabia Terra stretches as much as 2,800 miles across the planet, roughly the distance across the continental United States. Billions of years of weathering have left the region eroded, with craters and canyons across the terrain. Wind and loose debris moving across the surface produce the jagged rock edges visible today.

The images come from the High Resolution Imaging Science Experiment camera, known as HiRISE. The instrument is a telescope pointed down at Mars, fitted with a mirror about a foot and a half across. It records color data across three bands: near-infrared, red, and blue-green.

Technicians shifted the color bands and enhanced the contrast to make each band visible. To an observer at steady orbital altitude, the Martian surface has a reddish-brown tint. The underlying rock and sand is darker and more blue, composed of volcanic basalt rich in iron and magnesium. Lighter patches consist of finer-grained materials, including salt and clay.

Scientists are analyzing the rock layers to study the history of the Martian climate. Current observations remain limited to inferences drawn from orbit. A rover placed in Arabia Terra in the future would determine what the rocks are made of and how they formed.

Key facts

  • Arabia Terra stretches up to 2,800 miles across Mars.
  • The HiRISE camera carries a mirror about a foot and a half across.
  • The rock formations contain basalt rich in iron and magnesium, alongside salt and clay.

Thirty-one people die in two-train collision at Ladbroke Grove

A collision between two passenger trains at Ladbroke Grove killed 31 people and injured 417 after an outbound service passed a red signal.

Two passenger trains collided almost head-on at Ladbroke Grove in London at about 08:09 BST on 5 October 1999. The crash killed 31 people and injured 417. A British Rail Class 165 Turbo diesel train operated by Thames Trains left Paddington Station at 08:06 BST for Bedwyn in Wiltshire. About two miles west of Paddington, the train passed a red signal at Portobello Junction. Points directed the train onto the track of an incoming First Great Western service from Cheltenham. The trains collided at a combined speed of approximately 130 mph.

Both train drivers died in the crash. The Thames Trains driver was 31-year-old Michael Hodder. Brian Cooper, aged 52, drove the First Great Western train, an InterCity 125 high speed train. The collision killed 23 people on the Class 165 train and five on the high speed train. The leading carriage of the Class 165 was destroyed. Diesel fuel dispersed and ignited. Fires spread through the wreckage. Coach H near the front of the high speed train was completely burnt out, and one passenger died from the fire. The author Jilly Cooper survived the crash.

The immediate cause of the collision was the passage of the Class 165 past red signal SN109 on overhead gantry 8. The previous signal displayed a single yellow aspect to warn of the red aspect ahead. Hodder had qualified as a driver two weeks before the crash. An inquiry found his training defective in assessing situation-handling skills and in notifying him of signals passed at danger. Signal SN109 had been passed at danger eight times in the six years before the crash. Hodder received no specific warning of those incidents.

Visibility on the morning of 5 October 1999 was affected by sunlight and track infrastructure. The morning was clear, and low sunlight behind Hodder reflected off yellow signal aspects. The driver of an earlier westbound train reported that signals across gantry 6 "all lit up like a Christmas tree" at about 07:50 BST. The public inquiry found that poor placement of signal SN109 and the sunlight reflections made it probable that Hodder believed he was able to proceed. Equipment installed in 1994 for the Heathrow Express also obstructed drivers' views of signals on the route.

The crash was the second major collision on the Great Western Main Line in just over two years. The Southall rail crash occurred on the line in September 1997. An operational automatic train protection system would have prevented both crashes. Wider fitting of the system had been rejected on cost grounds. In 2000, Lord Cullen conducted a public inquiry into the crash. A separate joint inquiry that year confirmed the rejection of the automatic system and mandated a cheaper system. Major changes followed in the formal responsibilities for the safety management and regulation of British rail transport.

  • 31 people were killed and 417 were injured in the collision.
  • The trains collided at a combined speed of approximately 130 mph at about 08:09 BST on 5 October 1999.
  • Thames Trains driver Michael Hodder had qualified two weeks before the crash.
  • Signal SN109 had been passed at danger on eight occasions in the six years before the crash.

How force shapes the physical motion of objects

An introduction to force as a vector quantity that changes the velocity of objects.

A person pushing a cart across the ground experiences force directly. In mechanics, force makes everyday ideas like pushing or pulling mathematically precise. In physics, a force is an action that can cause an object to change its velocity or its shape. Magnitude and direction are both important, which makes force a vector quantity. The SI unit of force is the newton (N), and force is often represented by the symbol F.

Early thinkers struggled to define why objects move. Aristotle placed the concept of force within his cosmology, teaching that terrestrial objects composed of earth and water remain at rest on the ground if left alone. In Aristotle's view, keeping a cart in motion required the continuous application of a force. Projectiles challenged this explanation. When an archer releases an arrow, the arrow sails through the air without an obvious cause acting upon it. Aristotle proposed that air displaced along the path carries the arrow forward. His view suffered from an incomplete understanding of friction.

In the 17th century, Galileo Galilei corrected these early errors. Galileo rolled stones and cannonballs down an incline to test the Aristotelian theory of motion. The experiment showed that gravity accelerates bodies to an extent independent of their mass. Galileo argued that objects retain their velocity unless acted on by a force, such as friction. Force changes motion. A continuous force is unnecessary to maintain constant velocity.

Forces often act simultaneously on an extended body. In such a body, each part applies forces to adjacent parts, creating internal mechanical stress. When multiple forces act together, their vectors combine. For example, if multiple opposing forces act on an extended body such that the sum of the forces equals 0 newtons, the net force is zero. In this state, the body is in equilibrium.

Modern physics revised the laws of motion to rely on fundamental interactions. By the early 20th century, Albert Einstein developed the theory of relativity to predict how forces act on objects moving near the speed of light. In particle physics, the Standard Model describes forces between subatomic particles as the emission and absorption of gauge bosons. Physicists recognize four fundamental interactions. In order of decreasing strength, these are the strong, electromagnetic, weak, and gravitational interactions. Observations in the 1970s and 1980s confirmed that the weak and electromagnetic forces unite into an electroweak interaction.

Despite these modern insights, classical mechanics remains useful for practical purposes. Isaac Newton formulated mathematical laws of motion that were not improved for over two hundred years. The concept of force is central to all three of Newton's laws. How do Newton's three laws of motion use force to govern the movement of objects?

Key facts

  • The SI unit of force is the newton (N), and the concept is represented by the symbol F.
  • Sir Isaac Newton formulated laws of motion that were not improved for over two hundred years.
  • Only four fundamental interactions exist: strong, electromagnetic, weak, and gravitational.

Zelenskyy says Russia breached Trump's energy ceasefire

An attack on Ukrainian gas facilities has deepened confusion over a truce Trump announced before Kyiv had seen its terms.

Picture the Kharkiv region of Ukraine and gas facilities owned by the state company Naftogaz. Its workers are repairing equipment after an overnight Russian attack damaged the site and caused a fire. On Monday, October 12, Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy accused Russia of violating an "energy ceasefire" announced by US President Donald Trump.

Trump said on Sunday that Russia and Ukraine had agreed to stop attacks on energy infrastructure. Zelenskyy initially said the agreement was news to him. Speaking by video to a security forum in Warsaw, Poland's capital, he said Ukraine was waiting to learn the terms agreed between the Americans and Russians, and what the US would do to enforce them.

Both countries have attacked each other's energy sites for months. Those strikes have added pressure to global energy prices, which have risen during the US-Israeli war on Iran. A day before his accusation, Zelenskyy said Ukraine was ready to stop hitting Russian oil refineries if Moscow stopped attacking Ukrainian energy infrastructure.

In Paris, French President Emmanuel Macron said he had seen no Russian energy ceasefire over the previous 48 hours. In Brussels, the European Union announced almost 1,600 new sanctions on people and organisations tied to Russia's military industry. Sanctions restrict dealings with named targets. The EU says these measures aim to make Russian weapons harder and more expensive to produce.

Polish Prime Minister Donald Tusk said he would ask European partners to consider more sanctions. Zelenskyy said the truce could work if the US made sure Russia respected it. Kyiv is still waiting for the terms and for specific US steps to enforce them.

Key facts

  • Trump announced the proposed energy ceasefire on Sunday.
  • Naftogaz said an overnight attack damaged its gas facilities in the Kharkiv region.
  • The EU announced almost 1,600 new sanctions on Russia's military industrial complex.

Learn · Physics from first principles

Electricity and magnetism build on electric charges and fields

Calculus connects electric charges, fields, and electromagnetic induction into a unified framework.

A person notices that objects push or pull across a gap, like a spark or a magnet on a door. Physics explains this action through electric charges and fields. In a structured sequence of study, these ideas form unit 8 of physical principles. This unit comes after 7 units of Newtonian mechanics.

Electric charges create fields in the space around them. Gauss's law uses calculus to formulate how those electric fields spread outward from charges. Physical principles describe how forces act across space. Calculus provides the mathematical method to apply these principles to physical problems.

Magnetism connects to electricity in the same physical framework. Electromagnetism culminates in electromagnetic induction, which is unit 13 in the sequential curriculum. Through induction, changing magnetic fields produce electric effects. The study moves sequentially from charges in unit 8 to induction in unit 13.

Applying these principles to physical problems requires measuring rates and quantities. Consider the timing required to solve physical problems under College Board standards. A student has 80 minutes to answer 40 multiple choice questions on electromagnetism. Eighty minutes divided by 40 questions yields an average rate of 2 minutes per question. For free response problems, 100 minutes divided by 4 questions yields 25 minutes per question.

Electromagnetism was once taught alongside Newtonian mechanics in a single exam before 1973. In 1973, the College Board created two separate exams for Newtonian mechanics and electromagnetism. Students use methods of calculus to solve physical problems in electromagnetism. This study builds directly on the foundation of mechanics.

The Day, Connected

The distance between a promise and what holds

Today's headlines show how much depends on clear rules and systems that can bear strain.

A promise can reach the public before the people expected to live by it know its terms. That is the gap in the reported energy ceasefire, which Kyiv had not seen when Trump announced it. Trust depends on something quieter than an announcement: whether the parties share the same account of what was agreed, and whether events on the ground fit it.

Distance offers little protection when daily life depends on routes and schedules. The closure of Riyadh airport stranded 4,851 passengers in Dhaka. A disruption in one place becomes a missed journey somewhere else. The headline is a strike and a closed airport. Underneath are the connections people count on each day, usually without having to think about them.

There is another kind of strain that builds without a single dramatic moment. In Bangladesh, dengue deaths reached 303 as mosquito control fell short. In the AI industry, creditors are examining what data centre leases leave companies owing. These are different pressures, but both draw attention to the work and the costs that continue after a plan is announced.

News arrives as an event: a statement, a closure, a new count. The slower story is whether rules are clear, whether essential systems keep working, and who carries the cost when they do not. A day's headlines give those processes a visible moment. To understand any one of them, the question is: what had to be in place for this to happen, and who now has to live with it?

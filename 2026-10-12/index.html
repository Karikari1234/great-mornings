Donald Trump announced an energy ceasefire that Ukraine says Russia has already violated

Donald Trump declared a halt to strikes on power grids, but Russia hit Kharkiv anyway while Moscow says it does not want a ceasefire.

Donald Trump announced on Sunday that Ukraine and Russia had agreed to an energy ceasefire. The only problem was that Ukraine said it knew nothing about it.

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy called it news to him, adding that a real ceasefire needs to exist in reality, not just on Truth Social.

After the announcement, Russia struck several Ukrainian energy facilities, including a power plant in Kharkiv. Overnight, an attack on gas production facilities run by the state firm Naftogaz in the Kharkiv region caused a fire.

Here is the background. Both sides have spent months firing at each other's energy infrastructure. Trump announced a deal that Kyiv said it knew nothing about, two days after he declared a temporary easing of American sanctions on Russian diesel.

Speaking by video link to a security forum in Warsaw on Monday, Zelenskyy said Ukraine was horribly surprised by the fresh attacks. He warned that if Russian strikes continue, Ukraine will hit back.

So what does Moscow say? Kremlin spokesperson Dmitry Peskov bluntly cleared things up by stating that Russia does not want a ceasefire, only long-term peace. Peskov also dismissed a separate proposal from Kyiv for a 60-day election truce.

European leaders agree the truce never started. French President Emmanuel Macron noted no energy ceasefire from the Russian side over the last 48 hours. Meanwhile, in Brussels, the European Union, in a bid to maintain pressure on Moscow, added almost 1,600 new sanctions on Russia's military-industrial complex, its largest expansion of its sanctions list since the war started.

Key facts

  • Donald Trump announced an energy ceasefire between Ukraine and Russia, but Russia struck Ukrainian energy facilities despite the announcement.
  • Volodymyr Zelenskyy said the announcement was news to him and urged Washington to enforce the truce if it is real.
  • Kremlin spokesperson Dmitry Peskov said Russia does not want a ceasefire.
  • The European Union added nearly 1,600 new sanctions targeting Russia's military-industrial complex.

Protesters throw bricks at GM Quader's Uttara home, demand his arrest

A group calling itself "Anti-Autocracy General Public" broke windows at the Jatiya Party chairman's house, accusing him of propping up Hasina's rule and staying in touch with her.

GM Quader says he and his wife were having breakfast on Monday when they heard shouting outside their house on Road 33 in Uttara's Sector 7.

Outside, a group had sat down on the road and was chanting slogans. Witnesses say people then threw bricks and stones at the house, breaking windowpanes. Nobody got in. Quader says the locked gate stopped them.

Their banner named them "Anti-Autocracy General Public" and made one demand: arrest Quader, for betraying the July martyrs and for talking to Sheikh Hasina.

Here's the puzzle. Quader was leader of the opposition in parliament during Hasina's final term. So why would people angry at her rule go after the man whose job was to oppose it?

The protesters' answer is about elections. They say the Jatiya Party helped legitimise the Awami League government by taking part in the last three national polls. In their telling, being in the race was what made the race look real.

The second charge is one Quader does not deny. He confirmed he has spoken with Hasina, who left for India on August 5, 2024, as her government fell. He told India Today Digital he thought his recent remarks about that contact were "most probably" the trigger.

So who threw the bricks? Quader alleges supporters of the BNP, Jamaat-e-Islami and Inqilab Moncho were involved. Police had not identified anyone at the time of reporting, and his claim is unverified.

Even the basics are blurry. Police say 30 to 40 people threw bricks. Quader says he saw 50 to 60 young men. Other estimates go as high as 100 to 150. Reported times run from about 10am to 11:30am.

Khalid Mansur, officer-in-charge of Uttara West Police Station, said the attackers had gone by the time police arrived, and officers were posted there afterward. The reports do not say whether anyone was hurt, or whether anyone has been arrested.

Two things to watch now: what the Jatiya Party announces, and whether police can put names to the people who threw the bricks. Quader's press secretary says the party will set out its next step later.

Youth protests over 130 million removed voter names test Modi

A satirical youth group called the Cockroach Janta Party is protesting India's voter list revision, which opposition parties say favours Modi's party, and police crackdowns haven't stopped its protests.

130 million. That's how many names have been taken off India's voter lists since a revision began last year.

That is more people than live in all of Japan.

Here's how it works. India's Election Commission, which runs the country's elections, is carrying out what it calls a Special Intensive Revision. The commission and the government say the point is to clear out duplicate and ineligible entries.

Opposition parties say the removals favour Prime Minister Narendra Modi's Bharatiya Janata Party, or BJP, and could cut eligible voters off the rolls. The commission and the BJP deny any manipulation. Nobody yet knows how many of the removed names belonged to real, eligible voters.

So who has taken this to the streets? A group called the Cockroach Janta Party. Yes, really. It started in May as an online satirical youth movement, then moved into street protests over alleged exam paper leaks. Those protests ended with Education Minister Dharmendra Pradhan resigning in July.

Now the group wants Chief Election Commissioner Gyanesh Kumar to resign. On October 10, police stopped demonstrators heading to Jantar Mantar, New Delhi's designated protest site, and detained many of them before anything began. Metro stations were closed and mobile internet was cut in parts of the city.

How many people were detained? Reuters reported more than 2,000. The Guardian reported more than 7,000. Police said most were let go that night.

Here's the catch for anyone trying to shut this down. One detained activist, 29-year-old student leader Neha Bora, said in a video: "I am in detention. The movement is not." Bora urged anyone stopped by police to protest wherever they were.

The next day, small groups marched across New Delhi again. Three of the movement's leaders, who had been taken into custody as their flight from Mumbai landed, were released on the condition that they commit no "breach of peace" for a year. One of them, Saurav Das, said: "A situation has been created where the state is at war with its own citizens."

On October 12, opposition lawmakers and thousands of supporters marched toward the Supreme Court and handed the chief justice a petition alleging irregularities in the revision. The same day, police said Jantar Mantar would no longer be a rally site and named three new sites to replace it.

What to watch: national elections aren't due until 2029, but voters in Uttar Pradesh and Gujarat, two states the BJP governs, go to the polls in 2027.

Key facts

  • More than 130 million names have been removed from India's voter lists since the revision began last year.
  • The Cockroach Janta Party began as an online satirical youth movement in May 2026, and its exam leak protests ended with the education minister's resignation in July.
  • Reuters reported more than 2,000 protesters detained on October 10. The Guardian reported more than 7,000.
  • State elections in Uttar Pradesh and Gujarat are due in 2027, and national elections in 2029.

Why Saudi Arabia had to close its capital airport

Saudi Arabia shut its main international airport after repeated Houthi missile strikes killed 14 people, exposing how thin air defenses become when everything needs guarding at once.

Saudi Arabia is one of the heaviest spenders on modern military hardware anywhere. Yet on Monday, its capital city had to shut its own main international airport because incoming missiles kept getting through.

Thirteen people died on Saturday and more than 300 were injured when Houthi missiles hit the terminal at King Khalid International Airport. Saudi state television showed passengers caught in smoke and falling masonry while waiting in line to board a plane. Then another strike hit on Monday, killing one more person and pushing authorities to extend the suspension of all flights.

Here is why this keeps happening. Air defense sounds absolute, but in practice it is an impossible math problem. Saudi interceptors have to guard oil plants, government buildings, and cities scattered across a massive desert territory. Think of trying to catch tennis balls thrown at a large glass greenhouse when you only have a few mitts. If attackers fire enough drones and missiles, gaps appear.

The Houthis have vowed to attack Saudi airports across the country. Recent strikes destroyed a parked Airbus A320 and damaged at least five other planes. They have also aimed at Khamis Mushait and King Fahd International Airport in Dammam, warning that every airport in the country is on their list.

Inside Riyadh, officials tried to keep life moving. The kingdom pressed ahead with an international energy conference, where Energy Minister Abdulaziz bin Salman insisted that fear has no place in the Saudi lexicon. But outside the hall, schoolchildren and some office workers were advised to work from home.

The next move happens in New York on Tuesday, when the UN Security Council is expected to debate labeling the Houthis a foreign terrorist organization. If that passes, it may squeeze the group diplomatically, but it will not put more interceptor batteries on the tarmac in Riyadh.

Key facts

  • Houthi missile strikes killed 13 people on Saturday and one more on Monday at Riyadh's main airport.
  • More than 300 people were injured, one parked Airbus A320 was destroyed, and at least five other aircraft were damaged.
  • Saudi aviation authorities halted all flights at King Khalid International Airport to verify safety as air defenses struggled to intercept incoming fire.

Microsoft is building its own chips to lean less on Nvidia

Microsoft wants to rely less on Nvidia by building custom chips for its own products and services.

Running modern artificial intelligence costs an enormous amount of electric power. Microsoft wants to lean less on outside chipmakers like Nvidia.

To pull that off, the company just showed off the Maia 300, its third-generation home-grown processor. It will start running live production AI workloads in 2027.

Microsoft plans to roll out the silicon across multi-gigawatt facilities. One gigawatt is enough energy to power hundreds of thousands of standard homes.

Why build custom silicon instead of buying ready-made parts? The simple goal is to make every watt of electricity produce more work.

Scott Guthrie, who runs Microsoft's cloud division, says Maia 300 should produce 40 percent more tokens per megawatt than top graphics processors. Tokens are the basic pieces of words that AI models digest and generate.

That efficiency comes down to physical layout. Built by TSMC on advanced manufacturing lines, Maia 300 splits computing cores, memory controllers, and data connections into separate modular slices. Think of a workshop where noisy cutting machines and delicate assembly tables sit in separate rooms instead of crowding one bench.

Packing 96 of these processors into a single server rack delivers up to seven times the output of the older Maia 200 rack introduced in January. That older chip already powers tools like GitHub Copilot.

Mustafa Suleyman, who leads Microsoft AI, argues that Microsoft needs a platform it owns and co-designs with its models.

The real test arrives in 2027, when Maia 300 starts rolling out to production.

Key facts

  • Microsoft unveiled Maia 300, a third-generation custom AI chip arriving in 2027.
  • The company expects 40 percent better energy efficiency than leading GPU setups.
  • Deployments will scale to multi-gigawatt levels across Microsoft data centers.

Cuttlefish manage it. They are cephalopods, the group that also includes squid and octopuses, and they use changing skin patterns both to hide and to communicate. Scientists are now trying to work out how the cuttlefish brain pulls this off.

First, the hardware. Their skin holds tiny pigment-filled structures called chromatophores. Muscles controlled by nerves change the size of each one, and that changes the pattern you see. Cuttlefish can change their skin's texture, too.

So the real mystery is the software. What decides which ones to change, and by how much?

A 2023 study offered a peek. Researchers at the Max Planck Institute for Brain Research and the Okinawa Institute of Science and Technology filmed common cuttlefish against different backgrounds with an array of high-resolution cameras. They ended up with roughly 200,000 images of skin patterns.

The team used a neural network, a kind of AI that learns patterns from data, to measure features like brightness, roughness, shape and contrast.

Here's the catch. The cuttlefish matched natural and artificial backgrounds alike, yet they didn't run a fixed routine. Show them the same background again and the pattern came out subtly different. They took different routes from one pattern to the next, and sometimes paused midway.

"The cuttlefish would often overshoot their target skin pattern, pause, and then come back," said study co-author Theodosia Woo.

Think of setting a shower: too hot, wait, ease it back. That's a search for the right answer, and a search needs feedback.

Wait, so how does a cuttlefish know it missed? The 2023 researchers hadn't worked that out. It might check its skin with its eyes, or it might sense the contraction of the muscles around the chromatophores.

There's one more layer. When threatened, the animals turned pale fast, a response called blanching, and the researchers saw it laid on top of the camouflage. Back then, the team planned to record brain activity during both.

So the question this started with comes back sharper. When a cuttlefish gets its disguise wrong, what tells it?

Scientists can now use artificial intelligence to design completely new proteins from scratch. That could bring cures for nasty diseases, but it also creates a terrifying loophole: someone could ask an AI to invent a lethal new toxin that no human has ever seen.

Here is how biological security usually works. To actually turn a digital protein into physical matter, a researcher must order its DNA blueprint from a commercial synthesis company. Those companies screen incoming orders against databases of known viruses and poisons.

If a dangerous sequence differs widely from anything in those databases, existing databases can fail to flag it. The dangerous order slips right through.

DeepMind wants to close that blind spot with a tool called SynthID Bio. It borrows an idea from text watermarking, which nudges an AI's word choices without altering what a sentence says. Think of it like picking "researcher" instead of "scientist."

SynthID Bio does the same thing with the building blocks of proteins. As AlphaFold3 designs a molecule, the tool slightly nudges which amino acids are picked and makes tiny shifts to where atoms sit.

Proteins are finicky, and even tiny alterations can ruin how they work. Yet in lab tests, DeepMind found these marked proteins grabbed onto target molecules just as well as unwatermarked ones.

Not everyone is thrilled. Oliver Crook at the University of Oxford pointed out that jostling molecules creates extra complexity and could yield less optimal proteins for non-sensitive research.

There is also a simpler catch. If someone runs a marked design through another design tool, the watermark can be scrubbed right off.

If the software designing our future medicines cannot keep its own signature intact, how will screening companies tell a cure from a bioweapon?

The Day, Connected

The wire behind the curtain

The daily news captures the sudden collisions, but the real story is the slow wear on the steel underneath.

Most days feel like a sudden shower of accidents. Look past the flash, though, and you can see two older currents pulling everything along. One is how brittle our shared plumbing has become, and the other is a scramble to cut ties before the next shock hits.

The morning paper tracks the crash, while the real culprit is usually the slow rust underneath. Think of an overloaded power strip behind a desk that everyone ignores until a spark jumps. When systems run with zero slack, ordinary friction turns into an emergency overnight.

You see that wear everywhere today: missiles shut Riyadh's airport, hackers defrost supermarket chillers across France, Microsoft builds custom chips to shake off a supplier, and a promised energy truce in Ukraine collapses on day one. None of these fractures came out of nowhere; each one exposed an old dependence that finally gave way.

That gap between what happens and why it happens is the trick of following the news. An event arrives with a bang, while the process behind it moved in silence for years. We treat the puncture as the whole story and forget to inspect how thin the rubber was worn.

That leaves the one question that turns any headline into understanding. What kind of quiet load was the system carrying before it finally snapped?

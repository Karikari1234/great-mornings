Nvidia posted a great quarter, then lost 13.8% in a day

US stocks sank again on Friday as worries about AI spending beat good earnings, pushing the S&P 500 and Dow into a correction.

Nvidia's shares tumbled 13.8 percent on Friday. One investor, Chuck Carlson of Horizon Investment Services, said the company had just reported what might have been one of the greatest quarters in corporate earnings history, and that "they did everything right."

So why the plunge? Because the market was not really grading Nvidia's profits. It was worrying about the risks of AI and the massive spending on it.

Here's the scoreboard. A correction means a stock index has fallen more than 10 percent from its record high, and both the S&P 500 and the Dow are now there. The S&P 500 lost 3.70 percent on Friday, its seventh straight drop and its longest losing streak since February 2020.

The Nasdaq, which is packed with tech companies, fell 5.28 percent in a single day. That is about $5.28 gone from every $100. It is now down for the year, and its week was its worst since March 2020.

Where does Anthropic come in? It sold shares to the public for the first time on Thursday at $83 each, hoping to reach a market value of as much as $2 trillion. It closed 17.1 percent above that price, but enthusiasm faded as the session went on, and on Friday it fell 9.1 percent.

Carlson said many people, himself included, had looked to the debut as "a clearing event," a moment to calm the nerves. "It wasn't."

Here's the catch for anyone who bought the AI chip story. The Philadelphia semiconductor index was up as much as 106.6 percent for the year in mid-June. Now it is up 18.7 percent. Put in $100 on January 1 and you would have seen about $206.60 at the peak, and about $118.70 today.

The damage spread. Meta dropped 5.9 percent and Amazon 3.9 percent. The newer AI cloud firms CoreWeave and Nebius slid 12.1 and 16.1 percent. Wall Street's "fear gauge," the VIX, closed at its highest since April 7, 2025.

Bill Northey of US Bank Wealth Management put it simply. In the immediate term, he said, sentiment is the more powerful force, while fundamentals tend to win out over time.

Nvidia did everything right and still fell 13.8 percent. Friday, it turns out, was not over time.

Key facts

  • The S&P 500 and Dow closed more than 10 percent below their record highs, confirming corrections.
  • The S&P 500 fell for a seventh straight day, its longest losing streak since February 2020.
  • Anthropic closed 17.1 percent above its $83 offer price on Thursday, then fell 9.1 percent Friday.
  • Nvidia fell 13.8 percent and the Nasdaq lost 5.28 percent on Friday.

One river has a water deal between Bangladesh and India. It expires 12 December

Bangladesh and India share 54 rivers, only the Ganges has a bilateral water deal, and officials meet today as it nears its end.

Bangladesh and India share 54 rivers. Only one of them is covered by a water-sharing agreement between the two countries, and that agreement runs out on 12 December.

The river is the Ganges. The treaty was signed on 12 December 1996, so it expires on its own anniversary.

On Sunday, 22 November, the two countries' Joint Rivers Commission meets in Dhaka. The Indian delegation is already there, and a senior official told UNB this is the last meeting under the current treaty.

Here's the catch. Dhaka calls it a routine meeting. The committee's job is to check that the existing treaty is being carried out, and renewing or extending it is not within its jurisdiction.

So what does Bangladesh actually want? Water Resources Minister Shahiduddin Chowdhury Anee says a new or revised agreement, not a simple renewal. His main priority, in his words, is to bring back the guarantee clause from the 1977 agreement.

Think of that clause as a floor under the tap. The 1977 deal, negotiated by the BNP's founder Ziaur Rahman, said that if the Ganges flow at Farakka fell below the projected level in any 10-day period, Bangladesh's release would not drop below 80% of its scheduled share. Of every 10 units it was due, at least 8 would still arrive.

Why does a floor matter so much? Dhaka says it wants its share of dry-season flows protected, because that is when there is least water to go around.

India's line, from Foreign Ministry spokesperson Randhir Jaiswal, is that the commission is the established place to discuss all water issues, including the Ganga. He added that India stays committed to a constructive, forward-looking relationship while safeguarding its own interest.

Foreign Minister Dr Khalilur is thinking long. He said he wants a durable solution that wins public trust and lays the basis for the next 25 to 50 years.

As for whether the answer is renewal or replacement, State Minister for Foreign Affairs Humaiun Kobir would only say, "Whatever is best for Bangladesh's interest will be done." With the expiry date set and the choice unannounced, that is the question still hanging over the Ganges.

Key facts

  • The Ganges treaty was signed on 12 December 1996 and is due to expire on 12 December this year.
  • Bangladesh and India share 54 transboundary rivers, and only the Ganges has a bilateral water-sharing agreement.
  • Bangladesh wants a new or revised deal with a guarantee clause like the 1977 one, which set an 80% floor on its scheduled share.
  • The Joint Rivers Commission meeting on 22 November cannot decide renewal or extension.

Nepal's Prime Minister Balendra Shah had pledged not to travel abroad during his first year in office. On Sunday he arrives in Delhi for a two-day visit.

So what changed? Flash floods in August killed more than 1,450 people in Nepal, and thousands remain missing. A reconstruction package is on the agenda, along with trade, energy and new air corridors.

Shah is 36 and a rapper-turned-politician. His Rastriya Swatantra Party swept to power in March, six months after a Gen Z uprising against corruption and nepotism toppled K P Sharma Oli's government. In Delhi he meets Narendra Modi.

Why does this matter so much? Nepal is wedged between India and China, and its stability has direct implications for India's security. Delhi and Beijing both compete for influence in Kathmandu.

Here's the catch. The two countries share a largely open border of more than 1,750km, yet Nepal has long carried a 'Big Brother' grievance about India. Bilateral trade exceeds $9bn and is heavily skewed in India's favour.

Then there is the sky. Nepal wants India to open more air routes so large aircraft can fly in from the west to its new airports at Bhairahawa and Pokhara. Shorter flights cost less, and more flights could make those airports commercially viable.

Both airports have Chinese links. China's Northwest Civil Aviation Airport built the Bhairahawa airport, and Pokhara's was built with Chinese financial assistance.

Electricity is the easier story. India has approved projects to import up to 1,200MW from Nepal and wants more. India has the demand, and Nepal has the water and the mountains.

The hard one is the map. In 2019, India published a map that included areas Kathmandu claims, and Nepal then published its own. Analyst Kanak Mani Dixit hopes Shah raises it, saying talks should start even if there is no quick resolution.

Before leaving, Shah sent 20 party MPs to China for 11 days from 15 November. That looks like an effort to balance ties. Former Indian ambassador Ranjit Rae says 'it's an optimistic moment'.

Whether the map comes up in the room is the thing nobody can say yet.

EU and Indonesia sign trade deal after almost 10 years of talks

After almost a decade of haggling, the EU and Indonesia signed a trade deal, with palm oil among the major stumbling blocks.

Almost 10 years. That is how long the European Union and Indonesia negotiated before they signed a free trade deal on Saturday.

A free trade deal is an agreement to cut the taxes, called tariffs, that countries charge on each other's goods. The idea is simple: make it cheaper to sell across borders. Getting there was not simple.

Here's the catch. Palm oil and deforestation had been major stumbling blocks during the talks. Palm oil is one of Indonesia's main exports to Europe.

So how did they settle it? The agreement includes a dedicated protocol on palm oil to address environmental concerns, plus ways for the two sides to cooperate on regulation and sustainability. Palm oil exports are part of the deal, but will be closely regulated.

Now the money. Tariffs on about 98% of EU exports to Indonesia are to be done away with. The EU estimates European companies could save more than €600 million a year, which works out to roughly €1.6 million every day.

Indonesia's Minister of Economic Affairs, Airlangga Hartarto, called the benefits for exporters "concrete and measurable". He said the deal would help Indonesia's main exports to Europe, including palm oil, footwear and textiles.

So why now? The EU hopes to rely less on any single economic partner, as trade with both China and the US has become more difficult in recent years. Ursula von der Leyen, president of the European Commission, put it this way: "in a time when others are closing their doors, we keep ours open."

She said the two sides together represent a market of 730 million people. A separate Investment Protection Agreement is meant to attract investment in areas such as electric vehicles, electronics and pharmaceuticals.

Signing is not the finish line. The EU parliament is expected to approve the deal, and Indonesian authorities still have to complete their own ratification. If both happen, the deal can enter into force in 2027.

Key facts

  • The EU and Indonesia signed a free trade deal on Saturday after almost 10 years of negotiations.
  • Tariffs on about 98% of EU exports to Indonesia are to be removed.
  • Palm oil and deforestation were major stumbling blocks; the deal has a dedicated palm oil protocol.
  • The deal is expected to enter into force in 2027 after ratification.

Astronomers watched a giant star swallow its partner in about seven years

In the Andromeda galaxy, a small star spiraled into a supergiant, and telescopes caught the whole merger.

For thousands of years, two stars in the neighboring Andromeda galaxy circled each other and never touched. Then, in about seven years, one of them vanished into the other.

Astronomers caught it, which is rare. Mergers are hard to predict, so they are hard to watch live. First author Viraj Karambelkar said the team noticed something unusual in 2023 and kept watching.

The big star, nicknamed Big Bao, was about seven times the Sun's mass, with a radius 350 times larger. Lay 350 Suns in a row and you would just reach from its center to its surface. It shone with 18,000 times the Sun's brightness.

Its partner, Little Bao, held about 70% of the Sun's mass. In the data, one co-author said, the giant completely outshone it.

So how does a star swallow a star? Think of a spoon dragged through thick honey. Little Bao began brushing the giant's huge outer atmosphere, the gas resisted, and the small star lost orbital energy and spiraled deeper. Astronomers call this common envelope evolution.

Here's the catch. The first sign was a slow brightening, as the small star peeled gas off the giant. Then, in 2025, it erupted. Co-author Nadia Blagorodnova said they expected an outburst to take years, but the brightness started rising and peaked within a week.

NASA's James Webb Space Telescope looked 47 days after the peak, which the team calls the earliest follow-up of an event like this. Its infrared measurements also revealed dust the giant had thrown off over hundreds of years.

Early simulations suggested Little Bao might have escaped. Further work said otherwise: it was dragged deep into the core, releasing an immense amount of energy. Once the contact began, it took seven more orbits. Mergers of smaller pairs had needed thousands.

Why does this matter? Sometimes both stars survive in tighter orbits, and pairs like that can go on to become neutron stars, black holes, supernovae, gravitational wave sources or millisecond pulsars. Bao shows the other ending, where the stars become one.

And what is left? A single star that researchers say may be rejuvenated, with a new lease on life after swallowing its partner.

Key facts

  • Two stars in Andromeda orbited for thousands of years before merging after about seven more orbits (about seven years).
  • Big Bao was about 7 times the Sun's mass, with a radius 350 times larger and 18,000 times the brightness.
  • Webb observed the system 47 days after the eruption peaked, the earliest follow-up of such an event.
  • Little Bao had about 0.7 solar masses and was dragged into the giant's core.

Voyager 1 is now so far away that light takes a full day to reach it

As of November 18, 2026, a signal sent at light speed toward Voyager 1 needs more than 24 hours to arrive.

How far away is something when the fastest thing in the universe needs a whole day to get there?

Voyager 1 is that far. As of November 18, 2026, a signal sent at the speed of light toward the spacecraft takes more than 24 hours to arrive. Voyager 1 is humanity's most distant spacecraft.

Light is the fastest thing there is, so here's the thing to hold onto. Light covers about 300,000 kilometers every second. Stretch that across a full day and you get a distance called a light-day.

Now the numbers. Voyager 1 is over 16 billion miles away, which is over 25.9 billion kilometers. Divide that by the seconds in a day and you land right around light's speed, so the numbers check out.

That distance is more than three times the distance to Pluto. Pluto is the far-off world most of us think of as the edge of the neighborhood. Voyager 1 left it well behind.

Here's a way to feel it. From Voyager 1, the Sun looks 30,000 times fainter than it does from Earth. It is still the Sun, just a much smaller light in a very dark sky.

So what does a distance like this do to a conversation? It stretches it. Any message you send has to cross that gap before anyone can even start to reply.

That is the clever, slightly dizzying part. Send a message to Voyager 1 today and it arrives after more than a day. Its answer then needs the same trip back, so the whole exchange takes more than two days.

  • As of November 18, 2026, light takes more than a full day to reach Voyager 1.
  • Voyager 1 is over 16 billion miles (over 25.9 billion km) away.
  • That is more than three times the distance to Pluto.
  • The Sun looks 30,000 times fainter from there than from Earth.

Learn · Physics from first principles

Why physics still has open problems

Some questions need a better explanation. Others need a way to test one.

Your to-do list gets shorter when you finish a task. Think of an open-problems list in physics as a list of questions that have yet to be answered. Why do some stay on it?

One reason is that a theory cannot yet explain something observed. Another is that physicists have a proposed explanation but face a challenge in building an experiment to test it. Those are different kinds of unfinished work.

Here's a concrete count. In 1984, Barry Simon listed 15 open problems about quantum operators. About five had been solved by 2000. Subtract five from 15, and about ten were still open on that list.

A harder puzzle comes when two theories meet. The mathematical framework of the Standard Model is inconsistent with general relativity. Under extreme conditions, such as those associated with the Big Bang and the centers of black holes beyond their event horizons, both theories break down.

So an open problem can point to a gap in what a theory explains, a test we cannot yet carry out, or a clash between theories. The question that carries us into the next lesson is this: how could we test a proposed answer?

  • Open problems can be theoretical or experimental.
  • Simon listed 15 quantum-operator problems in 1984; about five had been solved by 2000.

The Rohingya ruling orders safe return while war continues. That sentence holds a puzzle: what changes when an institution makes a decision, but the conditions for carrying it out are still missing? Across today’s news, decisions arrive in a moment. Making them work takes longer.

The immediate event is the investigation; the slower question is how people keep relying on a system once its protection comes into doubt. The long view matters for agreements, too: the only river deal between Bangladesh and India expires on 12 December. A date can be clear even when what follows it is not.

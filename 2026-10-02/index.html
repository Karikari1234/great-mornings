Trump weighs diesel export ban as Washington requests European reserves

Donald Trump is weighing a ban on American diesel exports, and his administration has urged European countries to release their fuel reserves.

Donald Trump said he may ask European countries to release portions of their diesel reserves while he considers a ban on American fuel exports. On Thursday, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent urged European partners to release supplies immediately. Bessent wrote on social media that allies should accelerate delivery on existing commitments to address ongoing disruptions.

The United States is a supplier of diesel to the world and exports between 1.2 million and 1.5 million barrels per day. At the weekend, Trump said the administration was thinking about an export ban very seriously. The proposal arrives weeks before the November midterm elections, when control of Congress is at stake. Trump argued that keeping surplus barrels of diesel in the country would lower pump prices domestically.

British Energy Minister Martin McCluskey was on a call with European counterparts on Thursday to discuss the potential ban. A European Commission spokesperson said officials held multiple meetings on the diesel situation. These included contacts with high-level officials in the American administration. European nations retain fuel reserves from an earlier coordinated release of strategic stocks. A British government spokesperson said the country has a resilient supply and continues to engage with international partners.

Global fuel prices have surged following the outbreak of the US-Israel war in Iran in February. The closure of the Strait of Hormuz, which transports around a fifth of the world's oil and gas, has pushed up the price of oil products. An export ban from Russia has added to the price pressure. David Fyfe, chief economist at Argus Media, stated that cutting off American supply would cause international prices to skyrocket.

Diesel demand is difficult to reduce because the fuel is used in haulage and agriculture. Britain relies on foreign fuel shipments. Although four domestic refineries produce enough petrol to satisfy demand, their diesel output falls short of national needs. RAC figures show average British diesel prices reached 199.79p per litre, up from 142.38p. The Department for Transport recorded 15.1 million diesel vehicles on British roads at the end of June, down from 15.7 million a year earlier.

Key facts

  • The United States exports between 1.2 million and 1.5 million barrels of diesel per day.
  • British diesel prices reached 199.79p per litre, up from 142.38p, according to RAC figures.
  • The Strait of Hormuz transports around a fifth of global oil and gas.

Bangladesh remittance inflows fall to eleven-month low in September

Expatriates sent home $2.77 billion in September, while first-quarter receipts rose 13.3%.

Updated Bangladesh Bank data released on Thursday shows expatriates sent $2.77 billion home to Bangladesh in September. The intake is the lowest monthly amount in 11 months. It is 3% higher than the $2.69 billion received in September last year.

Receipts rose during the first quarter of FY27. Bangladesh received $8.59 billion from July to September. That is an increase of 13.3% from the $7.59 billion collected during the same period a year earlier.

The September receipts follow a break from the record pace seen earlier in the year. Remittances exceeded $3 billion a month for six consecutive months through May, before falling below that mark in June. The central bank recorded $2.82 billion in June, $2.86 billion in July, and $2.97 billion in August.

The recent slowdown comes after a record previous fiscal year. Inflows reached $35.59 billion, compared to the $30.33 billion received in FY25.

The highest monthly inflow in that run arrived in March, when expatriates sent $3.75 billion. May followed with $3.44 billion, while December recorded $3.22 billion.

  • $2.77 billion sent in September, the lowest monthly intake in 11 months but up 3% from $2.69 billion a year earlier.
  • $8.59 billion received in the first quarter of FY27, up 13.3% from $7.59 billion in the same period last year.

Google DeepMind develops invisible watermarks for artificial proteins

A tool called SynthIDBio embeds hidden tracking codes into protein sequences and structures.

Read

Google DeepMind has developed a watermarking system for artificial proteins, according to a paper published in Nature by David Stutz and colleagues. The method, called SynthIDBio, embeds hidden identifiers into protein sequences or three-dimensional shapes. Sequence watermarks use the order of amino acids, while structural watermarks use atomic coordinates. In laboratory tests, watermarked binders attached to target molecules, such as a coronavirus spike protein, with strength comparable to nonwatermarked versions.

A detection tool identified all watermarked sequences and more than 99.8% of watermarked structures. Standard structural adjustments, a process called relaxation, can erase the 3D patterns. The team reported that training adjustments could resolve this problem. Code for sequence watermarking is on GitHub.

Key facts

  • David Stutz and colleagues published the research in Nature in 2026.
  • The detector identified 100% of watermarked sequences and over 99.8% of 3D structures.

Swiss glaciers lost 5.5% of their ice in 2026

A long summer produced rapid melting across the Alps, with the country's largest ice fields recording historic volume loss.

Switzerland's glaciers lost 5.5% of their total volume in 2026, according to an annual report released by the glacier monitoring network GLAMOS and the Swiss Academy of Sciences. The decline followed a long, hot summer across the Alps. The seasonal loss is the second largest on record, behind the 6% drop measured in 2022.

Matthias Huss, the leader of GLAMOS, measured the country's ice fields at the end of the summer season. Slightly higher winter snowfall kept the total decline below the 2022 record. Two of the country's largest ice fields, the Rhône and Aletsch glaciers, recorded their highest ice losses this year. "They are completely devoid of ice," Huss said.

The melt disrupted water systems downstream. Glaciers feed major European waterways, including the Rhine and the Danube, during the warmer months. As water levels fell this summer, freight shipping on both rivers stopped. Nuclear power stations cooled by river water shut down temporarily.

Over the past 50 years, 1,200 smaller Swiss glaciers have disappeared. Ice fields and alpine permafrost anchor mountain slopes. Last year, a fragile glacier collapsed above the Swiss village of Blatten and destroyed 300 homes. Authorities evacuated the village before the slide, and residents remain in temporary housing under an estimated 9 million cubic meters of ice and rock.

Huss said disappearing ice and warming permafrost can trigger mountain collapses. Such events caused fatal damage outside Europe this year. On the border between Nepal and Tibet, a collapsing glacier triggered floods that killed at least 1,400 people.

A hoard of tenth-century pennies shows English residents used their own currency in medieval Rome.

Archaeologists have uncovered a hoard of tenth-century English coins in the walled garden of the Palazzo della Rovere near the Vatican. The discovery is the first material proof of the Schola Saxonum, an enclave where English residents lived with special privileges.

The site yielded 30 coins in all. Twenty-three date to the tenth century: 20 Anglo-Saxon pennies and one half-penny from the reigns of Edward the Elder and Æthelstan, with two Italian coins from the same era intermingled among them. Seven bronze coins from the Roman period lay in the same ground. Many of the pieces are fused together. Readable specimens were minted in London and Kent.

Tenth-century English coins found in Rome were previously regarded as religious donations, noted Rory Naismith at the University of Cambridge. Across medieval Europe, the norm was to use the local coin of the surrounding jurisdiction. The new finds indicate that English currency was used in daily trade alongside Italian coins. Naismith compared the district to modern immigrant communities in London or San Francisco. Francesca Tinti at the University of the Basque Country stated that the fused coins match the English pennies in size and thickness.

Excavations at the Palazzo della Rovere began in 2020. In 2023, the team discovered the ruins of Emperor Nero's theatre on the grounds. The dig also yielded craft items made of leather and bone. Historical documents record earlier ties between the English and the city: in 893, the Welsh scholar Asser wrote that Pope Marinus I granted the English special rights in recognition of their piety.

Naismith and his colleagues published their findings in the book Rome and the Others (300-1000): Foreigners in the city between conflicts and integration.

What a scientific law is

Scientific laws use empirical evidence to predict recurring physical phenomena.

Our senses perceive that mercury is liquid at standard temperature and pressure. That statement is a confirmed fact. It is too specific to qualify as a scientific law. In natural science, a law is a statement based on repeated experiments or observations that describes or predicts a range of natural phenomena.

Scientists develop laws from data, and they develop them further through mathematics. Every law rests on empirical evidence. A law applies to a physical system under repeated conditions. It implies a causal relationship among the elements of that system. In the philosophy of science, David Hume raised the problem of separating causal relationships from principles that arise due to constant conjunction.

A law differs from hypotheses and postulates. Hypotheses are proposed during the scientific process before and during validation by experiment. They lack the verification that supports a law. Scientific theories are broader in scope, and a single theory may entail several laws. Science also distinguishes laws from facts. Calling a law a fact is an overstatement. Mathematical theorems can be proved purely by mathematics, but scientific laws are empirical conclusions that do not express absolute certainty.

Many laws take mathematical forms and appear as equations to predict experimental outcomes. In the law of conservation of energy, the equation is written as Δ E = 0. Here, E is the total amount of energy in the universe. If a physical system changes, the total change Δ E equals 0. Newton's second law also takes an equation, written as F = d p / dt.

Some laws are approximations with a restricted domain of applicability. Newtonian dynamics is the low-speed limit of special relativity. The Newtonian gravitation law is a low-mass approximation of general relativity. Scientists use these simpler versions when speeds or masses remain low. General relativity applies when conditions involve enormous speeds or masses.

Laws face constant experimental testing to increasing degrees of precision. When new observations contradict a law, scientists formulate new statements that generalize upon the original equations. Repeatable experimental evidence can prove that an established law has limitations at very small scales of space or enormous speeds. Lesson 2 addresses the next question: how do experiments confirm whether a law continues to hold or breaks under new conditions?

Key facts

  • The law of conservation of energy is written as Δ E = 0, where E is the total amount of energy in the universe.
  • David Hume raised the problem of separating causal relationships from constant conjunction.
  • Newtonian dynamics is the low-speed limit of special relativity.
